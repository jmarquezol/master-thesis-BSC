\section{Time Evolution via Transverse Contraction}
\label{sec:transverse}

Section~\ref{sec:cft} showed that the Loschmidt echo of a critical quench encodes conformal data in the spectrum of a transfer matrix acting along the spatial direction of the corresponding strip. We now construct this transfer matrix and explain how it is used numerically. Transverse contraction was introduced for infinite chains by representing the dynamics with matrix product states extending along the time direction~\cite{banuls2009,muller_hermes2012}, and was subsequently developed to study temporal entropies~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024,carignano_tagliacozzo2025}. Our calculations use the \texttt{ITransverse.jl} implementation developed in~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}.

The construction proceeds by rotating the space--time tensor network so that it is contracted along the spatial direction. That rotation turns the contraction into an eigenproblem for the spatial transfer matrix, whose dominant eigenvectors are the temporal boundary states. From those states we build the reduced transition matrix that defines the generalized temporal entropies. We then compare the two schemes available for controlling the bond dimension.

\subsection{The rotated network}
\label{sec:transverse:network}

In the transverse contraction framework, the time evolution of a $D$-dimensional system is expressed as a $(D{+}1)$-dimensional tensor network, whose additional dimension represents time. For the one-dimensional chain studied here, this gives a two-dimensional network with no open physical legs; since the echo is a scalar amplitude, the network is analogous to the partition function of a two-dimensional classical model. Contracting such two-dimensional networks exactly is \#P-hard in general~\cite{schuch2007}, so the calculation requires an approximate representation with a controlled bond dimension (Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}).

The tensor-network representation of the echo follows directly from Eq.~\eqref{eq:echo} and is pictured in Figure~\ref{fig:echo_strip}a. The initial and final states are represented as \acp{MPS}, while the evolution operator is decomposed into a sequence of \ac{MPO} layers, one for each Trotter step. Because we require the Hamiltonian to be translation invariant and time independent, the same bulk tensor $W$ appears at every spatial site and time step. Section~\ref{sec:mporep} constructs this tensor for the model with \ac{NNN} interactions.

In traditional evolution algorithms, successive \ac{MPO} layers of the time-evolution operator $U(\delta t)$ are applied to the initial \ac{MPS} $\ket{\Psi_0}$. The resulting spatial entanglement grows linearly with time (volume law) and eventually makes the state intractable at fixed bond dimension~\cite{calabrese_cardy2005,schuch2008,paeckel2019}. Transverse contraction instead contracts the same network along the spatial direction, column by column. No tensor changes; each is reinterpreted, with the virtual spatial bonds playing the role of physical temporal indices and the physical spatial indices that of virtual temporal bonds. Figure~\ref{fig:rotation} shows the exchange. The physical dimension of the temporal problem is therefore set by the bond dimension of $U(\delta t)$. Each bulk column defines a \ac{tMPO} $\mathcal{E}$ that acts as a transfer matrix along the original spatial direction. Translation invariance ensures that the same $\mathcal{E}$ is applied at every site.

These objects provide the lattice representation of the strip geometry introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cft}. The strip is infinite along the spatial direction. The states $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ are obtained by applying infinitely many columns to either side of a cut, which projects them onto the dominant left and right eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$. Nothing therefore depends on how the chain is terminated far away. The two states encode the temporal boundary condition set by $\ket{\Psi_0}$ at the top and bottom edges. The width of the strip is set by the number of temporal sites, including both the real-time evolution and the cooling sites associated with the regulator $\beta_0$. The column transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ is the operator whose spectrum is constrained by the \ac{CFT}, while the Loschmidt echo is obtained from the overlap $\braket{L}{R}$ after the spatial contraction.

The row and column transfer operators have different spectral properties. Each row represents the unitary Trotter step $U(\delta t)=e^{-iH\delta t}$ and therefore has eigenvalues of unit modulus. By contrast, the column transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ is non-Hermitian and generally has complex eigenvalues with different moduli. The implications of its non-Hermiticity for the left and right eigenvectors are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:mporep} after the operator has been constructed explicitly.

\subsection{The dynamics as an eigenvalue problem}
\label{sec:transverse:eigenproblem}

The rotated network reduces the Loschmidt echo to repeated applications of the column transfer matrix. After the rotation and Trotter decomposition, the two-dimensional network of Figure~\ref{fig:echo_strip} can be contracted along the spatial direction between the temporal boundaries introduced in Section~\ref{sec:transverse:network}. For a chain of $N$ spatial sites,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(T)
    \;=\; \bra{L}\,\mathcal{E}(T)^{\,N}\,\ket{R}
    \;=\; \sum_i c_i\,\mu_i(T)^{\,N}
    \;=\; c_0\,\mu_0(T)^{\,N}
      \Big[\,1+\sum_{i\ge1}\frac{c_i}{c_0}\Big(\frac{\mu_i}{\mu_0}\Big)^{\!N}\,\Big],
    \label{eq:contraction}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathcal{E}$ and the coefficients $c_i$ contain the corresponding overlaps with the boundary states. If $\mu_0$ has the largest modulus, every other contribution is suppressed relative to it by $(|\mu_i|/|\mu_0|)^N$. The thermodynamic limit is therefore determined by the leading eigenvalue, and the intensive Loschmidt rate becomes
\begin{equation}
    \ell(T) = -\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log|\mathcal{L}(T)|
            = -\log|\mu_0(T)|,
    \label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
up to $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$ boundary corrections for a finite open chain. The calculation thus reduces to a spectral problem whose cost is independent of the spatial length.

Equation~\eqref{eq:contraction} also identifies the spectral data required for the conformal analysis. The modulus of $\mu_0$ determines the Loschmidt rate, its phase encodes the central charge, and the first spectral gap gives access to the boundary operator content. The generalized temporal entropies instead depend on the dominant left and right eigenvectors. Our calculations must therefore resolve the leading eigenpairs of the non-Hermitian operator $\mathcal{E}$.

Resolving these eigenpairs becomes more difficult in the long-time regime where the conformal predictions apply. Their asymptotic form requires $T$ to be large compared with both the regulator $\beta_0$ and the lattice scale. In this regime, the emergent-dual-unitarity prediction of Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal} states that the leading eigenvalues approach a common modulus, so a single dominant eigenvalue is no longer well separated from its neighbours. We therefore iterate a block of $k$ states that represents the full leading subspace, rather than a single candidate for the dominant eigenvector. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} develops the block power method that implements this idea. Before that, the model itself (Section~\ref{sec:annni}) and the translation-invariant MPO that evolves it (Section~\ref{sec:mporep}) must be specified.

Each iteration of that power method grows the bond dimension of the temporal states, so each one must be followed by a truncation; we return to how that truncation is chosen in Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}, once the object it acts on has been defined.

\subsection{Temporal states and the reduced transition matrix}
\label{sec:transverse:entropies}

Each application of $\mathcal{E}$ increases the bond dimension of the temporal boundary states. The computational cost is therefore controlled by their temporal entanglement, defined across a cut in the time direction. Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal} showed that in \ac{CFT} the generalized temporal entropies grow logarithmically with the evolution time. We now define the object from which these entropies are computed.

The dominant left and right fixed points of the non-Hermitian transfer matrix are distinct, and the echo depends on both. A reduced density matrix of either one alone therefore cannot describe it. The Loschmidt echo depends on both through $\braket{L}{R}$, so the relevant object is the \ac{RTM} constructed jointly from the two temporal boundaries,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}_t = \frac{\operatorname{Tr}_{>t}\ket{R}\bra{L}}{\braket{L}{R}},
    \label{eq:rtm}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Tr}_{>t}$ traces over the $T-t$ temporal sites above the cut, leaving the $t$ sites below it with open indices. The object $\mathcal{T}_t$ therefore acts on the first $t$ temporal sites: its subscript names the sites that are kept, in the same way as the subscript of a reduced density matrix $\rho_{\mathcal A}$, not the ones that are traced away. This normalization gives $\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{T}_t=1$. Since $\bra{L}$ is not the Hermitian conjugate of $\ket{R}$, $\mathcal{T}_t$ is generally non-Hermitian and its eigenvalues may be complex. They cannot be interpreted as probabilities, and the usual Schmidt interpretation of an entanglement spectrum does not apply. Figure~\ref{fig:rtm_strip} places this object in the strip geometry.

(c) Contracting every column to the left of the cut gives $\bra{L}$, and every column to its right gives $\ket{R}$; the power method of Section~\ref{sec:numerics} performs those contractions and converges them to the dominant left and right eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$.   \label{fig:rtm_strip}

The spectrum of $\mathcal{T}_t$ defines the generalized temporal entropies associated with the transition between the two boundary states. The conformal prediction for this family was introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cft}. We use its R\'enyi-2 member,
\begin{equation}
    S_{2}^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = -\log \operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\mathcal{T}_t^2\right).
    \label{eq:s2gen}
\end{equation}
The quantity $\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{T}_t^2)$ can be evaluated by contracting two copies of the \ac{RTM}, without diagonalizing it or computing a matrix logarithm. This is why we use the R\'enyi-2 member. When $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ differ there is no gauge in which $\mathcal{T}_t$ is locally diagonal, so its spectrum is not accessible from a local decomposition. The price is that the R\'enyi-2 entropy carries finite-time corrections of its own, discussed with the predictions in Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}. The same two-copy structure makes the generalized R\'enyi-2 temporal entropy accessible through the experimental replica protocol proposed in Ref.~\cite{boucomas2026measuring}.

Once $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ have converged for a target evolution time $T$, we evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:s2gen} at every internal bond of the temporal chain, corresponding to cuts $t\in(0,T)$. The entropy is generally complex because $\mathcal{T}_t$ is non-Hermitian, so its real and imaginary parts are analysed separately as functions of the normalized cut position. Our cuts are anchored at one edge of the strip: each extends from an edge to the temporal bond at $t$, so only one endpoint sits in the interior. The logarithmic slope in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} therefore carries the coefficient $\tfrac{c}{12}(1+1/n)$, which equals $c/8$ for $n=2$; an interval with both endpoints in the interior would carry twice that. The central charges reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} are extracted with the edge-anchored coefficient $c/8$.

\subsection{Truncation based on the RDM and the RTM}
\label{sec:transverse:truncation}

We have two complementary truncation schemes at our disposal, based respectively on the \ac{RDM} and the \ac{RTM}. The first is the conventional truncation available in most tensor-network libraries: one brings the boundary \ac{MPS} to mixed canonical form and discards the smallest singular values of its orthogonality centre, which gives the optimal low-rank approximation of that single state. The second is the gauge-invariant scheme that \texttt{ITransverse.jl} implements for the transverse picture, and truncates instead on the \ac{RTM} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rtm}, built from both boundaries at once. We use both, with the \ac{RTM} as our production default. Appendix~\ref{app:sub:rtm_truncation} works through the differences between them, in particular how each responds to a change of gauge, which is what singles out the \ac{RTM} spectrum as the physical one.

The reason the \ac{RTM} is our default is that it is considerably cheaper, and it is worth seeing why from what each scheme tries to preserve. The eigenvalues of the boundary \ac{RDM} are the squared Schmidt coefficients of $\ket{R}$ across the cut, so truncating on them keeps the directions in which $\ket{R}$ carries the most weight, whether or not those directions overlap with $\bra{L}$. It therefore represents the whole of $\ket{R}$ faithfully, including the component orthogonal to $\bra{L}$ that will contribute nothing once the overlap is taken. The \ac{RTM} instead weights each direction by its contribution to the overlap itself: a direction along which $\ket{R}$ is large but which is orthogonal to the corresponding part of $\bra{L}$ carries a small singular value and is discarded. By keeping only the part of each boundary that actually contributes to $\braket{L}{R}$, the \ac{RTM} needs fewer Schmidt values, and therefore a smaller bond dimension, than the \ac{RDM}, which pays to store information the final overlap throws away. Formally, the \ac{RDM} compression is bounded by the state entanglement and the \ac{RTM} compression by the smaller operator-space entanglement~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024}.

That saving comes at a price. For a Hermitian, positive \ac{RDM} the singular values coincide with the eigenvalues, so discarding the smallest of them is the same as discarding the smallest eigenvalues, and the truncation inherits a rigorous variational bound. The \ac{RTM} is not Hermitian, its eigenvalues can be complex or negative, and the trace that gives us the physical overlap is no longer in one-to-one correspondence with the singular-value magnitudes: two large singular values can belong to eigenvalues that cancel exactly in the trace. Truncating the \ac{RTM} is therefore a numerical criterion rather than a variational one, and its convergence need not be as smooth. It nevertheless works in practice, for a reason that is physical rather than mathematical and that Appendix~\ref{app:sub:rtm_truncation} explains. We keep the \ac{RDM} as a fallback for the cases where the \ac{RTM} output is noisy or hard to interpret, accepting the larger bond dimension in exchange for a cleaner answer, and Section~\ref{sec:results} verifies that the two agree wherever both converge.
