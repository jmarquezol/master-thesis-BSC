\section{Time Evolution via Transverse Contraction}
\label{sec:transverse}

Section~\ref{sec:cft} showed that the Loschmidt echo of a critical quench encodes conformal data in the spectrum of a transfer matrix acting along the spatial direction of the corresponding strip. We now construct this transfer matrix and explain how it is used numerically. Transverse contraction was introduced for infinite chains by representing the dynamics with matrix product states extending along the time direction~\cite{banuls2009,muller_hermes2012}, and was subsequently developed to study temporal entropies~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024,carignano_tagliacozzo2025}. Our calculations use the \texttt{ITransverse.jl} implementation~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}.

The construction proceeds by rotating the space--time tensor network and identifying its spatial transfer matrix, its temporal boundary states, and the transition matrix that defines the generalized temporal entropies. We then compare the two truncation schemes used to control the bond dimension and formulate the contraction as an eigenvalue problem.

\subsection{The rotated network}
\label{sec:transverse:network}

Transverse contraction represents the time evolution of a $D$-dimensional system as a $(D{+}1)$-dimensional tensor network, with time providing the additional dimension. For the one-dimensional chain studied here, this gives a two-dimensional network with no open physical legs: since the echo is a scalar amplitude, the network is analogous to the partition function of a two-dimensional classical model. Contracting such two-dimensional networks exactly is \#P-hard in general~\cite{schuch2007}, so the calculation requires an approximate representation with a controlled bond dimension.

The network follows directly from Eq.~\eqref{eq:echo}. The initial and final states are represented as \acp{MPS}, while the evolution operator is decomposed into a sequence of \ac{MPO} layers, one for each Trotter step. Because the Hamiltonian is translation invariant and time independent, the same bulk tensor $W$ appears at every spatial site and time step. Section~\ref{sec:mporep} constructs this tensor for the model with \ac{NNN} interactions.

In the conventional contraction, successive \ac{MPO} layers of the time-evolution operator $U(\delta t)$ are applied to the initial \ac{MPS} $\ket{\Psi_0}$. The resulting spatial entanglement grows linearly with time (volume law) and eventually makes the state intractable at fixed bond dimension. Transverse contraction instead contracts the same network along the spatial direction, column by column. No tensor changes; each is reinterpreted, with the virtual spatial bonds playing the role of physical temporal indices and the physical spatial indices that of virtual temporal bonds (Figure~\ref{fig:rotation}). The boundary columns define the \acp{tMPS} $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$, and each bulk column defines a \ac{tMPO} $\mathcal{E}$ that acts as a transfer matrix along the original spatial direction. Translation invariance ensures that the same $\mathcal{E}$ is applied at every site.

These objects provide the lattice representation of the strip geometry introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cft}. The strip is infinite along the spatial direction. The states $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ are obtained by applying infinitely many columns to either side of a cut, which projects them onto the dominant left and right eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$. Nothing therefore depends on how the chain is terminated far away. The two states encode the temporal boundary condition set by $\ket{\Psi_0}$ at the top and bottom edges. The width of the strip is set by the number of temporal sites, including both the real-time evolution and the cooling sites associated with the regulator $\beta_0$. The column transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ is the operator whose spectrum is constrained by the \ac{CFT}, while the Loschmidt echo is obtained from the overlap $\braket{L}{R}$ after the spatial contraction.

The row and column transfer operators have different spectral properties. Each row represents the unitary Trotter step $U(\delta t)=e^{-iH\delta t}$ and therefore has eigenvalues of unit modulus. By contrast, the column transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ is non-Hermitian and generally has complex eigenvalues with different moduli. The implications of its non-Hermiticity for the left and right eigenvectors are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:mporep} after the operator has been constructed explicitly.

\subsection{Temporal states and the reduced transition matrix}
\label{sec:transverse:entropies}

Each application of $\mathcal{E}$ increases the bond dimension of the temporal boundary states. The computational cost is therefore controlled by their temporal entanglement, defined across a cut in the time direction. Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal} showed that in \ac{CFT} the generalized temporal entropies grow logarithmically with the evolution time. We now define the object from which these entropies are computed.

The dominant left and right fixed points of the non-Hermitian transfer matrix are distinct, and the echo depends on both. A reduced density matrix of either one alone therefore cannot describe it. The Loschmidt echo depends on both through $\braket{L}{R}$, so the relevant object is the \ac{RTM} constructed jointly from the two temporal boundaries,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}_t = \frac{\operatorname{Tr}_{>t}\ket{R}\bra{L}}{\braket{L}{R}},
    \label{eq:rtm}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Tr}_{>t}$ traces over the $T-t$ temporal sites above the cut, leaving the $t$ sites below it with open indices. The object $\mathcal{T}_t$ therefore acts on the first $t$ temporal sites: its subscript names the sites that are kept, in the same way as the subscript of a reduced density matrix $\rho_{\mathcal A}$, not the ones that are traced away. This normalization gives $\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{T}_t=1$. Since $\bra{L}$ is not the Hermitian conjugate of $\ket{R}$, $\mathcal{T}_t$ is generally non-Hermitian and its eigenvalues may be complex. They cannot be interpreted as probabilities, and the usual Schmidt interpretation of an entanglement spectrum does not apply. Figure~\ref{fig:rtm_strip} places this object in the strip geometry.

Along the cut bond the horizontal bonds are absent over the height $t$, so the tensors on either side keep an open index there, while over the remaining height $T-t$ the two sides stay contracted. (c) Contracting every column to the left of the cut gives $\bra{L}$, and every column to its right gives $\ket{R}$; the power method of Section~\ref{sec:numerics} performs those contractions and converges them to the dominant left and right eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$. The spectrum of $\mathcal{T}_t$ defines the generalized temporal entropies associated with the transition between the two boundary states. The conformal prediction for this family was introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cft}. We use its R\'enyi-2 member,
\begin{equation}
    S_{2}^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = -\log \operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\mathcal{T}_t^2\right).
    \label{eq:s2gen}
\end{equation}
The quantity $\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{T}_t^2)$ can be evaluated by contracting two copies of the \ac{RTM}, without diagonalizing it or computing a matrix logarithm. This is why we use the R\'enyi-2 member. When $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ differ there is no gauge in which $\mathcal{T}_t$ is locally diagonal, so its spectrum is not accessible from a local decomposition. The price is that the R\'enyi-2 entropy carries finite-time corrections of its own, discussed with the predictions in Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}. The same two-copy structure makes the generalized R\'enyi-2 temporal entropy accessible through the experimental replica protocol proposed in Ref.~\cite{boucomas2026measuring}.

Once $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ have converged for a target evolution time $T$, we evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:s2gen} at every internal bond of the temporal chain, corresponding to cuts $t\in(0,T)$. The entropy is generally complex because $\mathcal{T}_t$ is non-Hermitian, so its real and imaginary parts are analysed separately as functions of the normalized cut position. Our cuts are anchored at one edge of the strip: each extends from an edge to the temporal bond at $t$, so only one endpoint sits in the interior. The logarithmic slope in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} therefore carries the coefficient $\tfrac{c}{12}(1+1/n)$, which equals $c/8$ for $n=2$; an interval with both endpoints in the interior would carry twice that. The central charges reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} are extracted with the edge-anchored coefficient $c/8$.

\subsection{The dynamics as an eigenvalue problem}
\label{sec:transverse:eigenproblem}

The rotated network reduces the Loschmidt echo to repeated applications of the column transfer matrix. After the rotation and Trotter decomposition, the two-dimensional network of Figure~\ref{fig:echo_strip} can be contracted along the spatial direction between the temporal boundaries introduced in Section~\ref{sec:transverse:network}. For a chain of $N$ spatial sites,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(T)
    \;=\; \bra{L}\,\mathcal{E}(T)^{\,N}\,\ket{R}
    \;=\; \sum_i c_i\,\mu_i(T)^{\,N}
    \;=\; c_0\,\mu_0(T)^{\,N}
      \Big[\,1+\sum_{i\ge1}\frac{c_i}{c_0}\Big(\frac{\mu_i}{\mu_0}\Big)^{\!N}\,\Big],
    \label{eq:contraction}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathcal{E}$ and the coefficients $c_i$ contain the corresponding overlaps with the boundary states. If $\mu_0$ has the largest modulus, every other contribution is suppressed relative to it by $(|\mu_i|/|\mu_0|)^N$. The thermodynamic limit is therefore determined by the leading eigenvalue, and the intensive Loschmidt rate becomes
\begin{equation}
    \ell(T) = -\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log|\mathcal{L}(T)|
            = -\log|\mu_0(T)|,
    \label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
up to $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$ boundary corrections for a finite open chain. The calculation thus reduces to a spectral problem whose cost is independent of the spatial length.

Equation~\eqref{eq:contraction} also identifies the spectral data required for the conformal analysis. The modulus of $\mu_0$ determines the Loschmidt rate, its phase encodes the central charge, and the first spectral gap gives access to the boundary operator content. The generalized temporal entropies instead depend on the dominant left and right eigenvectors. Our calculations must therefore resolve the leading eigenpairs of the non-Hermitian operator $\mathcal{E}$.

Resolving these eigenpairs becomes more difficult in the long-time regime where the conformal predictions apply. Their asymptotic form requires $T$ to be large compared with both the regulator $\beta_0$ and the lattice scale. In this regime, the emergent-dual-unitarity prediction of Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal} states that the leading eigenvalues approach a common modulus, so a single dominant eigenvalue is no longer well separated from its neighbours. We therefore iterate a block of $k$ states that represents the full leading subspace, rather than a single candidate for the dominant eigenvector. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} develops the block power method that implements this idea. Before that, the model itself (Section~\ref{sec:annni}) and the translation-invariant MPO that evolves it (Section~\ref{sec:mporep}) must be specified.

Each iteration of that power method grows the bond dimension of the temporal states, so each one must be followed by a truncation. Which truncation is appropriate is the subject of the next subsection.

\subsection{Truncation using the transition matrix}
\label{sec:transverse:truncation}

The temporal states $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ are the fixed points obtained by the power method of Section~\ref{sec:numerics}. Each application of $\mathcal{E}$ enlarges their bond dimensions, so each one must be followed by a truncation. Two local truncation criteria are available. The conventional scheme discards the smallest singular values obtained from the \ac{RDM} of an individual boundary state. The alternative constructs a local \ac{RTM} from both boundaries and truncates the components that contribute least to their joint contraction. We use both schemes, with the \ac{RTM} truncation as the production default, because their gauge properties, computational costs, and numerical stability differ.

The first distinction concerns gauge invariance. A tensor network may be regauged on any internal bond without changing the physical contraction. Under a general non-unitary gauge transformation $X$, the \ac{RDM} of one temporal boundary transforms as $\rho\to X^\dagger\rho X$, which changes its spectrum. The corresponding Schmidt values therefore depend on the chosen gauge. The \ac{RTM}, by contrast, transforms by similarity, so its eigenvalues remain unchanged. Its spectrum is consequently the appropriate gauge-invariant quantity and is the object constrained by the \ac{CFT}. Appendix~\ref{app:sub:rtm_truncation} gives the derivation.

The second distinction is computational cost. The \ac{RTM} can in principle keep only the components of each boundary that contribute to $\braket{L}{R}$, whereas the \ac{RDM} also represents components of $\ket{R}$ that are orthogonal to $\bra{L}$ and therefore do not contribute to the final contraction. The \ac{RDM} cost is controlled by the entanglement of an individual boundary state, while the \ac{RTM} cost is governed by the entanglement of the joint object built from both boundaries, which is typically smaller~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024}. In practice, this difference substantially reduces the bond dimension required by the \ac{RTM} scheme.

The third distinction is numerical stability. Truncation of a Hermitian positive \ac{RDM} inherits the variational control of standard tensor-network compression and generally converges smoothly: truncating each boundary state to norm accuracy $\varepsilon$ bounds the error of the overlap itself linearly in $\varepsilon$~\cite{hastings_mahajan2015}. No variational guarantee of this kind holds for singular-value truncation of the non-Hermitian \ac{RTM}. Its singular values and eigenvalues measure different properties, and components with large singular values may cancel in the trace that determines the physical overlap. Singular-value truncation of the \ac{RTM} is therefore a controlled numerical criterion rather than a variational approximation, and its convergence need not be monotonic. Appendix~\ref{app:sub:rtm_truncation} describes the local environment used for this truncation and discusses its limitations.

The \ac{RTM} scheme is thus gauge invariant and less expensive, but may converge less smoothly, whereas the \ac{RDM} scheme is more stable but requires a larger bond dimension. We use the \ac{RTM} for the production calculations and retain the \ac{RDM} as an independent check or as a fallback when the \ac{RTM} results become noisy. Section~\ref{sec:results} verifies that the two schemes agree in the regime where both converge.
