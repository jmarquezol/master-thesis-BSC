\section{Time Evolution via Transverse Contraction}
\label{sec:transverse}

Section~\ref{sec:cft} showed that the Loschmidt echo of a critical quench encodes conformal data in the spectrum of a transfer matrix acting along the spatial direction of the corresponding strip. We now construct this transfer matrix and explain how it is used numerically. Transverse contraction was introduced for infinite chains by representing the dynamics with \ac{MPS} extending along the time direction~\cite{banuls2009,muller_hermes2012}, and was subsequently developed to study temporal entropies~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024,carignano_tagliacozzo2025}. Our calculations use the \texttt{ITransverse.jl} implementation developed in~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}.

We rotate the space--time tensor network and contract it along the spatial direction. This reformulation maps the contraction to an eigenproblem for the spatial transfer matrix, whose dominant eigenvectors define the temporal boundary states. These states determine the \ac{RTM} used to compute the generalized temporal entropies. We finally compare two truncation schemes for controlling their bond dimension.

\subsection{The rotated network}
\label{sec:transverse:network}

In the transverse contraction framework, the time evolution of a $D$-dimensional system is represented by a $(D{+}1)$-dimensional tensor network, with the additional dimension corresponding to time. For the one-dimensional chain studied here, the result is a two-dimensional network with no open physical legs. The echo is a scalar amplitude, so this network is analogous to the partition function of a two-dimensional classical model. Its exact contraction is \#P-hard in general~\cite{schuch2007}, which requires us to use an approximate representation with a controlled bond dimension (Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}).

The tensor-network representation of the echo follows directly from Eq.~\eqref{eq:echo} and is pictured in Figure~\ref{fig:echo_strip}a. The initial and final states are represented as \acp{MPS}, while the evolution operator is decomposed into a sequence of \ac{MPO} layers, one for each Trotter step. Because we require the Hamiltonian to be translation invariant and time independent, the same bulk tensor $W$ appears at every spatial site and time step. Section~\ref{sec:mporep} constructs this tensor for the model with \ac{NNN} interactions.

In conventional evolution algorithms, successive \ac{MPO} layers of the time-evolution operator $U(\delta t)$ are applied to the initial \ac{MPS} $\ket{\Psi_0}$. The resulting spatial entanglement grows linearly with time (volume law) and eventually makes the state intractable at fixed bond dimension~\cite{calabrese_cardy2005,schuch2008,paeckel2019}. Transverse contraction instead contracts the same network column by column along the spatial direction. The tensors remain unchanged, but their indices exchange roles: the virtual spatial bonds become physical temporal indices, while the physical spatial indices become virtual temporal bonds. Figure~\ref{fig:rotation} illustrates this exchange. The bond dimension of $U(\delta t)$ therefore sets the physical dimension of the temporal problem. Each bulk column defines a \ac{tMPO} $\mathcal{E}$ that acts as a transfer matrix along the original spatial direction, and translation invariance ensures that the same $\mathcal{E}$ is applied at every site.

  \caption{\textbf{The rotation exchanges physical and virtual indices.} A rank-4 tensor read (a) along space, with $(i_x,j_x)$ physical and $(\alpha_x,\beta_x)$ virtual, and (b) along time, where the roles are exchanged.}
  \label{fig:rotation}
\end{figure}

These objects provide the lattice representation of the strip geometry introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cft}. The strip is infinite along the spatial direction, and applying infinitely many columns on either side of a cut projects the states $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ onto the dominant left and right eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$. The resulting fixed points are independent of how the chain is terminated far from the cut. They encode the temporal boundary condition imposed by $\ket{\Psi_0}$ at the top and bottom edges. The strip width is set by the number of temporal sites, including the real-time evolution and the cooling sites associated with the regulator $\beta_0$. The \ac{CFT} constrains the spectrum of the column transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$, while the overlap $\braket{L}{R}$ gives the Loschmidt echo after the spatial contraction.

The row and column transfer operators have different spectral properties. Each row represents the unitary Trotter step $U(\delta t)=e^{-iH\delta t}$ and therefore has eigenvalues of unit modulus. By contrast, the column transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ is non-Hermitian and generally has complex eigenvalues with different moduli. The implications of its non-Hermiticity for the left and right eigenvectors are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:mporep} after the operator has been constructed explicitly.

\subsection{The dynamics as an eigenvalue problem}
\label{sec:transverse:eigenproblem}

After the rotation and Trotter decomposition, the two-dimensional network in Figure~\ref{fig:echo_strip}a is contracted along the spatial direction by repeatedly applying the column transfer matrix between the temporal boundaries. For a chain of $N$ spatial sites,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(T)
    \;=\; \bra{L}\,\mathcal{E}(T)^{\,N}\,\ket{R}
    \;=\; \sum_i c_i\,\mu_i(T)^{\,N}
    \;=\; c_0\,\mu_0(T)^{\,N}
      \Big[\,1+\sum_{i\ge1}\frac{c_i}{c_0}\Big(\frac{\mu_i}{\mu_0}\Big)^{\!N}\,\Big],
    \label{eq:contraction}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathcal{E}$ and the coefficients $c_i$ contain the corresponding overlaps with the boundary states. If $\mu_0$ has the largest modulus, every other contribution is suppressed relative to it by $(|\mu_i|/|\mu_0|)^N$. The thermodynamic limit is therefore determined by the leading eigenvalue, and the intensive Loschmidt rate becomes
\begin{equation}
    \ell(T) = -\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log|\mathcal{L}(T)|
            = -\log|\mu_0(T)|,
    \label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
up to $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$ boundary corrections for a finite open chain. The calculation thus reduces to a spectral problem whose cost is independent of the spatial length.

Equation~\eqref{eq:contraction} also identifies the spectral data required for the conformal analysis. The modulus of $\mu_0$ determines the Loschmidt rate, its phase encodes the central charge, and the first spectral gap gives access to the boundary operator content. The generalized temporal entropies instead depend on the dominant left and right eigenvectors. Our calculations must therefore resolve the leading eigenpairs of the non-Hermitian operator $\mathcal{E}$.

To obtain these eigenpairs, we implement the block power method described in Section~\ref{sec:numerics}. It evolves a block of $k$ temporal states and resolves the $k$ leading eigenvalues together with their corresponding left and right eigenvectors.

Each iteration increases the bond dimension of the temporal states and must therefore be followed by a truncation. The available truncation schemes are introduced in Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}.

\subsection{Temporal states and the \ac{RTM}}
\label{sec:transverse:entropies}

Each application of $\mathcal{E}$ increases the bond dimension of the temporal boundary states. The computational cost is therefore controlled by their temporal entanglement, defined across a cut in the time direction. Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal} showed that in \ac{CFT} the generalized temporal entropies grow logarithmically with the evolution time. We now define the object from which these entropies are computed.

The dominant left and right fixed points of the non-Hermitian transfer matrix are distinct, and the echo depends on their overlap $\braket{L}{R}$. An \ac{RDM} formed from either state alone therefore does not contain the required information. The relevant object is instead the \ac{RTM} constructed jointly from the two temporal boundaries,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}_t = \frac{\operatorname{Tr}_{>t}\ket{R}\bra{L}}{\braket{L}{R}},
    \label{eq:rtm}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Tr}_{>t}$ traces over the $T-t$ temporal sites above the cut and leaves the $t$ sites below it open. Thus, $\mathcal{T}_t$ acts on the first $t$ temporal sites. The denominator normalizes the matrix so that $\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{T}_t=1$. Since $\bra{L}$ is not the Hermitian conjugate of $\ket{R}$, $\mathcal{T}_t$ is generally non-Hermitian and may have complex eigenvalues. The consequences of this complex spectrum for truncation are discussed in the next subsection. Figure~\ref{fig:rtm_strip} shows the position of $\mathcal{T}_t$ within the strip geometry.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{imgs/rtm_strip.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Position of the reduced transition matrix.} (a) The cut lies on one spatial bond of the strip and extends from the bottom edge to height $t$. (b) In the tensor-network representation, the horizontal bonds across the cut remain open over the first $t$ temporal sites, while the two sides remain contracted over the remaining $T-t$ sites. (c) Contracting the columns to the left and right of the cut gives $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$, respectively. The power method of Section~\ref{sec:numerics} converges these states to the dominant left and right eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$. Their contraction over the upper $T-t$ sites gives $\mathcal{T}_t$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:rtm}, with the lower $t$ indices left open.}
  \label{fig:rtm_strip}
\end{figure}

The spectrum of $\mathcal{T}_t$ defines the generalized temporal entropies associated with the transition between the two boundary states. We use the R\'enyi-2 member of the family introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cft},
\begin{equation}
    S_{2}^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = -\log \operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\mathcal{T}_t^2\right).
    \label{eq:s2gen}
\end{equation}
When $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ differ, there is no gauge in which $\mathcal{T}_t$ is locally diagonal, so its spectrum cannot be obtained from a local decomposition. We choose the R\'enyi-2 entropy because $\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{T}_t^2)$ can instead be evaluated directly by contracting two copies of the \ac{RTM}, without diagonalizing it. This simplification comes with finite-time corrections that must be accounted for when comparing with the conformal predictions, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}.

Once $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ have converged for a target evolution time $T$, we evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:s2gen} at every internal bond of the temporal chain, corresponding to cuts $t\in(0,T)$. The entropy is generally complex because $\mathcal{T}_t$ is non-Hermitian, so its real and imaginary parts are analysed separately as functions of the normalized cut position. 

The temporal region represented by $\mathcal{T}_t$ extends from the lower edge of the strip to the bond at $t$ and therefore has only one endpoint in the interior. Appendix~\ref{app:cft:maps} explains how this edge-anchored geometry fixes the entropy coefficient; if the interval instead had both endpoints in the interior, the logarithmic slope in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} would be twice as large. The central charges reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} are extracted using the edge-anchored geometry.

\subsection{Truncation based on RDM and RTM}
\label{sec:transverse:truncation}

We use two complementary truncation schemes based on the \ac{RDM} and the \ac{RTM}. The conventional \ac{RDM} truncation, available in most tensor-network libraries, brings a boundary \ac{MPS} into mixed canonical form and discards the smallest singular values of its orthogonality centre, giving the optimal low-rank approximation to that state~\cite{schollwock2011,vidal2003}. The gauge-invariant scheme implemented in \texttt{ITransverse.jl}~\cite{carignano2025itransverse} instead truncates the \ac{RTM} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:rtm}, which depends on both boundaries. We use the \ac{RTM} scheme for production calculations and retain the \ac{RDM} scheme as a complementary check. Appendix~\ref{app:tensor_networks} provides further details on both truncations, including their response to gauge transformations and the conditions for optimal truncation. Here we focus on their main differences: their computational cost and the quantities discarded during truncation.

The \ac{RTM} scheme is substantially cheaper because the two schemes preserve different information. The eigenvalues of the boundary \ac{RDM} are the squared Schmidt coefficients of $\ket{R}$ across the cut. Truncating them retains the directions in which $\ket{R}$ has the greatest weight, irrespective of their overlap with $\bra{L}$. The resulting approximation therefore preserves components of $\ket{R}$ that do not contribute to $\braket{L}{R}$. By contrast, the \ac{RTM} weights each direction by its contribution to this overlap: a large component of $\ket{R}$ carries little weight if it is orthogonal to the corresponding component of $\bra{L}$. Thus, the \ac{RTM} can in principle represent the relevant part of both boundaries with fewer retained values and a smaller bond dimension.

The lower cost of the \ac{RTM} scheme comes with a weaker truncation guarantee. For a Hermitian, positive \ac{RDM}, the singular values coincide with the eigenvalues, so retaining the largest Schmidt values gives the optimal approximation at a fixed bond dimension. The \ac{RTM} is instead non-Hermitian, and its eigenvalues may be complex or negative. Although its normalized trace satisfies $\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{T}_t=\sum_i\lambda_i=1$, contributions from different eigenvalues may cancel. There is therefore no longer a one-to-one correspondence between the eigenvalues and the singular values. The overlap error can nevertheless be bounded: if the truncation changes the left and right temporal states by at most $\epsilon_L$ and $\epsilon_R$ in norm, respectively, then the error in their overlap is bounded by $\epsilon_L+\epsilon_R+\epsilon_L\epsilon_R$~\cite{hastings_mahajan2015}. 

The \ac{RTM} truncation targets contributions to the overlap rather than minimising these norm errors, so the discarded singular values cannot be used directly to evaluate the bound; however, the scheme works in practice for the physical reasons discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:tensor_networks}. When its results are noisy or difficult to interpret, we use the more expensive \ac{RDM} scheme as a fallback. Appendix~\ref{app:failures} verifies that the two schemes agree at every evolution time where both were run.
