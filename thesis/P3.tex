\section{Time Evolution via Transverse Contraction}
\label{sec:transverse}

Section~\ref{sec:cft} showed that the Loschmidt echo of a critical quench encodes conformal data in the spectrum of a transfer matrix acting along the spatial direction of the corresponding strip. We now construct this transfer matrix and explain how it is used numerically. Transverse contraction was introduced for infinite chains by representing the dynamics with \ac{MPS} extending along the time direction~\cite{banuls2009,muller_hermes2012}, and was subsequently developed to study temporal entropies~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024,carignano_tagliacozzo2025}. Our calculations build on the \texttt{ITransverse.jl} implementation~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}.

Reading the network along the spatial direction maps the contraction to an eigenproblem for the spatial transfer matrix, whose dominant eigenvectors define the temporal boundary states. These states determine the \ac{RTM} used to compute the generalised temporal entropies. We finally compare two truncation schemes for controlling the bond dimension of the temporal states.

\subsection{The rotated network}
\label{sec:transverse:network}

The time evolution of a $D$-dimensional system is represented by a $(D{+}1)$-dimensional tensor network, with the additional dimension corresponding to time. For the one-dimensional chain studied here, the result is a two-dimensional network with no open physical legs. The echo is a scalar amplitude, so this network is analogous to the partition function of a two-dimensional classical model~\cite{fradkin2013}. Its exact contraction is \#P-hard in general~\cite{schuch2007}, so we must use an approximate representation with a controlled bond dimension (Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}).

The tensor-network representation of the echo follows directly from Eq.~\eqref{eq:echo} and is pictured in Figure~\ref{fig:echo_strip}a. The initial and final states are represented as \acp{MPS}, while the evolution operator is decomposed into a sequence of \ac{MPO} layers, one for each Trotter step. Because we require the Hamiltonian to be translation invariant and time independent, the same bulk tensor $W$ appears at every spatial site and time step. Section~\ref{sec:mporep} constructs this tensor for a model with \ac{NNN} interactions.

In conventional evolution algorithms, successive \ac{MPO} layers of $U(\delta t)$ are applied to $\ket{\Psi_0}$. The spatial entanglement then grows linearly in time~\cite{calabrese_cardy2005}, so the bond dimension needed to represent the state grows exponentially~\cite{schuch2008,paeckel2019}. Transverse contraction instead contracts the same network column by column along the spatial direction. The tensors remain unchanged, but their indices exchange roles: the virtual spatial bonds become physical temporal indices, while the physical spatial indices become virtual temporal bonds. Figure~\ref{fig:rotation} illustrates this exchange. The bond dimension of $U(\delta t)$ therefore sets the physical dimension of the temporal problem. Each bulk column defines a \ac{tMPO} $\mathcal{E}$ that acts as a transfer matrix along the original spatial direction, and translation invariance ensures that the same $\mathcal{E}$ is applied at every site.

  \label{fig:rotation}

These objects provide the lattice representation of the strip geometry introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cft}. The strip is infinite along the spatial direction, and applying infinitely many columns on either side of a cut projects the temporal boundary states $\bra{b_L}$ and $\ket{b_R}$ onto the dominant left and right eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$~\cite{hastings_mahajan2015,tang2025}, which we write $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$. These fixed points are independent of how the chain is terminated far from the cut. They encode the temporal boundary condition imposed by $\ket{\Psi_0}$ at the top and bottom edges. The strip width is set by the number of temporal sites, including the real-time evolution and the cooling sites associated with the regulator $\beta_0$. The \ac{CFT} constrains the spectrum of the column transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$, while the fixed points $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ are the states from which the echo and the temporal entropies are computed.

The row (Trotter-layer) and column transfer operators have different spectral properties. Each row represents the unitary Trotter step $U(\delta t)=e^{-iH\delta t}$ and therefore has eigenvalues of unit modulus, up to the error of its \ac{MPO} representation. By contrast, the column transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ is non-Hermitian and generally has complex eigenvalues with different moduli. The implications of its non-Hermiticity for the left and right eigenvectors are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:mporep} after the operator has been constructed explicitly.

\subsection{The dynamics as an eigenvalue problem}
\label{sec:transverse:eigenproblem}

Contracting the network at finite spatial size turns the echo into a sum over the spectrum of $\mathcal{E}$. For a chain of $N$ spatial sites,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(T)
  \;=\; \bra{b_L}\,\mathcal{E}(T)^{\,N}\,\ket{b_R}
  \;=\; \sum_i c_i\,\mu_i(T)^{\,N}
  \;=\; c_0\,\mu_0(T)^{\,N}
  \Big[\,1+\sum_{i\ge1}\frac{c_i}{c_0}\Big(\frac{\mu_i}{\mu_0}\Big)^{\!N}\,\Big],
  \label{eq:contraction}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ are the eigenvalues of $\mathcal{E}$, assumed diagonalisable, with left and right eigenvectors $\bra{\ell_i}$ and $\ket{r_i}$ normalised so that $\braket{\ell_i}{r_j}=\delta_{ij}$, and $c_i=\braket{b_L}{r_i}\braket{\ell_i}{b_R}$. The dominant pair is $\bra{\ell_0}=\bra{L}$ and $\ket{r_0}=\ket{R}$. If $\mu_0$ has the largest modulus, every other contribution is suppressed relative to it by $(|\mu_i|/|\mu_0|)^N$. The thermodynamic limit is therefore determined by the leading eigenvalue, and the intensive Loschmidt rate becomes
\begin{equation}
  \ell(T) = -\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log|\mathcal{L}(T)|
  = -\log|\mu_0(T)|,
  \label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
up to $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$ boundary corrections for a finite open chain. The calculation thus reduces to a spectral problem whose cost is independent of the spatial length.

Equation~\eqref{eq:contraction} reduces the contraction to the spectrum of $\mathcal{E}$. The modulus of $\mu_0$ gives the Loschmidt rate through Eq.~\eqref{eq:rate}, while Section~\ref{sec:cft} reads the central charge from its phase and the boundary dimensions from the phase gaps. The generalised temporal entropies instead require the dominant left and right eigenvectors. Our calculations must therefore resolve the leading eigenpairs of the non-Hermitian operator $\mathcal{E}$, which we obtain with the procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:numerics}.

\subsection{Temporal states and the \ac{RTM}}
\label{sec:transverse:entropies}

Each application of $\mathcal{E}$ increases the bond dimension of the temporal boundary states. The computational cost is therefore controlled by their temporal entanglement, defined across a cut in the time direction. Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal} showed that after a critical quench the generalised temporal entropies grow logarithmically with the evolution time. We now define the object from which these entropies are computed.

The dominant left and right fixed points of the non-Hermitian transfer matrix are distinct, and the echo depends on their overlap $\braket{L}{R}$. A \ac{RDM} formed from either state alone therefore does not contain the required information. The relevant object is instead the \ac{RTM} constructed jointly from the two temporal boundaries~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024,carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{T}_t = \frac{\operatorname{Tr}_{>t}\ket{R}\bra{L}}{\braket{L}{R}},
  \label{eq:rtm}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Tr}_{>t}$ traces over the $T-t$ temporal sites above the cut and leaves the $t$ sites below it open. Thus, $\mathcal{T}_t$ acts on the first $t$ temporal sites. The denominator normalises the matrix so that $\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{T}_t=1$. Since $\bra{L}$ is not the Hermitian conjugate of $\ket{R}$, $\mathcal{T}_t$ is generally non-Hermitian and may have complex eigenvalues. The consequences of this complex spectrum for truncation are discussed in the next subsection. Figure~\ref{fig:rtm_strip} shows the position of $\mathcal{T}_t$ within the strip geometry.

  \label{fig:rtm_strip}

The spectrum of $\mathcal{T}_t$ defines the generalised temporal entropies associated with the transition between the two boundary states. We use the R\'enyi-2 member of the family introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cft},
\begin{equation}
  S_{2}^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = -\log \operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathcal{T}_t^2\right).
  \label{eq:s2gen}
\end{equation}
When $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ differ, the local reduction of $\mathcal{T}_t$ preserves its singular values but not its eigenvalues, so its spectrum cannot be read from a local decomposition (Appendix~\ref{app:tensor_networks}). This is the member we can compute: $\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{T}_t^2)$ is evaluated by contracting two copies of the \ac{RTM}, without diagonalising it. The finite-time corrections to $S_2^{\mathrm{gen}}$ must be accounted for when comparing with the conformal predictions (Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}).

Once $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ have converged for a target evolution time $T$, we evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:s2gen} at every internal bond of the temporal chain, corresponding to cuts $t\in(0,T)$. The entropy is generally complex because $\mathcal{T}_t$ is non-Hermitian, so its real and imaginary parts are analysed separately as functions of the normalised cut position $t/T$.

The region represented by $\mathcal{T}_t$ touches the lower edge of the strip, so only one of its endpoints lies in the interior. This halves the logarithmic coefficient in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} relative to an interval with both endpoints in the bulk (Appendix~\ref{app:cft:maps}). The entropy-route central charges reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} are extracted using this geometry.

\subsection{Truncation based on RDM and RTM}
\label{sec:transverse:truncation}

We use two complementary truncation schemes based on the \ac{RDM} and the \ac{RTM}. The conventional \ac{RDM} truncation, available in most tensor-network libraries, brings a boundary \ac{MPS} into mixed canonical form and discards the smallest singular values of its orthogonality centre, giving the optimal low-rank approximation to that state~\cite{schollwock2011,vidal2003}. The gauge-invariant scheme implemented in \texttt{ITransverse.jl}~\cite{carignano2025itransverse} instead truncates the \ac{RTM} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rtm}, which depends on both temporal boundaries.

The difference between both schemes is in what they consider as relevant. The \ac{RDM} keeps the directions in which $\ket{R}$ or $\bra{L}$ has the largest weight, regardless of their contribution to $\braket{L}{R}$. The \ac{RTM} instead weights each direction by its contribution to this overlap, so a large component of $\ket{R}$ may have little importance if it is orthogonal to the corresponding component of $\bra{L}$ and can be discarded~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024,tang2025}. The \ac{RTM} therefore reaches a given accuracy at a smaller bond dimension, with the \ac{RDM} truncation being approximately an order of magnitude more expensive in our calculations (Appendix~\ref{app:failures}).

This reduction in cost comes at the expense of not having a variational guarantee for \ac{RTM} truncation. For a positive Hermitian \ac{RDM}, the eigenvalues are the squared Schmidt coefficients and coincide with its singular values, so retaining the largest gives the optimal approximation at fixed bond dimension. The \ac{RTM} on the other hand is non-Hermitian: its eigenvalues may be complex or negative and no longer correspond one to one with its singular values, so the truncation criterion carries no variational bound. We therefore use the \ac{RTM} scheme for production calculations and retain the \ac{RDM} scheme as a complementary check; Appendix~\ref{app:failures} verifies that the two agree at every evolution time where both were run. Appendix~\ref{app:tensor_networks} presents both truncations in detail, including their behaviour under gauge transformations and the conditions for optimality.
