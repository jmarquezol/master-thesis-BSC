\section{Time Evolution via Transverse Contraction}
\label{sec:transverse}

Section~\ref{sec:cft} showed that the Loschmidt echo of a critical quench encodes conformal data in the spectrum of a transfer matrix acting along the spatial direction of the corresponding strip. We now construct this transfer matrix and explain how it is used numerically. Transverse contraction was introduced for infinite chains by representing the dynamics with matrix product states extending along the time direction~\cite{banuls2009,muller_hermes2012}, and was subsequently developed to study temporal entropies~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024,carignano_tagliacozzo2025}. Our calculations use the \texttt{ITransverse.jl} implementation~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}.

The construction proceeds by rotating the space--time tensor network and identifying its spatial transfer matrix, its temporal boundary states, and the transition matrix that defines the generalized temporal entropies. We then compare the two truncation schemes used to control the bond dimension and formulate the contraction as an eigenvalue problem.

\subsection{The rotated network}
\label{sec:transverse:network}

Transverse contraction represents the time evolution of a $D$-dimensional system as a $(D{+}1)$-dimensional tensor network, with time providing the additional dimension. For the one-dimensional chain studied here, this gives a two-dimensional network that is a \ac{PEPS} up to the Trotter error. Exact contraction of a general \ac{PEPS} is \#P-hard~\cite{schuch2007}, so the calculation requires an approximate representation with a controlled bond dimension.

The network follows directly from Equation~\eqref{eq:echo}. The initial and final states are represented as \acp{MPS}, while the evolution operator is decomposed into a sequence of \ac{MPO} layers, one for each Trotter step. Because the Hamiltonian is translation invariant and time independent, the same bulk tensor $W$ appears at every spatial site and time step. Section~\ref{sec:mporep} constructs this tensor for the model with \ac{NNN} interactions.

In the conventional contraction, successive \ac{MPO} layers are applied to the initial \ac{MPS}. The resulting spatial entanglement grows with time and eventually makes the state intractable at fixed bond dimension. Transverse contraction instead rotates the network by $90^\circ$ and contracts it column by column. Under this rotation, the virtual spatial bonds become physical temporal indices, while the physical spatial indices become virtual temporal bonds (Figure~\ref{fig:rotation}). The boundary columns define the \acp{tMPS} $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$, and each bulk column defines a \ac{tMPO} $\mathcal{E}$ that acts as a transfer matrix along the original spatial direction. Translation invariance ensures that the same $\mathcal{E}$ is applied at every site.

These objects provide the lattice representation of the strip geometry introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cft}. The temporal states $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ encode the boundary conditions at the two edges of the strip. Its width is set by the number of temporal sites, including both the real-time evolution and the cooling sites associated with the regulator $\beta_0$. The column transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ is the operator whose spectrum is constrained by the \ac{CFT}, while the Loschmidt echo is obtained from the overlap $\braket{L}{R}$ after the spatial contraction.

The row and column transfer operators have different spectral properties. Each row represents the unitary Trotter step $U(\delta t)=e^{-iH\delta t}$ and therefore has eigenvalues of unit modulus. By contrast, the column transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ is non-Hermitian and generally has complex eigenvalues with different moduli. Prediction~4 of Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal} states that, after a critical quench, its leading eigenvalues approach a common modulus at long times. In this limit, $\mathcal{E}$ approaches a rescaled unitary operator. The implications of its non-Hermiticity for the left and right eigenvectors are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:mporep} after the operator has been constructed explicitly.

\subsection{Temporal states and the reduced transition matrix}
\label{sec:transverse:entropies}

Each application of $\mathcal{E}$ increases the bond dimension of the temporal boundary states. The computational cost is therefore controlled by their temporal entanglement, defined across a cut in the time direction. Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal} showed that the generalized temporal entropies grow logarithmically with the evolution time at a conformally invariant critical point, so the bond dimension required for a fixed accuracy grows polynomially rather than exponentially. We now define the object from which these entropies are computed.

An ordinary reduced density matrix of either boundary state is insufficient because the dominant left and right fixed points of the non-Hermitian transfer matrix are distinct. The Loschmidt echo depends on both through $\braket{L}{R}$, so the relevant object is the \ac{RTM} constructed jointly from the two temporal boundaries,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}_t = \frac{\ket{R}\bra{L}}{\braket{L}{R}},
    \label{eq:rtm}
\end{equation}
where the states are bipartitioned at the temporal cut $t$. This normalization gives $\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{T}_t=1$. Since $\bra{L}$ is not the Hermitian conjugate of $\ket{R}$, $\mathcal{T}_t$ is generally non-Hermitian and its eigenvalues may be complex. They cannot be interpreted as probabilities, and the usual Schmidt interpretation of an entanglement spectrum does not apply.

The spectrum of $\mathcal{T}_t$ defines the generalized temporal entropies associated with the transition between the two boundary states. The conformal prediction for this family was introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cft}. We use its R\'enyi-2 member,
\begin{equation}
    S_{2}^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = -\log \operatorname{Tr}\!\left(\mathcal{T}_t^2\right).
    \label{eq:s2gen}
\end{equation}
The quantity $\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{T}_t^2)$ can be evaluated by contracting two copies of the \ac{RTM}, without diagonalizing it or computing a matrix logarithm. By contrast, the generalized von Neumann entropy, $S_1^{\mathrm{gen}}=-\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{T}_t\log\mathcal{T}_t)$, requires the full spectrum of an exponentially large non-Hermitian matrix and is not used here. The same two-copy structure makes the R\'enyi-2 temporal entropy accessible through the experimental replica protocol proposed in Ref.~\cite{boucomas2026measuring}.

Once $\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$ have converged for a target evolution time $T$, we evaluate Equation~\eqref{eq:s2gen} at every internal bond of the temporal chain, corresponding to cuts $t\in(0,T)$. The entropy is generally complex because $\mathcal{T}_t$ is non-Hermitian, so its real and imaginary parts are analysed separately as functions of the normalized cut position. Our cuts extend from one edge of the strip to the temporal bond at $t$. The logarithmic slope in Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} therefore carries the boundary coefficient $\tfrac{c}{12}(1+1/n)$, which equals $c/8$ for $n=2$, rather than the coefficient for a bulk spatial interval. The central charges reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} are extracted using this convention.

\subsection{Truncation using the transition matrix}
\label{sec:transverse:truncation}

The temporal states must be truncated after each application of $\mathcal{E}$ to prevent their bond dimensions from growing without bound. Two local truncation criteria are available. The conventional scheme discards the smallest singular values obtained from the \ac{RDM} of an individual boundary state. The alternative constructs a local \ac{RTM} from both boundaries and truncates the components that contribute least to their joint contraction. We use both schemes, with the \ac{RTM} truncation as the production default, because their gauge properties, computational costs, and numerical stability differ.

The first distinction concerns gauge invariance. A tensor network may be regauged on any internal bond without changing the physical contraction. Under a general non-unitary gauge transformation, the \ac{RDM} of one temporal boundary transforms by congruence, which changes its spectrum. The corresponding Schmidt values therefore depend on the chosen gauge. The \ac{RTM}, by contrast, transforms by similarity, so its eigenvalues remain unchanged. Its spectrum is consequently the appropriate gauge-invariant quantity and is the object constrained by the \ac{CFT}. Appendix~\ref{app:sub:rtm_truncation} gives the derivation.

The second distinction is computational cost. The \ac{RTM} retains the components of each boundary that contribute to $\braket{L}{R}$, whereas the \ac{RDM} also represents components of $\ket{R}$ that are orthogonal to $\bra{L}$ and therefore do not contribute to the final contraction. The \ac{RDM} cost is controlled by the entanglement of an individual state, while the \ac{RTM} cost is governed by the smaller operator-space entanglement~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024}. In practice, this difference substantially reduces the bond dimension required by the \ac{RTM} scheme.

The third distinction is numerical stability. Truncation of a Hermitian positive \ac{RDM} inherits the variational control of standard tensor-network compression and generally converges smoothly. No analogous bound holds for the non-Hermitian \ac{RTM}. Its singular values and eigenvalues measure different properties, and components with large singular values may cancel in the trace that determines the physical overlap. Singular-value truncation of the \ac{RTM} is therefore a controlled numerical criterion rather than a variational approximation, and its convergence need not be monotonic. Appendix~\ref{app:sub:rtm_truncation} describes the local environment used for this truncation and discusses its limitations.

The \ac{RTM} scheme is thus gauge invariant and less expensive, but may converge less smoothly, whereas the \ac{RDM} scheme is more stable but requires a larger bond dimension. We use the \ac{RTM} for the production calculations and retain the \ac{RDM} as an independent check or as a fallback when the \ac{RTM} results become noisy. Section~\ref{sec:results} verifies that the two schemes agree in the regime where both converge.

\subsection{The dynamics as an eigenvalue problem}
\label{sec:transverse:eigenproblem}

Contracting the network along the spatial direction requires repeated applications of the same transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$. In the thermodynamic limit, this contraction is determined by its leading left and right eigenvectors, to which the temporal boundary states converge. The many-body dynamics relevant to the Loschmidt echo is thereby reduced to an eigenvalue problem for a single \ac{tMPO}, and the computational cost becomes independent of the spatial length of the chain.

The leading eigenvectors can be approximated with a tensor-network power method, in which one column is absorbed into each boundary state before truncation. Its convergence rate is controlled by $|\mu_1/\mu_0|$, where $\mu_0$ and $\mu_1$ are the two eigenvalues of largest modulus. The method is efficient when this ratio is well below one. At a critical quench, however, Prediction~4 of Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal} implies that the leading eigenvalues approach a common modulus as $T$ increases. The single-vector iteration therefore slows down in the same long-time regime in which the conformal spectrum emerges and may converge to a mixture of nearby eigenvectors.

We address this near-degeneracy by iterating a block of $k$ states that spans the leading invariant subspace. Convergence is then governed by the spectral separation between this block and the remaining eigenvalues, rather than by the smaller separations within the block. Since $\mathcal{E}$ is non-Hermitian, the left and right subspaces must be evolved and paired biorthogonally. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} develops the resulting block power method and the stabilization procedures used in the numerical calculations.
