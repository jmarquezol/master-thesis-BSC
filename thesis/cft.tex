\section{Universal Data from the Dynamics}
\label{sec:cft}

The real-time dynamics of a lattice model, however microscopic and messy, carries sharp universal information when the model is critical, and this information can be read directly from a simulation. At a continuous critical point, the lattice model forgets its microscopic details and is described by a \ac{CFT}---a continuum theory so constrained by symmetry that many of its predictions are fixed by a single number, the central charge $c$, together with a discrete list of operators. These quantities are universal: every model in the same universality class must reproduce them, which is exactly what makes them good targets for a numerical method. This section explains how a dynamical quantity, the Loschmidt echo, maps onto such a theory; what universal data the theory predicts; and how that data can be separated from the non-universal lattice constants present in any real simulation. The resulting predictions are tested in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{From the Loschmidt echo to a boundary CFT}
\label{sec:cft:boundary}

The object we compute is the Loschmidt echo introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro},
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(T) = \bra{\Psi_0}\, e^{-iHT} \ket{\Psi_0},
    \label{eq:echo}
\end{equation}
the amplitude for the system, prepared in an initial product state $\ket{\Psi_0}$, to return to that state after evolving for a time $T$ with the critical Hamiltonian. To connect this amplitude with the continuum theory, we rotate the evolution time to the imaginary axis, $T\to-i\tau$ (a Wick rotation). The real-time propagator $e^{-iHT}$ then becomes the Euclidean weight $e^{-\tau H}$, the same operator that generates the thermal partition function of a two-dimensional statistical system. Summing this weight over configurations while holding both ends of the evolution in the state $\ket{\Psi_0}$ expresses the echo as a Euclidean path integral on a slab of width $\tau$, with both edges fixed to $\ket{\Psi_0}$. In \ac{CFT} language, a state that can terminate this path integral is a conformal boundary state. The admissible choices are the Cardy states~\cite{cardy1989}, which represent the allowed boundary conditions of the theory. The echo is therefore the partition function of the critical theory on a long strip whose two edges carry the boundary condition set by $\ket{\Psi_0}$ and whose width is the (imaginary) evolution time.

Two features of this construction matter for the numerics that follow. First, a sharp product state has structure down to the lattice spacing and is not, by itself, a smooth conformal boundary state. We therefore pre-cool it in imaginary time for a short interval $\beta_0$, which on the lattice amounts to $N_\beta = 2\beta_0/\delta t$ imaginary-time sites split between the two ends. The strip width becomes $T+2\beta_0$, and the conformal predictions emerge as $\beta_0$ is reduced or the final time $T$ is increased. Without this UV regulator, short-distance lattice effects contaminate the boundary condition and obscure the universal signal. Second, the resulting boundary condition depends strongly on $\ket{\Psi_0}$. Each initial state realizes a particular Cardy state, whose lightest boundary operator has a dimension $x_1$ that controls how quickly finite-size corrections decay. For the Ising class, the disordered (free) boundary has $x_1=\tfrac12$ and converges quickly, whereas the ordered (fixed) boundary has $x_1=2$ and converges slowly. In the $\sigma^x$-field convention used for our model, the polarized $\ket{X+}$ state realizes the free boundary, which is why we quench from it.

The strip becomes computationally useful when we view it in the transverse direction. In the Euclidean description, space and imaginary time are coordinates of the same two-dimensional geometry, so either one can be chosen as the direction of propagation. In the transverse picture adopted in this work, we exchange their usual roles and contract the network along the original spatial direction. This defines a transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ that propagates from one spatial column to the next while acting on states supported on a temporal slice. Its tensor-network construction is developed in Section~\ref{sec:transverse}.

The value of this description is that conformal symmetry fixes the spectrum of $\mathcal{E}$. In two dimensions, this symmetry constrains a critical theory almost completely (Appendix~\ref{app:cft:maps};~\cite{bpz1984, difrancesco1997}). In a simulation, those constraints appear through two channels: the transfer-matrix spectrum and the scaling of entanglement.

Confining a \ac{CFT} to a strip of transverse size $\beta$ shifts its ground-state energy by an amount proportional to the central charge $c$, the single label of the universality class ($c=\tfrac12$ for Ising). This finite-size effect is the conformal anomaly. Quantizing on the strip makes it explicit: the generator of translations along the strip is~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}
\begin{equation}
    \hat H = \frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(L_0 - \frac{c}{24}\Big),
    \label{eq:cft:casimir}
\end{equation}
where $L_0$ is the scaling generator. This expression contains the two kinds of universal data we seek: the constant $-c/24$ captures the anomaly and hence the central charge $c$, while the spectrum of $L_0$ encodes the operator content.

A \ac{CFT} is organized around primary operators, each labelled by a scaling dimension
\begin{equation}
    x = h + \bar h,
    \label{eq:cft:dim}
\end{equation}
built from the holomorphic and antiholomorphic weights $h$ and $\bar h$. This dimension fixes the power law of the operator's correlations. The full list of dimensions is a fingerprint of the theory and can be read from the excited levels of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir}, stacked above the anomaly offset.

Our transfer matrix inherits that structure directly~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E} = e^{-\delta t\,\hat H}= \exp\!\Big[-\big(-\kappa + \pi L_0\big)\,g\Big],
    \qquad \kappa = \frac{\pi c}{24},
    \label{eq:cft:transfer}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the pure Casimir number and $g$ is a geometric factor set by the time step $\delta t$, the strip width, the regulator $\beta_0$, and the velocity $v$. The Casimir term appears in the dominant eigenvalue and carries $c$, while the gaps to the subleading eigenvalues encode the dimensions $x$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cft:spectrum}). A single diagonalisation therefore yields both kinds of universal data, which appear in the eigenvalue moduli or phases depending on the real-time continuation.

Entanglement provides the second channel. When a state is divided at a cut, the correlations between the two sides are measured by the entanglement entropy or, more generally, by the R\'enyi entropies $S_n$. This one-parameter family is labelled by an index $n$ and includes the von Neumann entropy in the limit $n\to1$. In a \ac{CFT}, computing $S_n$ amounts to evaluating a correlation function of twist operators---primary fields inserted at the two ends of the cut---whose scaling dimension is~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_n = \frac{c}{12}\Big(n - \frac{1}{n}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:twist}
\end{equation}
Since a primary correlation function decays as a power law set by its dimension, the entropy grows as the logarithm of the subsystem size, with a coefficient proportional to $c$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cft:twist}). This is the mechanism behind every entropy-based estimate of $c$ in this thesis; the precise coefficient, which depends on $n$ and on whether the cut lies in the bulk or at a boundary, is quoted with the predictions in Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}.

We use different members of this family in the equilibrium and dynamical settings. The equilibrium central charge (Section~\ref{sec:annni}) is extracted from the ground-state von Neumann entropy ($n\to1$), which \ac{DMRG} provides directly. For every temporal entropy in this thesis---both at the $p=0$ Ising benchmark and away from it---we instead use the R\'enyi-2 entropy ($n=2$), computed through the same construction at all $p$. We choose it because it avoids the matrix logarithm required for the von Neumann entropy, is numerically robust, and can be accessed experimentally through the two-replica protocol of Ref.~\cite{boucomas2026measuring}. We return to this choice when defining the temporal entropy in Section~\ref{sec:transverse}.

\subsection{Universal versus non-universal constants}
\label{sec:cft:universal}

The results above are exact only in the continuum limit. A simulation instead runs on a microscopic lattice, at finite evolution time $T$, bond dimension $\chi$ and Trotter step $\delta t$, and with a nonzero regulator $\beta_0$. Its measurements therefore contain both universal and non-universal contributions. The universal quantities, fixed by the universality class, are the central charge $c$, the scaling dimensions $x_i$, the logarithmic coefficients, the Casimir number $\kappa=\pi c/24$, and the pattern by which the leading eigenvalue moduli become degenerate (emergent dual unitarity, the fourth prediction of Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}). The non-universal quantities introduced by the lattice and the algorithm are the regulator $\beta_0$, the entropy offset $s_0$, the Trotter step $\delta t$, the sound velocity $v$, and the overall normalization of the transfer matrix. This normalization is model dependent and causes the modulus of the dominant eigenvalue $\mu_0$ to differ from one. How well these contributions can be separated determines which predictions can be compared with a simulation and how that comparison should be made.

The sound velocity plays a distinct role. Although it is non-universal and varies with $p$ (Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity}), it can be measured independently from the equilibrium dispersion. Since the eigenvalue phases depend on the combination $\pi x/(vT)$, the velocity is needed to convert a measured phase gap into the universal scaling dimension $x$.

This separation is possible because the universal and non-universal contributions affect the spectrum differently. The overall normalization of the transfer matrix multiplies every eigenvalue by the same constant: it rescales all the moduli $|\mu_i|$ while leaving the phases untouched. The universal level structure therefore appears in the phases, and we read scaling dimensions from phase differences, using the moduli only through ratios or their limiting behaviour.

Ratios remove more than the normalization. A single phase gap contains a universal dimension, but also depends on the regulator $\beta_0$ and the velocity. The ratio of two gaps, $(x_i-x_0)/(x_j-x_0)$, is independent of all three at once. It therefore probes the operator content without any external input and provides the cleanest measurement available to us.

Not every quantity can be turned into a ratio of this kind. The entropy slope measures $c$ directly, but at finite $(T,\chi,\delta t)$ it is not the continuum value $c/8$: finite-time, truncation and Trotter corrections multiply it. We write its leading behaviour as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{slope}(p; T, \chi, \delta t) = \frac{c(p)}{8}\,K(T,\chi,\delta t,\beta_0) + \text{(subleading)},
    \label{eq:cft:calib}
\end{equation}
where $K$ collects the corrections shared by models in the same universality class. We run the integrable point $p=0$, for which $c=\tfrac12$, through the same pipeline at matched $(T,\chi,\delta t,\beta_0)$. Taking the ratio then cancels the common factor and yields
\begin{equation}
    c(p) \approx \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{slope}(p)}{\mathrm{slope}(0)}.
    \label{eq:cft:calibratio}
\end{equation}
The remaining uncertainty comes from coupling-dependent subleading terms, which do not cancel completely.

The corrections collected in $K$ need not be treated as noise, because their functional form is itself fixed by the \ac{CFT}~\cite{boucomas2026}. They can therefore either be cancelled through the ratios above or included explicitly in the fit, allowing $c$ to be extracted without calibration. The two routes have different costs: modelling the correction gives an absolute estimate but requires a sufficiently long window in $T$ to separate it from the leading term, whereas calibration works at shorter times but requires a reference simulation at $p=0$. An absolute extraction from the leading eigenvalue also requires a constant branch offset to be fixed first, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:eq3}.

\subsection{The universal predictions}
\label{sec:cft:temporal}
\label{sec:emergent}

Conformal entropies on a finite interval are naturally expressed through the chord variable
\begin{equation}
    W(x,\ell) \;=\; \log\!\left[\frac{2\ell}{\pi}\,\sin\frac{\pi x}{\ell}\right],
    \label{eq:chord}
\end{equation}
the logarithm of the chord subtended by a cut at position $x$ in a system of extent $\ell$. We use
it throughout, both for the temporal cuts here and for the spatial ones of
Section~\ref{sec:annni:equilibrium}.

In the transverse description of the strip, the generalized R\'enyi-$n$ entropy across a temporal cut takes the form~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025, boucomas2026}
\begin{equation}
    S_n^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{i\pi c}{24}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)
      + \frac{c}{12}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\,W(t,T).
    \label{eq:cft:sgen}
\end{equation}
Because the separation is timelike, the entropy grows logarithmically with the total evolution time $T$ rather than linearly, which is what allows the transverse contraction to remain efficient.

Combined with the transfer spectrum in Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer}, this result leads to four predictions, all of which are tested in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Three concern the transfer eigenvalues $\mu_i$, which we order by decreasing modulus. In practice, we work with their logarithms, $\lambda_i = \log(-\mu_i)$, so that $\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_i = \arg(-\mu_i)$ gives the phase of $\mu_i$ measured from the negative real axis. The minus sign follows the convention of Ref.~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025} and adds the same shift of $\pi$ to every phase. It therefore cancels from the phase differences $\mathrm{Im}(\lambda_i-\lambda_0)$ used below, but must be accounted for when $\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0$ is considered on its own (Appendix~\ref{app:eq3}).

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The generalized-entropy profile. Separating Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:sgen} into real and imaginary parts gives
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{Re}\,S_n^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{c}{12}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\,W(t,T),
        \qquad
        \mathrm{Im}\,S_n^{\mathrm{gen}} = \frac{\pi c}{24}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big).
        \label{eq:cft:chord}
    \end{equation}
    The real part follows a symmetric chord profile. When plotted against $W(t,T)$, it becomes a straight line with slope $c/8$ for the R\'enyi-2 entropy used here. We extract $c$ either through the calibration in Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:calibratio} or by including the finite-time correction explicitly. The imaginary part does not depend on $t$ at all: it forms a plateau at $\pi c/16$~\cite{boucomas2026} and therefore provides a second estimate of $c$ from the same data, without fitting the profile.

    \item The central charge from the leading eigenvalue. The imaginary part of the dominant eigenvalue obeys
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0}{T} = a_0 + \frac{C}{T^2} + \frac{a_4}{T^4} + \cdots,
        \qquad C = -\frac{\kappa}{v} = -\frac{\pi c}{24\,v}.
        \label{eq:cft:eq3}
    \end{equation}
    Here $a_0$ is the non-universal extensive contribution, while the coefficient of $1/T^2$ carries the Casimir number divided by the velocity. Once $v$ has been measured independently (Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity}), the relation $c = 24\,v\,|C|/\pi$ determines the central charge from the eigenvalue phase alone, without relying on the entropy. Because this term is small, the extraction requires a nonzero $\beta_0$, sufficiently long times, and a fixed branch offset, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:eq3}.

    \item The boundary exponent from the first gap. The gap between the leading and first subleading eigenvalues measures the lightest boundary dimension,
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{Im}(\lambda_1-\lambda_0) = -\frac{\pi x_1}{v\,T}.
        \label{eq:cft:eq4}
    \end{equation}
    Once the sound velocity is known, this phase gap determines $x_1$. More generally, the subleading eigenvalues reproduce the tower of boundary scaling dimensions through phase gaps of the form $\pi x/(vT)$.

    \item Emergent dual unitarity. Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} also fixes how the spectrum evolves with $T$: the moduli of the leading eigenvalues approach a common value while their phases stay distinct,
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{|\mu_i(T)|}{|\mu_0(T)|}
            = 1 - \frac{a_i}{T} - \frac{b_i}{T^2} - \cdots
            \;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\; 1 .
        \label{eq:gapclose}
    \end{equation}
    Equal moduli with distinct phases indicate that the temporal transfer matrix approaches a unitary operator up to an overall (model-dependent) normalization. This emergent dual unitarity follows from conformal invariance and accounts for the logarithmic entropy growth above. The closing of the modulus gaps also makes the leading eigenvectors increasingly difficult to resolve, which ultimately limits the numerical method (Section~\ref{sec:numerics}). We test this behaviour by following the dominant eigenvalue $\mu_0(T)$ in the complex plane, where it approaches a trajectory of nearly constant radius with a winding phase.
\end{enumerate}

Before applying these predictions, Section~\ref{sec:annni} verifies that the ANNNI-type chain is critical, establishes that it belongs to the Ising universality class, and determines the model's frustration-dependent velocity, $v(p)$.
