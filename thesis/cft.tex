\section{Conformal Field Theory and Universal Dynamics}
\label{sec:cft}

The rest of this thesis is, in the end, an elaborate way of measuring a single number---the central charge $c$---from the temporal direction of a quench, and of asking whether that number survives the breaking of integrability. This section collects the conformal-field-theory background needed to make that quantitative: where these universal numbers come from, and how they turn into the handful of formulas we later fit. We keep the main line of reasoning here and defer the supporting derivations---the stress-tensor origin of the spectrum, the twist-field origin of the entropy coefficient, and the conformal maps behind both---to Appendix~\ref{app:cft}, to which every result below points.

The one-paragraph version is this. At a continuous critical point a lattice model forgets its microscopic details and is governed by a \emph{conformal field theory}: a continuum theory whose enormous symmetry, in two dimensions, fixes almost everything in terms of $c$ and a discrete list of operators. Our whole approach exploits a fortunate accident of Euclidean physics---that after a Wick rotation time and space play interchangeable roles---so that the same CFT formulas that describe a spatial slice also describe a temporal one. The eigenvalues of the transverse transfer matrix are then, quite literally, the CFT's list of operators, and the temporal entropy is a CFT correlation function. Everything below unpacks that sentence.

\subsection{Criticality, scale invariance, and universality}
\label{sec:cft:criticality}

Away from a phase transition a spin chain has a finite correlation length $\xi$: spins farther apart than $\xi$ are essentially independent, and microscopic details (the exact couplings, the lattice spacing) still matter. At a continuous critical point $\xi\to\infty$. There is now no intrinsic length scale between the lattice spacing and the system size, and a system with no scale looks the same at every magnification---it is \emph{scale invariant}. This is the engine of \emph{universality}: if we blur the lattice into a continuum, the microscopic details wash out and what survives is a continuum field theory that depends only on the symmetries and dimensionality of the problem. Wildly different microscopic models---the Ising chain, a lattice gas, or the ANNNI-type chain we study in Section~\ref{sec:annni} at small frustration---can flow to the \emph{same} continuum theory, and belong to the same \emph{universality class}. That continuum theory is massless (no scale means no gap), and promoting ``scale invariance plus locality'' to its full local form yields \emph{conformal invariance}: the theory is a conformal field theory (CFT). This is precisely why we may replace the messy lattice Hamiltonian by a clean CFT when predicting universal quantities, and why a number computed once in the Ising CFT can be compared directly against numbers extracted from our ANNNI-type simulations.

\subsection{Conformal invariance in two dimensions, and the central charge}
\label{sec:cft:2d}

A conformal transformation preserves angles but not lengths: it can stretch, but only locally isotropically. In a generic number of dimensions the conformal group is finite-dimensional and only mildly constraining. Two dimensions is the exception, and the exception is the whole story: there, ``locally rotate and scale'' is exactly what multiplication by the derivative $f'(z)$ of a holomorphic function of $z=x+i\tau$ does, so \emph{every} analytic map $z\mapsto f(z)$ is a symmetry. The symmetry algebra becomes infinite-dimensional---the Virasoro algebra---and constrains the theory so tightly that 2D CFTs are essentially solvable~\cite{bpz1984, difrancesco1997}. Two habits of thought follow, and we use both: one works on the complex plane, where quantities factorize into holomorphic ($z$) and antiholomorphic ($\bar z$) pieces; and one changes geometry by changing coordinates, so that a correlation function known on the plane is known on any shape an analytic map can reach (a cylinder, a strip). This ``conformal map'' trick, spelled out in Appendix~\ref{app:cft:maps}, is what takes textbook plane results to the specific strip geometry of our quench.

One remark will matter a great deal later. In \emph{Euclidean} CFT the two coordinates $x$ and $\tau$ enter completely symmetrically; nothing intrinsically distinguishes ``space'' from ``imaginary time''. That democracy is exactly what makes the transverse, or temporal, contraction legitimate (Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}): we are free to call whichever direction we like ``space'' and quantize along the other.

Every 2D CFT carries a single most-important number, the central charge $c$, which can be read in several equivalent ways. It \emph{labels the universality class}: fixing $c$ together with the operator content essentially fixes the theory. For the models relevant here,
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
model & universality class & central charge $c$ \\
\hline
transverse-field Ising ($p=0$) & Ising & $\tfrac{1}{2}$ \\
ANNNI-type at small $p$ & Ising & $\tfrac{1}{2}$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
so the central question of this work---does temporal Ising universality survive next-nearest-neighbour frustration?---is, concretely, whether the ANNNI-type chain still returns $c=\tfrac12$ when the central charge is measured through \emph{time}. Second, $c$ is a \emph{conformal anomaly}: putting the theory on a finite or curved region costs a universal ground-state (Casimir) energy proportional to $c$, which is the most direct handle we have and exactly how $c$ enters the transfer-matrix spectrum below. Finally, $c$ loosely \emph{counts gapless degrees of freedom} ($c=\tfrac12$ is a single Majorana/Ising mode), so larger $c$ means more entanglement and more cost. Throughout, $c$ is the target: nearly every formula below is a way to isolate it.

\subsection{Operators and the transfer-matrix bridge}
\label{sec:cft:operators}

A CFT is organized not around particles but around local \emph{primary} operators, each carrying two conformal weights $(h,\bar h)$ whose sum
\begin{equation}
    x = h + \bar h
    \label{eq:cft:dim}
\end{equation}
is the \emph{scaling dimension}: scale invariance forces every two-point function to be a pure power law set by $x$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cft:ops}), so a small $x$ means a slowly decaying, ``light'' operator and a large $x$ a fast-decaying, ``heavy'' one. The list of primary operators, with their dimensions, is a fixed fingerprint of the CFT---and, as the next subsection makes precise, it is \emph{literally} the list of eigenvalues of our transfer matrix:
\begin{quote}
operators $\leftrightarrow$ transfer-matrix eigenvalues.
\end{quote}
That correspondence is the single most important bridge in the method: it is why staring at the spectrum of a matrix tells us about a continuum field theory.

\subsection{The transfer-matrix spectrum}
\label{sec:cft:spectrum}

The abstract central charge turns into a fittable number through the stress-energy tensor, the operator that generates conformal transformations and whose modes generate the Virasoro algebra; the scaling generator $L_0$ has the operator dimensions as its spectrum. Quantizing on a strip of transverse size $\beta$---the geometry of our quench (Section~\ref{sec:cft:boundary})---the generator of translations along it is, up to the sound velocity $v$, an integral of the stress tensor, and the conformal map from the plane leaves behind one extra, purely geometric term: the Casimir anomaly proportional to $c$~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
    \hat H = \frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(L_0 - \frac{c}{24}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:casimir}
\end{equation}
This equation is the crux. The $-c/24$ term is a universal ground-state offset set only by $c$ and $\beta$; isolate it and you have measured $c$. The $L_0$ term adds the operator dimensions $x$ on top, as the excited levels. Since the transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ is essentially $e^{-\delta t\,\hat H}$ (it advances the system one step along the strip; Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}), Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir} hands us its entire spectrum at once: a dominant eigenvalue whose modulus encodes the Casimir (hence $c$) term, and a tower of subleading eigenvalues whose ratios and phases encode the operator dimensions. Written out~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E} = \exp\!\Big[-\big(-\kappa + \pi L_0\big)\cdot(\text{geometry})\Big],
    \qquad \kappa = -\frac{\pi c\,\delta t}{24},
    \label{eq:cft:transfer}
\end{equation}
the geometric factor collecting the strip size and $v$ (derivation in Appendix~\ref{app:cft:spectrum}). This is the origin of the two independent routes by which we read $c$ in Section~\ref{sec:results}: one fits $\kappa$ directly out of the leading eigenvalue, the other uses the entanglement, through the twist operators we introduce next. Same $c$, two windows onto it.

\subsection{Entanglement and the entropy coefficients}
\label{sec:cft:entropy}

Entanglement knows about $c$ because computing it forces one to evaluate a correlation function of special operators whose dimension is built from $c$. In the replica approach of Calabrese and Cardy~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}, the $n$-th R\'enyi moment $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}$ of an interval $\mathcal A$ is computed by inserting a pair of \emph{twist operators} $\sigma_n$ at its endpoints; the twist operator is itself a primary, with dimension
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_n = \frac{c}{12}\Big(n - \frac{1}{n}\Big),
    \label{eq:cft:twist}
\end{equation}
and its power-law two-point function yields the familiar logarithmic entanglement entropy whose slope is proportional to $c$ (full derivation in Appendix~\ref{app:cft:twist}). Two things carry forward. First, \emph{the entropy is a logarithm whose slope measures $c$}---the entire logic of the entropy route and of the equilibrium fits of Section~\ref{sec:annni}. Second---and this requires care---the R\'enyi order $n$ enters through a known factor. For a boundary (half-line) cut, which is our situation in the temporal problem, the coefficient is halved relative to the bulk, giving the family
\begin{equation}
    \frac{c}{12}\,\frac{n+1}{n},
    \qquad
    \text{i.e.}\quad \frac{c}{6}\ (n\to1), \qquad \frac{c}{8}\ (n=2).
    \label{eq:cft:coeff}
\end{equation}
This is exactly why we use $c/6$ for the $n\to1$ generalized entropy and $c/8$ for the generalized R\'enyi-2 entropy we actually compute---\emph{not} the bulk value $c/3$. It is a small factor, but the wrong one shifts every extracted central charge.

\subsection{The quench as a boundary state}
\label{sec:cft:boundary}

We can now specialize to our problem, the Loschmidt echo $\mathcal L(t)=\bra{\Psi_0}e^{-i\hat H t}\ket{\Psi_0}$: the amplitude to return to the initial product state $\ket{\Psi_0}=\ket{X+}^{\otimes N}$ after evolving with the \emph{critical} Hamiltonian. Written as a Euclidean path integral (through the rotation $t\to-i\tau$), the two ends of the evolution are copies of $\ket{\Psi_0}$. In CFT language a state that can terminate a path integral is a \emph{conformal boundary state} $\ket b$, and the consistent ones---the Cardy states~\cite{cardy1989}---correspond to allowed boundary conditions. The upshot is a clean geometric picture:
\begin{quote}
the initial product state acts as a conformally invariant boundary condition, and the echo is the partition function of the CFT on an infinitely long \emph{strip} whose two edges carry $\ket b$, with a width set by the (imaginary) evolution time.
\end{quote}

Two ingredients decorate this picture, and both recur throughout the numerics. The first is a UV regulator $\beta_0$: a sharp product state has structure down to the lattice scale, so we pre-evolve it in imaginary time for a short amount $\beta_0$, cooling it into a smooth boundary state. On the lattice this is $N_\beta = 2\beta_0/\delta t$ imaginary-time sites split between the two ends; the strip width is then $T+2\beta_0$, and the CFT predictions are recovered at small $\beta_0$. This is why every production run uses a nonzero $\beta_0$: with $\beta_0=0$ the conformal boundary condition is dirty and the universal predictions are spoiled. The second is the \emph{choice} of boundary condition. Different initial states are different Cardy states; the lightest boundary operator has a boundary scaling dimension $x_1$ that controls how fast finite-size corrections decay. For the Ising CFT the disordered (\emph{free}) boundary has $x_1=\tfrac12$ and converges fast, whereas the ordered (\emph{fixed}) boundary has $x_1=2$ and converges slowly. In the $\sigma^x$-field convention of our model the field-polarized $\ket{X+}$ is the \emph{free} boundary with $x_1=\tfrac12$, which is why we quench from it and why measuring $x_1\approx\tfrac12$ in Section~\ref{sec:results} confirms we have the boundary physics right.

\subsection{The temporal reading, and what we will fit}
\label{sec:cft:temporal}

Everything so far was standard CFT. The move that defines our method is to read the strip \emph{sideways}. Since the two Euclidean coordinates are interchangeable (Section~\ref{sec:cft:2d}), instead of treating the strip's long direction as imaginary time and quantizing along space, we do the opposite and build a transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ that advances along the \emph{temporal} direction, acting on states that live on a time-like slice---the temporal matrix product states of Section~\ref{sec:transverse}. Because it is the same CFT seen along the other axis, all the machinery above applies verbatim with space and time swapped: the eigenvalues of $\mathcal{E}$ are the operator content, and the entanglement is again a twist-field correlation function.

There is one genuinely new twist, and it is the reason the whole approach can be efficient. The relevant object is not an ordinary reduced density matrix but the reduced transition matrix built from \emph{both} dominant vectors (Section~\ref{sec:transverse}), and its generalized entropies are computed by a twist-field two-point function for a \emph{time-like} separation---the $\sin$ (rather than $\sinh$) version of the cylinder correlator. Inserting the twist dimension~\eqref{eq:cft:twist} and continuing $n\to1$ gives the master formula of the whole approach, the strip generalized entropy~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
    S^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{i\pi c}{12}
      + \frac{c}{6}\,\log\!\Big[\frac{2T}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi t}{T}\Big].
    \label{eq:cft:sgen}
\end{equation}
The striking fact is hidden in ``time-like'': because the separation is temporal, the entropy grows only logarithmically in $T$, rather than linearly as an ordinary spatial entanglement would after a quench. Logarithmic growth means a polynomially bounded bond dimension, which is the reason the transverse contraction is efficient at all. The \emph{entanglement barrier} of this thesis is precisely the onset of the regime where this favourable logarithm is about to be spoiled.

We collect the concrete predictions we fit against the numerics in Section~\ref{sec:results}; all descend from Equations~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} and~\eqref{eq:cft:sgen}~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}.

\emph{Central charge from the leading eigenvalue.} Writing $\lambda_i=\log(-\tau_i)$ for the transfer eigenvalues $\tau_i$, the imaginary part of the leading one obeys
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0}{T} = a_0 + \frac{\kappa}{T^2} + \frac{a_4}{T^4} + \cdots
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    c = \frac{24\,v\,|\kappa|}{\pi\,\delta t},
    \label{eq:cft:eq3}
\end{equation}
so isolating the universal $1/T^2$ Casimir coefficient $\kappa$ and converting it gives $c$ directly. This eigenvalue route needs a nonzero $\beta_0$ and long times, because the tiny $\kappa/T^2$ sits under a large non-universal constant.

\emph{Boundary exponent from the first gap.} The gap between the leading and first-subleading eigenvalue measures the lightest boundary operator dimension,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Im}(\lambda_1-\lambda_0) = -\frac{\pi x_1}{v\,T},
    \label{eq:cft:eq4}
\end{equation}
so recovering $x_1\approx\tfrac12$ confirms the free boundary condition, i.e.\ that $\ket{X+}$ is the boundary state we think it is.

\emph{The generalized-entropy chord.} Splitting Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:sgen} into real and imaginary parts,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Re}\,S^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{c}{6}\log\!\Big[\frac{2T}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi t}{T}\Big],
    \qquad
    \mathrm{Im}\,S^{\mathrm{gen}} = \frac{\pi c}{12}.
    \label{eq:cft:chord}
\end{equation}
The real part is a \emph{chord}: plotted against $\log[\tfrac{2T}{\pi}\sin\tfrac{\pi t}{T}]$ it is a straight line of slope $c/6$ for the $n\to1$ entropy, and $c/8$ for the generalized R\'enyi-2 entropy we compute (Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:coeff}). The imaginary part is a flat plateau at $\pi c/12$, which for $c=\tfrac12$ is $\pi/24\approx0.131$---an independent read of $c$ needing no fit. The ``conformal dome'' in every entropy plot below is this chord, peaking at the midpoint $t=T/2$ and symmetric about it.

Finally, one physical hallmark ties these together. From Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer}, as $T$ grows the transfer eigenvalues $\tau_i$ migrate onto the unit circle: their moduli tend to one, with only their phases (the operator dimensions) distinguishing them. A spectrum on the unit circle is unitary, so the temporal transfer matrix becomes effectively unitary at long times---\emph{emergent dual unitarity}, the physical fingerprint of a clean quench to a conformal point. As we will see in Section~\ref{sec:emergent}, it is also a double-edged sword: the very degeneracy of moduli that signals the emergent conformal structure is what eventually makes the transfer matrix hard to resolve, and it is the spectral root of the entanglement barrier.
