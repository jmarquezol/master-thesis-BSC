\section{Universal Data from the Dynamics}
\label{sec:cft}

The real-time dynamics of a lattice model, however microscopic and messy, carries sharp universal information when the model is critical, and this information can be read directly off a simulation. The reason is that at a continuous critical point a lattice model forgets its microscopic details and is described by a \ac{CFT}---a continuum theory so constrained by its symmetry that many of its predictions are fixed by a single number, the central charge $c$, together with a discrete list of operators. These numbers are universal: every model in the same universality class must reproduce them, which is exactly what makes them good targets for a numerical method. This section explains how a dynamical quantity, the Loschmidt echo, is mapped onto such a theory; what universal data the theory then predicts; and how that data is separated from the non-universal lattice constants that come with it in any real simulation. The predictions collected at the end are what Section~\ref{sec:results} measures.

\subsection{From the Loschmidt echo to a boundary CFT}
\label{sec:cft:boundary}

The object we compute is the Loschmidt echo introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro},
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(T) = \bra{\Psi_0}\, e^{-iHT} \ket{\Psi_0},
    \label{eq:echo}
\end{equation}
the amplitude for the system, prepared in an initial product state, to return to it after evolving for a time $T$ with the critical Hamiltonian. To connect it with the continuum theory we rotate the evolution time to the imaginary axis, $T\to-i\tau$ (a Wick rotation). The real-time propagator $e^{-iHT}$ then becomes the Euclidean weight $e^{-\tau H}$, the same operator that generates the thermal partition function of a two-dimensional statistical system. Summing this weight over configurations, with the two ends of the evolution held in the state $\ket{\Psi_0}$, expresses the echo as a Euclidean path integral on a slab of width $\tau$ whose two edges are fixed to $\ket{\Psi_0}$. In \ac{CFT} language, a state that can terminate such a path integral is a conformal boundary state, and the admissible ones, the Cardy states~\cite{cardy1989}, are the allowed boundary conditions of the theory. The echo is therefore the partition function of the critical theory on a long strip whose two edges carry the boundary condition set by $\ket{\Psi_0}$ and whose width is the (imaginary) evolution time.

Two features of this construction matter for the numerics that follow. First, a sharp product state has structure down to the lattice spacing and is not, by itself, a smooth conformal boundary state; we therefore pre-cool it in imaginary time for a short interval $\beta_0$, which on the lattice amounts to $N_\beta = 2\beta_0/\delta t$ imaginary-time sites split between the two ends. The strip width becomes $T+2\beta_0$, and the conformal predictions are recovered as $\beta_0$ is made small (or we move to longer final times $T$). Without this UV regulator, short-distance lattice effects contaminate the boundary condition and obscure the universal signal. Second, which boundary condition we obtain depends strongly on $\ket{\Psi_0}$. Each initial state realizes a particular Cardy state, whose lightest boundary operator has a dimension $x_1$ that controls how fast finite-size corrections die away. For the Ising class, the disordered (free) boundary has $x_1=\tfrac12$ and converges quickly, whereas the ordered (fixed) boundary has $x_1=2$ and converges slowly; in the $\sigma^x$-field convention we take for our model, the polarized $\ket{X+}$ state realizes the free boundary, which is why we quench from it.

The strip becomes computationally useful when we view it in the transverse direction. In the Euclidean description, space and imaginary time are coordinates of the same two-dimensional geometry, so either one can be chosen as the direction of propagation. In the transverse picture adopted in this work, we exchange their usual roles and contract the network along the original spatial direction. This defines a transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ that propagates from one spatial column to the next while acting on states supported on a temporal slice. Its tensor-network construction is developed in Section~\ref{sec:transverse}; the rest of this subsection describes the universal information encoded in its spectrum.

The value of this description is that the spectrum of $\mathcal{E}$ is fixed by the continuum theory. Conformal symmetry constrains a two-dimensional critical theory almost completely (Appendix~\ref{app:cft:maps};~\cite{bpz1984, difrancesco1997}), and three of its consequences supply the universal quantities we measure.

The first is the central charge $c$ itself, the single label of the universality class ($c=\tfrac12$ for Ising). It is a conformal anomaly: confining the theory to a strip of transverse size $\beta$ costs a universal ground-state energy proportional to $c$. Quantizing on that strip, the generator of translations along it is~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}
\begin{equation}
    \hat H = \frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(L_0 - \frac{c}{24}\Big),
    \label{eq:cft:casimir}
\end{equation}
where $L_0$ is the scaling generator. Its two terms carry the two kinds of universal data at once: the $-c/24$ term is the Casimir anomaly, so isolating it measures $c$; the $L_0$ term stacks the operator dimensions on top of that offset, as the excited levels.

The second is the operator content. A \ac{CFT} is organized around primary operators, each with a scaling dimension
\begin{equation}
    x = h + \bar h
    \label{eq:cft:dim}
\end{equation}
that fixes the power law of its correlations; the list of dimensions is a rigid fingerprint of the theory. Because our transfer matrix is essentially $\mathcal{E}=e^{-\delta t\,\hat H}$, Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir} hands us its spectrum directly~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E} = \exp\!\Big[-\big(-\kappa + \pi L_0\big)\,g\Big],
    \qquad \kappa = \frac{\pi c}{24},
    \label{eq:cft:transfer}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the pure Casimir number and $g$ is a geometric factor set by the time step $\delta t$, the strip width, the regulator $\beta_0$, and the velocity $v$; its explicit form, after the continuation to real time, is what enters the fitted Equations~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3} and~\eqref{eq:cft:eq4}. The Casimir term $\kappa$ appears in the dominant eigenvalue and carries $c$, while the gaps of the subleading eigenvalues to it encode the dimensions $x$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cft:spectrum}). A single diagonalisation therefore determines both the central charge and the operator content; whether each appears in the modulus or the phase of the eigenvalues depends on the real-time continuation, and is made precise in Section~\ref{sec:cft:universal}.

The third is the scaling of entanglement. When a state is divided at a cut, the correlation between the two sides is measured by the entanglement entropy, or more generally by the R\'enyi entropies $S_n$, a one-parameter family labelled by an index $n$ that includes the von Neumann entropy at $n\to1$. Computing any $S_n$ in a \ac{CFT} maps it onto a correlation function of twist operators---primary fields inserted at the two ends of the cut---whose scaling dimension is~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_n = \frac{c}{12}\Big(n - \frac{1}{n}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:twist}
\end{equation}
Because a primary correlation function decays as a power law set by its dimension, the entropy grows as the logarithm of the subsystem size, with a coefficient proportional to $c$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cft:twist}). This is the mechanism behind every entropy-based estimate of $c$ in this thesis. For a boundary (half-line) cut the coefficient of the logarithm is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{c}{12}\,\frac{n+1}{n},
    \qquad \text{i.e.}\quad \frac{c}{6}\ (n\to1), \qquad \frac{c}{8}\ (n=2),
    \label{eq:cft:coeff}
\end{equation}
half the corresponding bulk value. The equilibrium central charge (Section~\ref{sec:annni}) is read from the ground-state von Neumann entropy ($n\to1$); every temporal entropy in this thesis---at the $p=0$ Ising benchmark and away from it---is the R\'enyi-2 member ($n=2$), computed through the same construction at all $p$. We use it because it avoids the matrix logarithm the von Neumann case requires, is numerically robust, and is the entropy accessible to experiment through the two-replica protocol of Ref.~\cite{boucomas2026measuring}. We return to this choice where the temporal entropy is defined, in Section~\ref{sec:transverse}.

\subsection{Universal versus non-universal constants}
\label{sec:cft:universal}

The results above are exact only in the continuum limit. A numerical simulation, however, is performed on a microscopic lattice and at finite evolution time $T$, bond dimension $\chi$, Trotter step $\delta t$, and a non-zero regulator $\beta_0$. The measured quantities therefore contain both the universal CFT data of interest and non-universal contributions from the lattice and the numerical method. Separating these two parts is essential, because it determines how the predictions below must be compared with the simulations.

The universal information is fixed by the universality class: it includes the central charge $c$, the scaling dimensions $x_i$, the logarithmic coefficients $c/6$ and $c/8$, the Casimir number $\kappa=\pi c/24$, and the asymptotic degeneracy pattern of the leading eigenvalue moduli. The simulation also introduces non-universal quantities, including the regulator $\beta_0$, the entropy offset $s_0$, the Trotter step $\delta t$, the sound velocity $v$, and the overall normalization of the transfer matrix. This normalization is model dependent, so the modulus of the dominant eigenvalue $\mu_0$ is not generally one; in our convention, it is approximately $|\mu_0|\simeq1.5$.

The sound velocity plays a distinct role. Although it is non-universal and varies with $p$ (Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity}), it can be measured independently from the equilibrium dispersion. Since the eigenvalue phases depend on the combination $\pi x/(vT)$, the velocity is needed to convert a measured phase gap into the universal scaling dimension $x$.

We use four complementary strategies to separate the universal information from these non-universal contributions.

First, ratios of eigenvalue gaps remove the factors shared by individual gaps. In particular, the ratio $(x_i-x_0)/(x_j-x_0)$ is independent of $\beta_0$, the transfer-matrix normalization, and the sound velocity. It therefore provides a particularly clean measure of the operator content.

Second, we calibrate the entropy slope against the integrable point. At finite $(T,\chi,\delta t)$, the measured slope differs from the continuum value $c/8$ (the R\'enyi-2 boundary coefficient of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:coeff}) because of finite-time, truncation, and Trotter corrections. We write its leading behaviour as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{slope}(p; T, \chi, \delta t) = \frac{c(p)}{8}\,K(T,\chi,\delta t,\beta_0) + \text{(subleading)},
    \label{eq:cft:calib}
\end{equation}
where $K$ collects the corrections shared by models in the same universality class. We run the integrable point $p=0$, for which $c=\tfrac12$, through the same pipeline at matched $(T,\chi,\delta t,\beta_0)$. Taking the ratio then cancels the common factor,
\begin{equation}
    c(p) = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{slope}(p)}{\mathrm{slope}(0)},
    \label{eq:cft:calibratio}
\end{equation}
and yields $c(p)$. The calibration is reliable when this ratio remains stable across the clean time window, even if the individual slopes drift with $T$ (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The remaining uncertainty comes from coupling-dependent subleading terms, which do not cancel completely.

Third, we extract spectral information primarily from eigenvalue phases. The phases contain the universal level structure, whereas the moduli also depend on the normalization of the transfer matrix. We therefore use phase differences to determine scaling dimensions and use the moduli mainly through ratios or their asymptotic pattern. In particular, the signature of emergent dual unitarity is that the leading moduli approach a common value, not the value itself.

Finally, finite-time corrections can be modelled rather than treated only as numerical error. Their functional form is fixed by the \ac{CFT}~\cite{boucomas2026}, so they can either be cancelled through ratios and calibration or included explicitly in a fit for the absolute value of $c$. The additional branch offset required to expose the smaller Casimir contribution is discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:eq3}.

\subsection{The universal predictions}
\label{sec:cft:temporal}
\label{sec:emergent}

In the transverse description of the strip, the generalized R\'enyi-$n$ entropy across a temporal cut takes the form~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025, boucomas2026}
\begin{equation}
    S_n^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{i\pi c}{24}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)
      + \frac{c}{12}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\,\log\!\Big[\frac{2T}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi t}{T}\Big].
    \label{eq:cft:sgen}
\end{equation}
Because the separation is timelike, the entropy grows logarithmically with the total evolution time $T$, rather than linearly. This scaling is what allows the transverse contraction to remain efficient. Together with the transfer spectrum in Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer}, it leads to four predictions that we test in Section~\ref{sec:results}. We denote the transfer eigenvalues by $\mu_i$, ordered by modulus, and define $\lambda_i=\log(-\mu_i)$, with phase differences obtained from $\arg(-\mu_i)$.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The generalized-entropy profile. Separating Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:sgen} into real and imaginary parts gives
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{Re}\,S_n^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{c}{12}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\log\!\Big[\frac{2T}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi t}{T}\Big],
        \qquad
        \mathrm{Im}\,S_n^{\mathrm{gen}} = \frac{\pi c}{24}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big).
        \label{eq:cft:chord}
    \end{equation}
    The real part follows a symmetric chord profile. When plotted against the variable $\log[\tfrac{2T}{\pi}\sin\tfrac{\pi t}{T}]$, it becomes a straight line whose slope is proportional to $c$, with the boundary coefficient of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:coeff}: $c/6$ for the von Neumann ($n\to1$) entropy of the equilibrium check, and $c/8$ for the R\'enyi-2 ($n=2$) entropy used for all temporal entropies here. We extract $c$ either through the calibration of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:calibratio} or by including the finite-time correction explicitly. The imaginary part is independent of $t$ and forms a plateau at $\tfrac{\pi c}{24}(1+\tfrac{1}{n})$---$\pi c/12$ for the von Neumann entropy and $\pi c/16$ for R\'enyi-2~\cite{boucomas2026}---providing a second estimate of $c$ from the same data.

    \item The central charge from the leading eigenvalue. The imaginary part of the dominant eigenvalue obeys
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0}{T} = a_0 + \frac{C}{T^2} + \frac{a_4}{T^4} + \cdots,
        \qquad C = -\frac{\kappa}{v} = -\frac{\pi c}{24\,v}.
        \label{eq:cft:eq3}
    \end{equation}
    Here $a_0$ is the non-universal extensive contribution, while the coefficient of $1/T^2$ carries the Casimir number divided by the velocity. With $v$ measured independently (Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity}), it yields $c = 24\,v\,|C|/\pi$, a determination of the central charge from the eigenvalue phase alone, independently of the entropy. Because this term is small, the extraction requires a nonzero $\beta_0$, sufficiently long times, and a fixed branch offset, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:eq3}.

    \item The boundary exponent from the first gap. The gap between the leading and first subleading eigenvalue measures the lightest boundary dimension,
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{Im}(\lambda_1-\lambda_0) = -\frac{\pi x_1}{v\,T}.
        \label{eq:cft:eq4}
    \end{equation}
    Once the sound velocity is known, this phase gap determines $x_1$. Recovering $x_1\approx\tfrac12$ confirms that $\ket{X+}$ realizes the free boundary condition. More generally, the subleading eigenvalues reproduce the tower of boundary scaling dimensions through phase gaps of the form $\pi x/(vT)$. In the interacting model, part of this tower is displaced by momentum $\pi$, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

    \item Emergent dual unitarity. Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} also fixes how the spectrum evolves with $T$: the moduli of the leading eigenvalues approach a common value while their phases stay distinct,
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{|\mu_i(T)|}{|\mu_0(T)|}
            = 1 - \frac{a_i}{T} - \frac{b_i}{T^2} - \cdots
            \;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\; 1 .
        \label{eq:gapclose}
    \end{equation}
    Equal moduli with distinct phases indicate that the temporal transfer matrix approaches a unitary operator up to an overall normalization. This emergent dual unitarity follows from conformal invariance and accounts for the logarithmic entropy growth above. The closing of the modulus gaps also makes the leading eigenvectors increasingly difficult to resolve, which ultimately limits the numerical method (Section~\ref{sec:numerics}). We test this behaviour by following the dominant eigenvalue $\mu_0(T)$ in the complex plane, where it approaches a trajectory of nearly constant radius with a winding phase.
\end{enumerate}

Before applying these predictions, Section~\ref{sec:annni} verifies that the ANNNI-type chain is critical, belongs to the Ising universality class, and computes the frustration-dependent model's velocity, $v(p)$.
