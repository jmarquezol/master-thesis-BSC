\section{Universal Data from the Dynamics}
\label{sec:cft}

At a continuous critical point, a lattice model forgets its microscopic details and is described by a \ac{CFT}, characterised by a small set of universal numbers: the central charge $c$ and the scaling dimensions of its operators. Every model in the same universality class reproduces them, which is what makes them good targets for a numerical method. This section relates the Loschmidt echo to a boundary \ac{CFT}, derives the four predictions that Section~\ref{sec:results} tests, and separates them from the non-universal lattice quantities, which we remove or measure independently.

\subsection{From the Loschmidt echo to a boundary CFT}
\label{sec:cft:boundary}

The object we compute is the Loschmidt echo,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(T) = \bra{\Psi_0}\, e^{-iHT} \ket{\Psi_0}.
  \label{eq:echo}
\end{equation}
For an infinite, translation-invariant chain prepared in $\ket{\Psi_0}$, this is the return amplitude after evolving for a time $T$ with the critical Hamiltonian. We take $\ket{\Psi_0}$ to be a product state, whose choice fixes the boundary condition discussed below.

To connect this amplitude with the continuum theory, we rotate the evolution time to the imaginary axis, $T\to-i\tau$ (a Wick rotation~\cite{wick1954}). The real-time propagator $e^{-iHT}$ then becomes the Euclidean weight $e^{-\tau H}$, the same operator that generates the thermal partition function of a two-dimensional statistical system. The matrix element in Eq.~\eqref{eq:echo} can consequently be written as a Euclidean path integral on a strip of width $\tau$, with both edges fixed by $\ket{\Psi_0}$. A state that can terminate a path integral in this way, without introducing any scale of its own, is what a \ac{CFT} calls a conformal boundary state~\cite{cardy1989}. The echo is therefore the partition function of the critical theory on a strip whose width is the (imaginary) evolution time and whose edges carry a conformal boundary condition. The rotation is used only to obtain the conformal predictions, which are derived on the Euclidean strip; they are continued back to real time before being compared with the simulation. Figure~\ref{fig:echo_strip} shows this construction, first as a tensor network and then as the strip it represents.

Two features of this construction are important for the numerics. First, a bare product state carries weight on high-energy configurations and is not itself a conformal boundary state. We therefore cool it in imaginary time for a short interval $\beta_0$, which suppresses that content and brings the state into the regime where the \ac{CFT} applies. On the lattice, the cooling contributes $N_\beta = 2\beta_0/\delta t$ imaginary-time sites split between the two ends, so the strip spans $T+2\beta_0$ in the time direction. Second, the initial state is the boundary condition of the strip: each $\ket{\Psi_0}$ realises a different conformal boundary condition, and that choice selects which operators appear in the transfer-matrix spectrum.

In the Euclidean description, space and imaginary time are coordinates of the same two-dimensional geometry, so either can be chosen as the direction of propagation. In the transverse picture, we exchange their roles and contract the network along the original spatial direction. This defines a transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ that propagates from one spatial column to the next while acting on states supported on a temporal slice. Its tensor-network construction is developed in Section~\ref{sec:transverse}.

In two dimensions, conformal symmetry constrains both the spectrum of $\mathcal{E}$ and the entanglement across a cut in the strip~\cite{bpz1984,difrancesco1997}. These two observables provide independent routes to the universal data; Appendix~\ref{app:cft} summarises the conformal mappings used to derive them.

\subsection{The universal predictions}
\label{sec:cft:temporal}
\label{sec:emergent}

Confining a \ac{CFT} to an infinite strip of transverse size $\beta$ shifts its ground-state energy by an amount set by the central charge~\cite{blote_cardy_nightingale1986}. The excited levels above this shift are organised by the operator scaling dimensions $x_i$~\cite{cardy1984}. The transfer matrix inherits this structure~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E} = e^{-a\,\hat H}= \exp\!\Big[-\big(-\kappa + \pi L_0\big)\,g\Big],
  \qquad \kappa = \frac{\pi c}{24},
  \label{eq:cft:transfer}
\end{equation}
where $L_0$ is the operator whose eigenvalues list the scaling dimensions, $\kappa$ is the Casimir number, and $g=a/\beta$ is the ratio of the column spacing to the strip width, with $a=1$ in lattice units. The width is complex, $\beta=v(2\beta_0+iT)$, because the regulator contributes imaginary time while the quench contributes real time. Substituting this width and expanding for $\beta_0\ll T$ shows that the universal contributions enter the eigenvalue phases at order $1/T$ but affect their moduli only at order $\beta_0/T^2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cft:spectrum}). A single diagonalisation can therefore determine both $c$ and the dimensions $x_i$ from the phases.

The second route uses entanglement. In a \ac{CFT}, the R\'enyi entropies follow from correlators of twist operators whose scaling dimension is proportional to $c$~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}. In the transverse setting, the cut separates earlier from later times rather than dividing space, and its two sides do not belong to a single quantum state. The entropies are instead built from the left and right temporal \acp{MPS} of Section~\ref{sec:transverse}; they are therefore generalised entropies~\cite{doi2023} and are complex in general. For a cut at time $t$~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025,boucomas2026},
\begin{equation}
  S_n^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{i\pi c}{24}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)
  + \frac{c}{12}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\,W(t,T),
  \label{eq:cft:sgen}
\end{equation}
with the chord variable
\begin{equation}
  W(x,\ell) \;=\; \log\!\left[\frac{2\ell}{\pi}\,\sin\frac{\pi x}{\ell}\right],
  \label{eq:chord}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the position of the cut and $\ell$ is the extent of the system. Evaluating $W$ on the interval $(0,T)$ makes the evolution time play the role of the subsystem size in the spatial formula. Because the quench is critical, the entropy grows only as $\log T$, and the bond dimension required for the contraction grows only polynomially with $T$~\cite{schuch2008,paeckel2019}. We use the R\'enyi-2 member throughout because it requires only two copies of the system, which also makes it experimentally accessible~\cite{boucomas2026measuring}. The equilibrium calculations of Section~\ref{sec:annni} instead use the ground-state von Neumann entropy, which \ac{DMRG} provides directly from the entanglement spectrum.

Equations~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} and~\eqref{eq:cft:sgen} now yield four predictions.

\textit{Emergent dual unitarity.} Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} fixes how the spectrum evolves with $T$, and predicts that the moduli of the leading eigenvalues approach a common value while their phases stay distinct (see Appendix~\ref{app:cft:spectrum} for details),
\begin{equation}
  \frac{|\mu_i(T)|}{|\mu_0(T)|}
  = 1 - \frac{b_i}{T^2} - \cdots
  \;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\; 1 ,
  \qquad b_i = \frac{2\pi\beta_0\,(x_i-x_0)}{v}.
  \label{eq:gapclose}
\end{equation}
This indicates that the temporal transfer matrix becomes unitary up to an overall non-universal normalisation~\cite{bertini2019,carignano_tagliacozzo2025}. We show this behaviour through the trajectory of $\mu_0(T)$ in the complex plane, which should trace a circle whose coupling-dependent radius reflects the non-universal normalisation.

\textit{The central charge from the leading eigenvalue.} We work with the logarithms of the transfer-matrix eigenvalues, $\lambda_i = \log(-\mu_i)$, so that $\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_i = \arg(-\mu_i)$ is the phase of $\mu_i$ measured from the negative real axis. The minus sign shifts every phase by the same constant $\pi$; this shift cancels in phase differences and is absorbed by a constant in the fits. The central-charge and boundary-gap relations are expansions in $1/T$ of these logarithms, rather than of the eigenvalues themselves. Substituting the strip width into Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} and expanding gives the following relation~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}, derived in Appendix~\ref{app:cft:spectrum}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0}{T} = A + \frac{B}{T} + \frac{C}{T^2} + \frac{D}{T^4} + \cdots,
  \qquad C = -\frac{\kappa}{v} = -\frac{\pi c}{24\,v}.
  \label{eq:cft:eq3}
\end{equation}
Here $A$ is a non-universal coefficient, $D$ absorbs the subleading finite-time corrections, and $B=-\pi$ is the branch constant introduced by $\lambda_0=\log(-\mu_0)$, while the coefficient of $1/T^2$ carries the Casimir number divided by the velocity. Once $v$ has been measured independently (Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity}), the relation $c = 24\,v\,|C|/\pi$ determines the central charge from the eigenvalue phase alone. The phase is dominated by the non-universal term $A$, which grows linearly with the evolution time, while the universal contribution is suppressed relative to it by two powers of $1/T$. Isolating $C$ therefore requires phases measured over a wide range of evolution times. In practice we fit the unwrapped phase with $B$ pinned to $-\pi$, leaving $A$ and $C$ free.

\textit{The boundary dimensions from the phase gaps.} Each subleading eigenvalue sits at a phase distance from the leading one that measures a boundary dimension,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Im}(\lambda_i-\lambda_0) = \frac{\pi\,(x_i-x_0)}{v\,T}.
  \label{eq:cft:eq4}
\end{equation}
Once the sound velocity is known, the gaps determine the dimensions; the first of them gives the lightest, $x_1$. We refer to this spectrum---the dominant eigenvalue followed by one subleading eigenvalue for each boundary operator, ordered by increasing phase distance---as the conformal tower, and to its members as tower states.

Not every dimension appears. Which ones do is fixed by the pair of boundary conditions at the two ends of the strip~\cite{cardy1986bc}, and for the Ising class there are three conditions: the order parameter may be free to fluctuate or held fixed in either direction. Each pair admits its own set of operators, and each operator brings its own descendants, spaced by integers~\cite{cardy1989,cardy2004bcft}. The quench of this thesis uses the same free state at both ends, for which $x_0=0$ and the spectrum is $\{0,\tfrac12,\tfrac32,2,\dots\}$. The lightest dimension also controls how quickly the finite-time corrections decay, so the free boundary at $x_1=\tfrac12$ converges faster than the fixed one at $x_1=2$. Appendix~\ref{app:x1} sets out the selection rule for all four pairs and measures each of them.

\textit{The generalised entropy profile.} Separating Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:sgen} into real and imaginary parts gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Re}\,S_n^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{c}{12}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\,W(t,T),
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Im}\,S_n^{\mathrm{gen}} = \frac{\pi c}{24}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big).
  \label{eq:cft:chord}
\end{equation}
The real part follows a symmetric chord profile: plotted against $W(t,T)$ it becomes a straight line whose slope is $\tfrac{c}{12}(1+1/n)$, derived in Appendix~\ref{app:cft:maps}. The imaginary part is independent of $t$, so it delivers $c$ from a single plateau value with no profile to fit. Both parts contain $c$, but only the plateau gives a usable estimator on the times we can reach (Appendix~\ref{app:re}). Its finite-time correction decays slowly, as $T^{-1/2}$ for the R\'enyi-2 entropy used here~\cite{boucomas2026}, so the plateau must be extrapolated to long times.

These predictions apply to the continuum theory, whereas a simulation contains additional lattice scales and numerical parameters. The sound velocity $v$, the entropy offset $s_0$ and the overall normalisation of the transfer matrix are fixed by the lattice construction rather than by the universality class. We measure $v$ independently in Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity}, absorb $s_0$ into the entropy fits, and remove the normalisation by working with phase differences, so that only universal quantities are compared in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Section~\ref{sec:transverse} constructs the transfer and transition matrices used to make these measurements.
