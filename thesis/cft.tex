\section{Conformal Field Theory and Universal Dynamics}
\label{sec:cft}

This section collects the conformal-field-theory background that the rest of the thesis relies on. The idea we need is the following: at a continuous critical point, a lattice model forgets its microscopic details and is described by a \ac{CFT}---a continuum theory so constrained by its symmetry that many of its predictions are fixed by a single number, the central charge $c$, together with a discrete list of operators. These predictions are universal: any microscopic model in the same universality class must reproduce them, which makes them ideal targets for a numerical method---they are sharp, parameter-free, and independent of lattice details. Moreover, because in the Euclidean formulation time and space enter on the same footing, the same predictions apply to correlations and entropies read along the time direction, which are precisely the quantities the transverse method computes. Our goal in this section is to make these statements quantitative and to arrive at the concrete predictions that Section~\ref{sec:results} tests.

\subsection{Criticality, universality, and conformal invariance}
\label{sec:cft:criticality}

Away from a phase transition, a spin chain has a finite correlation length $\xi$: spins farther apart than $\xi$ are essentially independent, and microscopic details---the exact couplings, the lattice spacing---still matter. At a continuous critical point, $\xi\to\infty$. There is then no intrinsic length scale between the lattice spacing and the system size, and a system with no scale looks the same at every magnification: it is scale invariant. This is the engine of universality. If we blur the lattice into a continuum, the microscopic details wash out and what survives is a field theory that depends only on the symmetries and dimensionality of the problem. Wildly different microscopic models---the Ising chain, a lattice gas, or the ANNNI-type chain of Section~\ref{sec:annni} at small frustration---can flow to the same continuum theory, and are then said to belong to the same universality class. This is what allows us to replace a messy lattice Hamiltonian by a clean continuum theory when predicting universal quantities, and to compare numbers computed once in that theory directly against our simulations.

The continuum theory at a critical point is not merely scale invariant; it is conformally invariant, and the extra ingredient responsible for this enhancement is locality. By locality we mean that the theory is built from degrees of freedom that interact only over microscopic distances: in the continuum, its action is the integral of a local density, and its response to deformations of space is encoded in a local operator, the stress tensor. For such a theory, consider a transformation that rescales space by a factor that varies smoothly from point to point while preserving angles. In a small neighbourhood of any given point, this transformation is indistinguishable from a global rotation combined with a uniform rescaling---and both of those are already symmetries. Since a local theory is only sensitive to physics neighbourhood by neighbourhood, invariance under global rescalings upgrades to invariance under all such position-dependent, angle-preserving maps. These maps are the conformal transformations, and a scale-invariant local theory is therefore a \ac{CFT}.

In two dimensions this enhancement is dramatic. Writing the two Euclidean coordinates as a single complex variable $z = x + i\tau$, a map preserves angles precisely when its local action is multiplication by a complex number---a rotation and a rescaling combined---which is the defining property of a holomorphic function $f(z)$. Every analytic map is therefore a symmetry, the symmetry algebra becomes infinite-dimensional (the Virasoro algebra), and 2D CFTs are constrained so tightly that they are essentially solvable~\cite{bpz1984, difrancesco1997}. Two practical consequences follow, and we use both. First, quantities factorize into holomorphic ($z$) and antiholomorphic ($\bar z$) pieces. Second, one can change the geometry by changing coordinates: a correlation function known on the plane is known on any shape an analytic map can reach, such as a cylinder or a strip. This mapping technique, developed in Appendix~\ref{app:cft:maps}, is what will take textbook results on the plane to the specific strip geometry of our quench.

One remark deserves emphasis, because the whole method rests on it. In the Euclidean theory the two coordinates $x$ and $\tau$ enter completely symmetrically; nothing intrinsically distinguishes ``space'' from ``imaginary time''. This democracy is what makes the transverse, or temporal, contraction legitimate: we are free to call whichever direction we like ``space'' and quantize along the other, as we do in Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}.

Finally, every 2D \ac{CFT} carries one especially important number, the central charge $c$, which can be read in several equivalent ways. It labels the universality class: the critical Ising chain has $c=\tfrac12$, and---as Section~\ref{sec:annni} verifies in equilibrium---so does the ANNNI-type chain at small frustration, so the central question of this thesis becomes, concretely, whether the model still returns $c=\tfrac12$ when the central charge is measured through time. It is also a conformal anomaly: placing the theory on a finite or curved region costs a universal ground-state (Casimir) energy proportional to $c$, which is the most direct handle we have on it and exactly how it enters the transfer-matrix spectrum below. And it loosely counts the gapless degrees of freedom ($c=\tfrac12$ corresponds to a single Majorana mode), so a larger $c$ means more entanglement and a higher computational cost. Throughout this work, $c$ is the target: nearly every formula that follows is a way of isolating it.

\subsection{Operator content and the transfer-matrix spectrum}
\label{sec:cft:spectrum}
\label{sec:cft:operators}

A \ac{CFT} is organized not around particles but around local primary operators. Each primary carries two conformal weights $(h,\bar h)$ whose sum
\begin{equation}
    x = h + \bar h
    \label{eq:cft:dim}
\end{equation}
is its scaling dimension: scale invariance forces every two-point function to be a pure power law with exponent set by $x$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cft:ops}), so a small $x$ corresponds to a slowly decaying, ``light'' operator and a large $x$ to a fast-decaying, ``heavy'' one. The list of primaries with their dimensions is a fixed fingerprint of the theory. The reason this list matters for a numerical method is that it is directly measurable: as we now show, the scaling dimensions appear as the spectrum of a transfer matrix, so diagonalizing a matrix amounts to reading off the operator content of the \ac{CFT}.

The link is provided by the stress tensor, the operator that generates conformal transformations and whose modes form the Virasoro algebra; its scaling generator $L_0$ has the operator dimensions as its spectrum. Quantizing the theory on a strip of transverse size $\beta$---which, as Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal} shows, is the geometry of our quench---the generator of translations along the strip is, up to the sound velocity $v$, an integral of the stress tensor, and the conformal map from the plane leaves behind one extra, purely geometric term: the Casimir anomaly proportional to $c$~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
    \hat H = \frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(L_0 - \frac{c}{24}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:casimir}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir} does two things for us at once. The $-c/24$ term is a universal ground-state offset fixed only by $c$ and the strip width: isolate it, and the central charge has been measured. The $L_0$ term places the operator dimensions $x$ on top of that offset, as the excited levels. Since the transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ advances the system by one step along the strip, it is essentially $e^{-\delta t\,\hat H}$, and Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir} hands us its entire spectrum: a dominant eigenvalue encoding the Casimir term (hence $c$), and a tower of subleading eigenvalues whose gaps to the dominant one encode the operator dimensions. Written out~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E} = \exp\!\Big[-\big(-\kappa + \pi L_0\big)\cdot(\text{geometry})\Big],
    \qquad \kappa = -\frac{\pi c\,\delta t}{24},
    \label{eq:cft:transfer}
\end{equation}
where the geometric factor collects the strip size and the velocity $v$ (the derivation is given in Appendix~\ref{app:cft:spectrum}). This equation is the origin of the eigenvalue-based measurements of Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}: the central charge from the dominant eigenvalue, and the operator dimensions from the spectral gaps.

\subsection{Entanglement in a CFT}
\label{sec:cft:entropy}

Entanglement provides a second, independent window onto the central charge, because computing it in a \ac{CFT} forces one to evaluate a correlation function of special operators whose dimension is built from $c$. In the replica approach of Calabrese and Cardy~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}, the $n$-th R\'enyi moment $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}$ of a subsystem $\mathcal A$ is computed by inserting a pair of twist operators $\sigma_n$ at its endpoints. The twist operator is itself a primary, with dimension
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_n = \frac{c}{12}\Big(n - \frac{1}{n}\Big),
    \label{eq:cft:twist}
\end{equation}
and its power-law two-point function yields the characteristic logarithmic entanglement entropy of critical systems, with a slope proportional to $c$ (the derivation is given in Appendix~\ref{app:cft:twist}). This is the logic behind every entropy-based measurement of the central charge in this thesis, including the equilibrium fits of Section~\ref{sec:annni}: the entropy is a logarithm whose slope measures $c$. The R\'enyi order $n$ enters through a known prefactor; for a boundary (half-line) cut, which is the situation relevant to both our equilibrium and temporal entropies, the coefficient of the logarithm is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{c}{12}\,\frac{n+1}{n},
    \qquad
    \text{i.e.}\quad \frac{c}{6}\ (n\to1), \qquad \frac{c}{8}\ (n=2).
    \label{eq:cft:coeff}
\end{equation}
We will return to this coefficient in Section~\ref{sec:transverse}, when we define the specific entropy our simulations compute and fix which member of this family applies to it.

\subsection{The quench as a boundary-CFT problem, and the universal predictions}
\label{sec:cft:temporal}
\label{sec:cft:boundary}
\label{sec:emergent}

We can now specialize this machinery to our problem, the Loschmidt echo introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro},
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(T) = \bra{\Psi_0}\, e^{-iHT} \ket{\Psi_0},
    \label{eq:echo}
\end{equation}
the amplitude to return to the initial product state $\ket{\Psi_0}=\ket{X+}^{\otimes N}$ after evolving with the critical Hamiltonian. Written as a Euclidean path integral (through the rotation $t\to-i\tau$), the two ends of the evolution are copies of $\ket{\Psi_0}$. In \ac{CFT} language, a state that can terminate a path integral is a conformal boundary state $\ket b$, and the consistent ones---the Cardy states~\cite{cardy1989}---correspond to the allowed boundary conditions of the theory. The resulting geometric picture is simple: the initial product state acts as a conformally invariant boundary condition, and the echo is the partition function of the \ac{CFT} on an infinitely long strip whose two edges carry $\ket b$, with a width set by the (imaginary) evolution time.

Two ingredients decorate this picture, and both recur throughout the numerics. The first is a UV regulator $\beta_0$: a sharp product state has structure down to the lattice scale, so we pre-evolve it in imaginary time for a short amount $\beta_0$, cooling it into a smooth boundary state. On the lattice this corresponds to $N_\beta = 2\beta_0/\delta t$ imaginary-time sites split between the two ends; the strip width is then $T+2\beta_0$, and the \ac{CFT} predictions are recovered at small $\beta_0$. This is why every production run uses a nonzero $\beta_0$: without it, the conformal boundary condition is dirty and the universal predictions are spoiled. The second ingredient is the choice of boundary condition. Different initial states correspond to different Cardy states, and the lightest boundary operator of each has a boundary scaling dimension $x_1$ that controls how fast finite-size corrections decay. For the Ising \ac{CFT}, the disordered (free) boundary has $x_1=\tfrac12$ and converges fast, whereas the ordered (fixed) boundary has $x_1=2$ and converges slowly. In the $\sigma^x$-field convention of our model, the field-polarized $\ket{X+}$ realizes the free boundary, which is why we quench from it---and why measuring $x_1\approx\tfrac12$ in Section~\ref{sec:results} confirms that we have the boundary physics right.

The step that defines our method is to read this strip sideways. Since the two Euclidean coordinates are interchangeable (Section~\ref{sec:cft:criticality}), instead of treating the strip's long direction as imaginary time and quantizing along space, we do the opposite: we build a transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ that advances along the temporal direction, acting on states that live on a time-like slice---the temporal matrix product states of Section~\ref{sec:transverse}. Because it is the same \ac{CFT} seen along the other axis, all of the machinery above applies with space and time swapped: the eigenvalues of $\mathcal{E}$ are the operator content, and the temporal entropy is again a twist-field correlation function. There is one genuinely new element: the relevant object is not an ordinary density matrix but the reduced transition matrix built from both dominant boundary states (Section~\ref{sec:transverse}), and its entropies are computed from the twist-field correlator at time-like separation. Carrying this out yields the strip generalized entropy~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
    S^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{i\pi c}{12}
      + \frac{c}{6}\,\log\!\Big[\frac{2T}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi t}{T}\Big],
    \label{eq:cft:sgen}
\end{equation}
whose decisive feature is hidden in ``time-like'': because the separation is temporal, the entropy grows only logarithmically in $T$, rather than linearly as the spatial entanglement does after a quench. Logarithmic growth means a polynomially bounded bond dimension, which is the reason the transverse contraction can be efficient at all.

Everything we do numerically in this thesis reduces to testing four universal predictions, all descending from Equations~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} and~\eqref{eq:cft:sgen}~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}. We collect them here, in the order in which Section~\ref{sec:results} confronts them with the simulations.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The generalized-entropy profile. Splitting Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:sgen} into real and imaginary parts,
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{Re}\,S^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{c}{6}\log\!\Big[\frac{2T}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi t}{T}\Big],
        \qquad
        \mathrm{Im}\,S^{\mathrm{gen}} = \frac{\pi c}{12}.
        \label{eq:cft:chord}
    \end{equation}
    The real part is a symmetric ``dome'' peaking at the mid-cut $t=T/2$; plotted against the chord variable $\log[\tfrac{2T}{\pi}\sin\tfrac{\pi t}{T}]$ it becomes a straight line whose slope measures $c$ (with the R\'enyi-order-dependent coefficient of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:coeff}). The imaginary part is a flat plateau at $\pi c/12$---for $c=\tfrac12$, the value $\pi/24\approx0.131$---giving a second, fit-free read of the central charge from the same data.

    \item The central charge from the leading eigenvalue. Writing $\lambda_i=\log(-\tau_i)$ for the transfer eigenvalues $\tau_i$, the imaginary part of the leading one obeys
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0}{T} = a_0 + \frac{\kappa}{T^2} + \frac{a_4}{T^4} + \cdots,
        \label{eq:cft:eq3}
    \end{equation}
    so isolating the universal $1/T^2$ Casimir coefficient $\kappa$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} and converting it gives $c$ directly---a route through the eigenvalues alone, independent of the entropy. It requires a nonzero $\beta_0$ and long times, because the small universal term sits underneath large non-universal contributions; a further subtlety of this fit (a branch constant that must be pinned) is discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:eq3}.

    \item The boundary exponent from the first gap. The gap between the leading and first subleading eigenvalue measures the lightest boundary operator dimension,
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{Im}(\lambda_1-\lambda_0) = -\frac{\pi x_1}{v\,T},
        \label{eq:cft:eq4}
    \end{equation}
    so recovering $x_1\approx\tfrac12$ confirms the free boundary condition---that $\ket{X+}$ is the boundary state we think it is. More generally, every subleading eigenvalue corresponds to a boundary operator, with phase gap $\pi x/(vT)$: the spectrum is a ladder of \ac{CFT} states, and reading it correctly is a central part of Section~\ref{sec:results}.

    \item Emergent dual unitarity. Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} also fixes how the spectrum evolves with the evolution time. As $T$ grows, the moduli of the leading eigenvalues approach a common value while their phases remain distinct,
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{|\mu_i(T)|}{|\mu_0(T)|}
            = 1 - \frac{a_i}{T} - \frac{b_i}{T^2} - \cdots
            \;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\; 1,
        \label{eq:gapclose}
    \end{equation}
    with corrections decaying as integer powers of $1/T$. A band of eigenvalues with equal moduli and distinct phases is the spectral signature of a unitary operator up to an overall scale: the temporal transfer matrix becomes effectively unitary at long times. This emergent dual unitarity is a consequence of conformal invariance at the critical point---not of integrability---and it is the physical hallmark behind the logarithmic entropy growth above. It is also a double-edged prediction: the same degeneracy of moduli that signals the emergent conformal structure makes the leading eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$ progressively harder to resolve, a difficulty that shapes the numerical methods of Section~\ref{sec:numerics} and the reach of the method itself. In practice, we verify it by tracing the leading eigenvalue $\lambda_0(T)$ in the complex plane: a circle of near-constant radius with a winding phase.
\end{enumerate}

These four predictions are the targets. Section~\ref{sec:annni} first establishes that the model we study is critical and Ising-class in equilibrium, so that the predictions apply; the sections that follow build the machinery to measure them through time.
