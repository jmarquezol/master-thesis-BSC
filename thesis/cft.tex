\section{Universal Data from the Dynamics}
\label{sec:cft}

The real-time dynamics of a critical lattice model contains universal information that can be extracted from a simulation. At a continuous critical point, the lattice model forgets its microscopic details and is described by a \ac{CFT}---a continuum theory so constrained by symmetry that its predictions are fixed by a small set of numbers: the central charge $c$ and the spectrum of scaling dimensions of its operators. These quantities are universal: every model in the same universality class reproduces them, which is exactly what makes them good targets for a numerical method. This section relates the Loschmidt echo to a boundary \ac{CFT}, states the resulting universal predictions, and identifies the non-universal lattice parameters needed to test them.

\subsection{From the Loschmidt echo to a boundary CFT}
\label{sec:cft:boundary}

The object we compute is the Loschmidt echo,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(T) = \bra{\Psi_0}\, e^{-iHT} \ket{\Psi_0}.
  \label{eq:echo}
\end{equation}
For an infinite, translation-invariant chain prepared in $\ket{\Psi_0}$, this is the amplitude to return to the initial state after evolving for a time $T$ with the critical Hamiltonian. We take $\ket{\Psi_0}$ to be a product state; its particular choice determines the boundary condition discussed below.

To connect this amplitude with the continuum theory, we rotate the evolution time to the imaginary axis, $T\to-i\tau$ (a Wick rotation~\cite{wick1954}). The real-time propagator $e^{-iHT}$ then becomes the Euclidean weight $e^{-\tau H}$, the same operator that generates the thermal partition function of a two-dimensional statistical system. The matrix element in Eq.~\eqref{eq:echo} can consequently be written as a Euclidean path integral on a slab of width $\tau$, with both edges fixed by $\ket{\Psi_0}$. A state that can terminate a path integral in this way, without introducing any scale of its own, is what a \ac{CFT} calls a conformal boundary state. The echo is therefore the partition function of the critical theory on a long strip whose two edges carry the boundary condition set by $\ket{\Psi_0}$ and whose width is the (imaginary) evolution time. The rotation is used only to obtain the conformal predictions, which are derived on the Euclidean strip; they are continued back to real time before being compared with the simulation. Figure~\ref{fig:echo_strip} shows this construction, first as a tensor network and then as the strip it represents.

Two features of this construction are important for the numerics. First, a bare product state carries weight on high-energy configurations and is not itself a conformal boundary state. We therefore cool it in imaginary time for a short interval $\beta_0$, which suppresses this high-energy content and brings the state into the regime where the \ac{CFT} applies. On the lattice, the cooling contributes $N_\beta = 2\beta_0/\delta t$ imaginary-time sites split between the two ends, so the strip spans $T+2\beta_0$ in the time direction. Second, the initial state is the boundary condition of the strip. Each $\ket{\Psi_0}$ realizes a particular conformal boundary condition, and that choice selects which tower of boundary operators appears in the spectrum~\cite{boucomas2026}. In our convention, we quench from the free boundary, so the Ising class admits the dimensions $\{0,\tfrac12,\tfrac32,2,\dots\}$; a different choice of initial and final states selects a different tower. The lightest dimension also controls how quickly finite-size corrections decay, so the free boundary at $x_1=\tfrac12$ converges quickly and the fixed boundary at $x_1=2$ slowly. Appendix~\ref{app:extraction} measures both conditions at the integrable point.

We contract the strip in the transverse direction. In the Euclidean description, space and imaginary time are coordinates of the same two-dimensional geometry, so either can be chosen as the direction of propagation. In the transverse picture, we exchange their roles and contract the network along the original spatial direction. This defines a transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ that propagates from one spatial column to the next while acting on states supported on a temporal slice. Its tensor-network construction is developed in Section~\ref{sec:transverse}.

In two dimensions, conformal symmetry strongly constrains both the spectrum of $\mathcal{E}$ and the entanglement across a cut in the strip~\cite{bpz1984,difrancesco1997}. These two observables provide independent routes to the universal data; Appendix~\ref{app:cft:maps} summarises the conformal mappings used to derive them.

\subsection{The universal predictions}
\label{sec:cft:temporal}
\label{sec:emergent}

Confining a \ac{CFT} to an infinite strip of transverse size $\beta$ shifts its ground-state energy by an amount set by the central charge. The excited levels above this shift are organised by the operator scaling dimensions $x_i$. The transfer matrix inherits this structure~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E} = e^{-a\,\hat H}= \exp\!\Big[-\big(-\kappa + \pi L_0\big)\,g\Big],
  \qquad \kappa = \frac{\pi c}{24},
  \label{eq:cft:transfer}
\end{equation}
where $L_0$ is the operator whose eigenvalues list the scaling dimensions, $\kappa$ is the Casimir number, and $g=a/\beta$ is the ratio of the column spacing to the strip width, with $a=1$ in lattice units. The width is complex, $\beta=v(2\beta_0+iT)$, because the regulator contributes imaginary time while the quench contributes real time. Substituting this width and expanding for $\beta_0\ll T$ shows that the universal contributions enter the eigenvalue phases at order $1/T$ but affect their moduli only at order $\beta_0/T^2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cft:spectrum}). A single diagonalisation can therefore determine both $c$ and the dimensions $x_i$ from the phases.

The second route uses entanglement. In a \ac{CFT}, the R\'enyi entropies follow from correlators of twist operators whose scaling dimension is proportional to $c$~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}. In the transverse setting, the cut runs across the strip in the time direction, and its two sides do not belong to a single quantum state. The entropies are instead built from the left and right temporal \acp{MPS} of Section~\ref{sec:transverse}; they are therefore generalized entropies~\cite{doi2023} and are generally complex. For a cut at time $t$~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025,boucomas2026},
\begin{equation}
  S_n^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{i\pi c}{24}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)
  + \frac{c}{12}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\,W(t,T),
  \label{eq:cft:sgen}
\end{equation}
with the chord variable
\begin{equation}
  W(x,\ell) \;=\; \log\!\left[\frac{2\ell}{\pi}\,\sin\frac{\pi x}{\ell}\right],
  \label{eq:chord}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the position of the cut and $\ell$ is the extent of the system. Evaluating $W$ on the interval $(0,T)$ makes the evolution time play the role of the subsystem size in the spatial formula. The entropy therefore grows as $\log T$, and the bond dimension required for the contraction grows only polynomially with $T$~\cite{schuch2008,paeckel2019}. We use the R\'enyi-2 member throughout because it is the least expensive to compute and requires only two copies of the system, making it experimentally accessible~\cite{boucomas2026measuring}. The equilibrium calculations of Section~\ref{sec:annni} instead use the ground-state von Neumann entropy, which \ac{DMRG} provides directly from the entanglement spectrum.

Equations~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} and~\eqref{eq:cft:sgen} give four predictions, all tested in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Three concern the transfer eigenvalues $\mu_i$. We work with their logarithms, $\lambda_i = \log(-\mu_i)$, so that $\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_i = \arg(-\mu_i)$ is the phase of $\mu_i$ measured from the negative real axis. The minus sign shifts every phase by the same constant $\pi$; this shift cancels in phase differences and is absorbed by a constant when fitting $\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0$ alone.

\textit{The generalized-entropy profile.} Separating Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:sgen} into real and imaginary parts gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Re}\,S_n^{\mathrm{gen}}(t) = s_0 + \frac{c}{12}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)\,W(t,T),
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Im}\,S_n^{\mathrm{gen}} = \frac{\pi c}{24}\Big(1+\frac{1}{n}\Big).
  \label{eq:cft:chord}
\end{equation}
The real part follows a symmetric chord profile. Plotted against $W(t,T)$, it becomes a straight line whose slope is $\tfrac{c}{12}(1+1/n)$, derived in Appendix~\ref{app:cft:maps}, and therefore determines $c$. The imaginary part is independent of $t$ and gives a second estimate of $c$ without fitting the spatial profile. Finite-time corrections to $S_n^{\mathrm{gen}}$ decay as $T^{-1/n}$~\cite{boucomas2026}; for the R\'enyi-2 entropy used here this is $T^{-1/2}$, so extracting $c$ requires extrapolation to the long-time limit.

\textit{The central charge from the leading eigenvalue.} The central-charge and boundary-gap relations are expansions in $1/T$ of the logarithms $\lambda_i$, rather than of the eigenvalues themselves. Substituting the strip width into Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} and expanding gives the following relation~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}, derived in Appendix~\ref{app:cft:spectrum}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0}{T} = a_0 + \frac{B}{T} + \frac{C}{T^2} + \frac{a_4}{T^4} + \cdots,
  \qquad C = -\frac{\kappa}{v} = -\frac{\pi c}{24\,v}.
  \label{eq:cft:eq3}
\end{equation}
Here $a_0$ and $a_4$ are non-universal coefficients and $B=-\pi$ is the branch constant introduced by $\lambda_0=\log(-\mu_0)$, while the coefficient of $1/T^2$ carries the Casimir number divided by the velocity. Once $v$ has been measured independently (Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity}), the relation $c = 24\,v\,|C|/\pi$ determines the central charge from the eigenvalue phase alone, without relying on the entropy. Because this term is small, the extraction requires a nonzero $\beta_0$ and sufficiently long times. In practice we fit $\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0 = A\,T + B + C/T + D/T^3$ on the unwrapped phase, where $B$ absorbs the branch shift.

\textit{The boundary exponent from the first gap.} The gap between the leading and first subleading eigenvalues measures the lightest boundary dimension,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Im}(\lambda_1-\lambda_0) = \frac{\pi x_1}{v\,T}.
  \label{eq:cft:eq4}
\end{equation}
Once the sound velocity is known, this phase gap determines $x_1$. More generally, each subleading eigenvalue corresponds to a boundary dimension $x_i$ through the same relation. We refer to this spectrum---the dominant eigenvalue followed by one subleading eigenvalue for each boundary operator, ordered by increasing phase distance---as the conformal tower, and to its members as tower states.

\textit{Emergent dual unitarity.} Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} also fixes how the spectrum evolves with $T$. Unlike the two predictions above, this one is an expansion of the eigenvalues themselves rather than of their logarithms: the moduli of the leading eigenvalues approach a common value while their phases stay distinct,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{|\mu_i(T)|}{|\mu_0(T)|}
  = 1 - \frac{a_i}{T} - \frac{b_i}{T^2} - \cdots
  \;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\; 1 .
  \label{eq:gapclose}
\end{equation}
Equal moduli with distinct phases indicate that the temporal transfer matrix approaches a unitary operator up to an overall non-universal normalisation. We test this behaviour by following the dominant eigenvalue $\mu_0(T)$ in the complex plane as it moves along a trajectory of nearly constant radius with a winding phase.

These predictions apply to the continuum theory, whereas a simulation contains additional lattice scales and numerical parameters. The sound velocity $v$, the entropy offset $s_0$ and the overall normalisation of the transfer matrix are fixed by the lattice construction rather than by the universality class. We measure $v$ independently in Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity}, absorb $s_0$ into the entropy fits, and remove the normalisation by working with phase differences, so that only universal quantities are compared in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Section~\ref{sec:transverse} constructs the transfer and transition matrices used to measure them.
