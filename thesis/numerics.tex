\section{The Block Power Method}
\label{sec:numerics}
\label{sec:nonhermitian}

Section~\ref{sec:transverse} reduced the dynamical calculation to finding the leading eigenpairs of the transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$. We obtain them with a block power method that evolves several temporal states simultaneously. The method returns the dominant eigenvalues and their left and right eigenvectors, which we then identify and order for comparison with the \ac{CFT} predictions. Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm} gives the implementation details.

The right and left eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}=\mu_i\ket{R_i},
  \qquad
  \bra{L_i}\mathcal{E}=\mu_i\bra{L_i}.
  \label{eq:nonhermitian_eigenproblem}
\end{equation}
Because $\mathcal{E}$ is non-normal (Section~\ref{sec:mporep:fsm}), its left and right eigenvectors differ, and neither set is orthogonal by itself. Instead, the two sets are biorthogonal, $\braket{L_i}{R_j}\propto\delta_{ij}$. Here the bra notation does not imply complex conjugation: $\bra{L_i}$ is a row vector, and $\braket{L_i}{R_j}$ denotes the direct contraction of the two temporal networks. We therefore propagate the left states with the transpose $\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf T}$, which implements the second equation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:nonhermitian_eigenproblem}.

Different outputs of the calculation require different parts of this eigensystem. The modulus of the dominant eigenvalue $\mu_0$ determines the Loschmidt rate, while its phase encodes the central charge. Phase gaps to the subleading eigenvalues contain the boundary operator content, and the dominant pair $(\bra{L_0},\ket{R_0})$ defines the generalized temporal entropies. We must therefore resolve the leading eigenvalues individually, identify $\mu_0$, and order the remaining eigenvalues for comparison with the conformal tower.

\subsection{The algorithm}
\label{sec:blockpm}

A single-vector power method is sufficient when only the dominant eigenpair is required. It therefore provides the Loschmidt rate, the central charge obtained from the phase of $\mu_0$, and the dominant left and right states used in the entropy calculation. Extracting the boundary operator content also requires the subleading eigenvalues, whose separations decrease as $T$ grows. We resolve these eigenvalues by iterating a block of $k$ states. The block converges to the leading $k$-dimensional invariant subspace at a rate controlled by $|\mu_{k+1}/\mu_k|$; near-degeneracies among eigenvalues within the block therefore do not slow the convergence of the subspace. The projected eigenproblem then resolves the individual eigenpairs within this subspace.

The block method evolves $k$ right states $\ket{R_j}$ and $k$ left states $\bra{L_j}$, each represented as a \ac{tMPS}. We initialise the two blocks independently with random complex tensors. Algorithm~\ref{alg:blockpm} summarises one iteration, and Figure~\ref{fig:blockpm} shows the tensor-network operations used to apply and project the transfer matrix.

(a) Applying $\mathcal{E}$ to one member of the right block, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_apply}, increases the temporal bond dimension from $\chi$ to $\chi D$, where $D$ is the \ac{tMPO} bond dimension. The result is then truncated back to $\chi$. The left block is treated analogously using the transpose. (b) Contractions defining the two $k\times k$ matrices in Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_pencil}. The left chain enters directly as a bra, without complex conjugation.}
  \label{fig:blockpm}
\end{figure}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textbf{Block power method} for the $k$ dominant eigenpairs of $\mathcal{E}$.}
\label{alg:blockpm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\ket{R_j},\ \bra{L_j} \gets$ random complex \acp{tMPS}, \quad $j=1,\dots,k$
\Repeat
  \State $\ket{\tilde R_j} \gets \operatorname{truncate}_\chi\big(\mathcal{E}\ket{R_j}\big)$, \qquad $\bra{\tilde L_j} \gets \operatorname{truncate}_\chi\big(\bra{L_j}\,\mathcal{E}\big)$ \Comment{apply}
  \State $S_{ij} \gets \braket{L_i}{R_j}$, \qquad $M_{ij} \gets \braket{L_i}{\tilde R_j}=\bra{L_i}\mathcal{E}\ket{R_j}$ \Comment{project}
  \State solve $M v=\theta\,S v$ for Ritz values $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k$ and coefficients $V$, $U$
  \State $\ket{R_j} \gets \operatorname{truncate}_\chi\big(\textstyle\sum_i V_{ij}\ket{\tilde R_i}\big)$, \qquad $\bra{L_j} \gets \operatorname{truncate}_\chi\big(\textstyle\sum_i U_{ij}\bra{\tilde L_i}\big)$ \Comment{de-mix}
  \State normalise every block member
\Until{the leading Ritz values stop changing within tolerance}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The first step applies the transfer matrix to each member of the right block and its transpose to each member of the left block,
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\tilde R_j} = \mathcal{E}\ket{R_j},
  \qquad
  \bra{\tilde L_j} = \bra{L_j}\,\mathcal{E}
  \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
  \ket{\tilde L_j} = \mathcal{E}^{\mathsf T}\ket{L_j}.
  \label{eq:blockpm_apply}
\end{equation}
Applying the \ac{tMPO} multiplies the bond dimension of each state by $D$, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:blockpm}a. After each application, we use either scheme from Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation} to truncate the propagated state to the maximum bond dimension $\chi$, and then normalise it. This truncation bounds the cost of subsequent iterations.

The next step projects the eigenproblem onto the current block subspaces. We approximate a right eigenvector by
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\psi} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} v_j \ket{R_j}.
  \label{eq:blockpm_ansatz}
\end{equation}
Inserting this ansatz into $\mathcal{E}\ket{\psi}=\theta\ket{\psi}$ produces a residual. We require its contraction with every member of the left block to vanish, so that $\bra{L_i}(\mathcal{E}-\theta)\ket{\psi}=0$. This condition reduces the full eigenproblem to the two $k\times k$ matrices shown in Figure~\ref{fig:blockpm}b,
\begin{equation}
  S_{ij}=\braket{L_i}{R_j},
  \qquad
  M_{ij}=\bra{L_i}\mathcal{E}\ket{R_j},
  \label{eq:blockpm_pencil}
\end{equation}
and the generalized eigenvalue problem
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j} M_{ij}\,v_j = \theta \sum_{j} S_{ij}\,v_j .
  \label{eq:blockpm_smallproblem}
\end{equation}
The overlap matrix $S$ describes the relative orientation of the left and right subspaces, while $M$ is the transfer matrix projected between them. Solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_smallproblem} yields the Ritz values $\theta_j$, which approximate $\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{k-1}$. It also yields the coefficient matrices $V$ and $U$ used in Algorithm~\ref{alg:blockpm}. The columns of $V$ specify the combinations of propagated right states associated with the approximate right eigenvectors, and $U$ provides the corresponding combinations for the left block. If $S$ is ill-conditioned, the oblique projection amplifies numerical errors. We therefore use the pseudoinverse regularisation described in Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm} when solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_smallproblem}.

We then use the Ritz coefficient matrices to separate the propagated blocks into approximate eigenpairs. Before the next iteration, we truncate the resulting states back to $\chi$ and normalise them. The iteration stops once the leading Ritz values remain stable within the prescribed tolerance.

The tensor-network operations dominate the computational cost, whereas solving the $k\times k$ eigenproblem is negligible. Most of the cost arises from the sums used to separate the propagated block states. Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm:cost} quantifies these contributions and describes the measures used to reduce them.

When only the eigenvalues are required, we retain the blocks as orthonormal bases of the leading invariant subspaces instead of assigning individual members to separate eigenvectors. This representation is better conditioned near degeneracies and does not change the Ritz values, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm:schur}. We use it for the late-time spectral measurements in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

Whenever $\mathcal{E}$ is small enough to construct explicitly, we compare the block method with exact diagonalisation. The leading eigenvalues agree with this benchmark, validating the numerical pipeline (Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm:validation}).

\subsection{Selecting and tracking the physical branch}
\label{sec:reach}

At each evolution time, the block method returns $k$ Ritz values from which we must identify the physical eigenvalue $\mu_0$. At the first time point, we select the value of largest modulus. This criterion is unambiguous at small $T$, where the leading spectral gap is wide. At subsequent times, we again select the value of largest modulus unless the two leading candidates agree within one per cent. In that case, we choose the candidate closest in the complex plane to the previously accepted $\mu_0$. Since the tied candidates have almost equal moduli, this criterion reduces in practice to continuity of the phase. Such near-ties are expected from the conformal spectrum: as $T$ increases, the moduli of the states in the conformal tower approach a common value because of the emergent dual unitarity discussed in Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}. We then order the subleading eigenvalues by their phase distance from $\mu_0$, following the ordering of the conformal tower.

For a sequence of evolution times, the converged block at one time provides the initial state for the next. Consecutive calculations therefore begin from the same leading subspace, which helps the selection remain on a continuous spectral branch as the eigenvalues become closer. If the block contains too few tower states to resolve the required operator content, we increase its size in steps, $k=4\to6\to8$. Each larger calculation is initialised from the converged smaller block, so the additional cost is incurred only when the spectrum requires it. Appendices~\ref{app:selector} and~\ref{app:conditioning} describe the selection safeguards and conditioning diagnostics used throughout this procedure.
