\section{Numerical Methods}
\label{sec:numerics}

Section~\ref{sec:transverse:eigenproblem} reduced the contraction to an eigenproblem for the transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$ and explained why a single-vector iteration cannot solve it in the regime we care about. This section builds the solver: the ordinary power method first, then the block extension that carries the rest of this work, followed by the infrastructure that makes long sweeps reliable and the figures of merit that say where the method stops being trustworthy. The complete derivation of the algorithm is given in Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm}; here we keep to the level of detail needed to understand the results.

\subsection{The power method}
\label{sec:powermethod}

Before describing the iteration, it is worth stating what the spectrum of $\mathcal{E}$ gives us. The dominant eigenvalue $\mu_0$ controls the contraction of a long chain and therefore fixes the Loschmidt rate in the thermodynamic limit,
\begin{equation}
    \ell(T) = -\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log|\mathcal{L}(T)|
            = -\log|\mu_0(T)|.
    \label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
For a finite open chain this relation acquires $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$ boundary corrections, which must be accounted for when comparing $-\log|\mu_0|$ against a finite-size echo divided by $N$. The subleading eigenvalues are suppressed in the contraction by powers of the gap ratio $|\mu_1/\mu_0|<1$, which is simultaneously the quantity that governs how fast the iteration below converges, and the one that emergent dual unitarity drives towards one.

Starting from initial guesses $\bra{L_0}$ and $\ket{R_0}$ for the temporal boundary states, the iteration absorbs one column of the network into each boundary at every step,
\begin{equation}
    \bra{\tilde L_{i+1}} = \bra{L_i}\,\mathcal{E},
    \qquad
    \ket{\tilde R_{i+1}} = \mathcal{E}\,\ket{R_i}.
    \label{eq:pm_iteration}
\end{equation}
Each application multiplies the bond dimension, so the states are truncated after every step in one of the two modes of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}, and renormalized as well, since $\mathcal{E}$ is not unitary. The two boundaries are iterated independently: for a non-Hermitian operator the left and right eigenvectors are distinct objects, and the left one is not obtained by conjugating the right. The iteration stops once the generalized temporal entropy no longer changes between consecutive steps.

One caveat about the truncation deserves attention, because it applies to both schemes. Ideally one would truncate against the full remaining network, but the power method builds that network column by column, so at each step the states are optimized using only the current local environment, with no knowledge of the columns still to be applied. Weight that looks negligible now could in principle matter later. For a homogeneous problem this is harmless: every bulk column is the same $\mathcal{E}$, so any relevant weight discarded at one step is regenerated by the next application, and the iteration still converges to the dominant eigenspace, provided that eigenspace is separated from the rest of the spectrum. It is that proviso, rather than the truncation, that fails here, for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:transverse:eigenproblem}.

\subsection{The block power method}
\label{sec:blockpm}

The algorithm, implemented in the routine \texttt{block\_transfer\_eigs}, maintains $k$ right vectors $\ket{R_1},\dots,\ket{R_k}$ and, independently, $k$ left vectors $\bra{L_1},\dots,\bra{L_k}$, all of them temporal MPS on the same time sites, seeded with random complex tensors. One iteration applies the transfer matrix to every member of the right block, and its bilinear transpose---the adjoint with respect to the overlap of Section~\ref{sec:nonhermitian}, implemented as a prime-level swap of the MPO legs---to every member of the left block; this is what makes the left block converge to the true left eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$, which for a non-normal operator are not those of $\mathcal{E}^\dagger$. As in the single-vector method, each application grows the bond dimension, so every product is truncated back to a cap and renormalized.

After the applications, all the spectral information carried by the blocks is compressed into two small $k\times k$ matrices: the overlap metric $S_{ij}=\braket{L_i}{R_j}$ and the projected transfer matrix $M_{ij}=\bra{L_i}\mathcal{E}\ket{R_j}$, both evaluated with the bilinear overlap. Demanding that the residual of the eigenvalue equation vanishes inside the subspace spanned by the blocks---an oblique (Petrov--Galerkin) projection---turns the intractable eigenproblem of $\mathcal{E}$ into the small generalized eigenproblem $M v=\theta\,S v$, whose solutions $\theta$ (the Ritz values) approximate the leading eigenvalues of $\mathcal{E}$; the step-by-step derivation is given in Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm}. Near the degeneracy the metric $S$ becomes nearly singular, so the pencil is solved through a pseudoinverse-regularized form rather than handed to a generalized eigensolver, and the left mixing coefficients are obtained from the same decomposition---which pairs each left combination with its right partner exactly, with no matching heuristic. The iteration closes by rotating the applied blocks onto these combinations and truncating, in either of the two modes of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation} (RTM, the cheap gauge-invariant default, or RDM, the safe Hermitian fallback). One structural refinement matters near a degeneracy: rotating onto the eigenvector combinations themselves becomes ill-conditioned exactly when eigenvalues cluster, so the block is instead carried on an orthonormalized (Schur-like) basis of the same leading subspace, from which the Ritz values are still read at every step. Convergence is declared when the leading Ritz values stop moving between iterations.

Two properties of this construction deserve to be highlighted. First, it degrades gracefully: when the leading cluster contains $m\le k$ nearly degenerate eigenvalues, the block converges at a rate set by the gap between the whole cluster and the rest of the spectrum, which remains finite. Second, it is validated independently of any power-method comparison: at small bond dimension the transfer matrix can be built densely and diagonalized exactly, and the block method reproduces the exact leading spectrum of every model we tested to between $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-13}$.

\subsection{Practical machinery: sweeps, warm starts, and branch selection}
\label{sec:numerics:practical}

The production calculations of Section~\ref{sec:results} are sweeps: ladders of increasing evolution time $T$, repeated for several frustrations $p$, with each $(p,T)$ point requiring a converged block. These are long calculations---the heavier points run for hours on the BSC cluster---and the solver exposes several options that help its convergence considerably. The ones that matter most in practice are the following.

The first is warm starting. Along a ladder $T_1<T_2<\cdots$, the converged block at one rung is re-indexed onto the longer time-site set of the next and padded with a small random tail, so the iteration only has to relax the newly added sites. This is not only a speed-up: near the degeneracy, a cold random restart has nothing biasing it onto the physical branch, so warm continuity is what keeps the tracker on the correct eigenvector family as the spectrum tightens.

The second is the selection of the physical branch. The block returns $k$ Ritz values, and the analysis must decide, at every rung, which one is the physical $\mu_0$. The rule that proved robust is modulus dominance: the physical branch is the eigenvalue of largest modulus, with continuity to the previous rung used only to break near-exact ties (the top two moduli within one percent). An earlier rule that tracked the previous rung's eigenvalue by proximity in the complex plane turned out to fail systematically at stronger frustration, for a physical reason that Section~\ref{sec:results} makes clear once the structure of the spectrum is in view: the phase of $\mu_0$ winds with $T$, so a nearest-value rule drifts onto a spurious neighbouring branch and, re-anchoring on its own mistake, corrupts the entire ladder that follows. A companion guard flags any rung whose selected $|\mu_0|$ jumps by more than a set fraction from the previous trusted one---the signature of a non-converged point---so that it is excluded from fits rather than silently trusted.

The third is adaptivity in the block size. At stronger frustration the leading $k$-block can fill up with members of a single near-degenerate cluster, leaving the subleading eigenvalue needed by prediction 3 of Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal} outside the captured window. The driver detects this and escalates the block size in steps ($k=4\to6\to8$), warm-seeding each larger solve with the converged smaller block, so the extra cost is paid only where the spectrum demands it.

\subsection{Limitations and figures of merit}
\label{sec:reach}

The block method extends the usable window considerably, but it cannot extend it indefinitely, and the boundary is worth stating precisely because it separates what an algorithm can fix from what the physics forbids. The leading eigenvalues of $\mathcal{E}$ are well-conditioned essentially always: their sensitivity to perturbations involves the biorthogonal pair through $1/|\braket{L_0}{R_0}|$, which the block method keeps under control. The individual eigenvectors, by contrast, have a condition number that scales as the inverse of the spectral gap: as emergent dual unitarity closes the gap, any numerical representation of the dominant eigenvector---and hence any entropy built from it---degrades at a rate fixed by the physics itself, not by the iteration. The method therefore delivers clean eigenvalues (moduli, phases, spectral gaps) arbitrarily deep into the dual-unitary regime, while the entropy profile, which needs the eigenvector, is trustworthy only in the window where the leading pair is still isolated:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\text{spectrum of } \mathcal{E}}_{\text{robust: eigenvalue conditioning}}
  \qquad\text{vs}\qquad
  \underbrace{S^{\mathrm{gen}}_2 \text{ from } (L_0, R_0)}_{\text{bounded by } 1/\text{gap}} .
  \label{eq:reach}
\end{equation}
Where that window ends for each model, and why, is itself one of the results of this work (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

This limitation can be sharpened into a single measurable quantity, which we use as the diagnostic of the breakdown. For a Hermitian operator the left and right eigenvectors coincide and remain orthonormal; a small perturbation only rotates them gently, provided the eigenvalues stay apart. For our non-normal $\mathcal{E}$ the left and right eigenvectors are distinct objects paired by the bilinear form, and they can do something a Hermitian pair cannot: as two eigenvalues approach each other, the corresponding left and right eigenvectors can coalesce, so that the pair becomes self-orthogonal, $\braket{L}{R}\to0$, even though neither vector vanishes. The limiting configuration is an exceptional point, where the matrix is defective and can no longer be diagonalized at all. The order parameter for this approach is the phase rigidity of a biorthogonal pair~\cite{rotter2009},
\begin{equation}
    r_j = \frac{|\braket{L_j}{R_j}|}{\lVert L_j\rVert\,\lVert R_j\rVert},
    \label{eq:rigidity}
\end{equation}
which equals one for a well-conditioned (near-Hermitian) pair and tends to zero as the pair approaches an exceptional point. Its usefulness is that it discriminates two scenarios that are otherwise easy to confuse: an ordinary avoided crossing keeps $r_j\approx1$ and nothing breaks, whereas a collapse $r_j\to0$ signals that the eigenvectors themselves are becoming ill-defined, so that any observable built from an individual eigenvector---the temporal entropy in particular---loses meaning, even while the eigenvalue it belongs to stays accurate. This is the asymmetry of Equation~\eqref{eq:reach} with a number attached to it, and its measurement across the sweep is one of the central diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:results}.
