\section{Numerical Methods}
\label{sec:numerics}
\label{sec:nonhermitian}

The transverse contraction is controlled by the leading spectrum of the transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$, as shown in Eq.~\eqref{eq:contraction}. The numerical problem is therefore to extract its leading spectral subspace while keeping the temporal bond dimension of its \ac{tMPO} representation under control. This becomes more difficult at long times, when the leading eigenvalues approach one another in modulus (Section~\ref{sec:transverse:eigenproblem}). We implement a block power method that evolves several states simultaneously.

The right and left eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}=\mu_i\ket{R_i},
  \qquad
  \bra{L_i}\mathcal{E}=\mu_i\bra{L_i}.
  \label{eq:nonhermitian_eigenproblem}
\end{equation}
Because $\mathcal{E}$ is non-normal (Section~\ref{sec:mporep:fsm}), its left and right eigenvectors are distinct and are not generally orthogonal within either set. We pair them biorthogonally, $\braket{L_i}{R_j}\propto\delta_{ij}$. Throughout this section the brackets denote the bilinear contraction of the tensor network, taken without complex conjugation, rather than a Hermitian inner product. The adjoint associated with this pairing is the transpose $\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf T}$, and every left object below is propagated with it.

We order the generally complex eigenvalues by modulus and denote the dominant one by $\mu_0$. Its modulus determines the Loschmidt rate, its phase encodes the central charge, and the gap to $\mu_1$ gives access to the boundary operator content. The generalized temporal entropies instead depend on the dominant pair $(\bra{L_0},\ket{R_0})$. The calculation must therefore recover both the eigenvalues and the individual left and right eigenvectors within the leading block. Further implementation details are given in Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm}.

\subsection{The block power method}
\label{sec:blockpm}

The block method evolves $k$ right \acp{tMPS}, $\{\ket{R_1},\dots,\ket{R_k}\}$, together with $k$ left \acp{tMPS}, $\{\bra{L_1},\dots,\bra{L_k}\}$. The two blocks are initialized independently with random complex tensors. Algorithm~\ref{alg:blockpm} summarises one iteration, while Figure~\ref{fig:blockpm} shows the tensor-network operations involved in applying and projecting the transfer matrix.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{imgs/blockpm.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{One iteration of the block power method.} Each \ac{tMPS} is a column, and $\mathcal{E}$ is the corresponding column of the space--time network, closed at both ends by the product state (small open circles). (a) Application of $\mathcal{E}$ to one member of the right block, Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_apply}: the temporal bond dimension grows from $\chi$ to $\chi \cdot D$, with $D$ the \ac{tMPO} bond dimension, and is truncated back to $\chi$. The left block is treated analogously with the transpose. (b) The contractions defining the two $k\times k$ matrices of Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_pencil}; the left chain enters directly as a bra, without conjugation.}
  \label{fig:blockpm}
\end{figure}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textbf{Block power method} for the $k$ dominant eigenpairs of $\mathcal{E}$.}
\label{alg:blockpm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $\ket{R_j},\ \bra{L_j} \gets$ random complex \acp{tMPS}, \quad $j=1,\dots,k$
\Repeat
  \State $\ket{\tilde R_j} \gets \operatorname{truncate}_\chi\big(\mathcal{E}\ket{R_j}\big)$, \qquad $\bra{\tilde L_j} \gets \operatorname{truncate}_\chi\big(\bra{L_j}\,\mathcal{E}\big)$ \Comment{apply}
  \State $S_{ij} \gets \braket{L_i}{R_j}$, \qquad $M_{ij} \gets \braket{L_i}{\tilde R_j}=\bra{L_i}\mathcal{E}\ket{R_j}$ \Comment{project}
  \State solve $M v=\theta\,S v$ for Ritz values $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k$ and coefficients $V$, $U$
  \State $\ket{R_j} \gets \operatorname{truncate}_\chi\big(\textstyle\sum_i V_{ij}\ket{\tilde R_i}\big)$, \qquad $\bra{L_j} \gets \operatorname{truncate}_\chi\big(\textstyle\sum_i U_{ij}\bra{\tilde L_i}\big)$ \Comment{de-mix}
  \State normalize every block member
\Until{the leading Ritz values stop changing within tolerance}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The first step applies the transfer matrix to each member of the right block and its transpose to each member of the left block,
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\tilde R_j} = \mathcal{E}\ket{R_j},
  \qquad
  \bra{\tilde L_j} = \bra{L_j}\,\mathcal{E}
  \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
  \ket{\tilde L_j} = \mathcal{E}^{\mathsf T}\ket{L_j}.
  \label{eq:blockpm_apply}
\end{equation}
Applying the \ac{tMPO} multiplies the bond dimension of each state by the \ac{tMPO} bond dimension $D$, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:blockpm}a. We therefore truncate each propagated state back to the maximum bond dimension $\chi$ using either scheme of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}, and then normalize it.

The next step projects the eigenproblem onto the current block subspaces. We approximate a right eigenvector by
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\psi} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} v_j \ket{R_j}.
  \label{eq:blockpm_ansatz}
\end{equation}
This approximation leaves a residual when inserted into $\mathcal{E}\ket{\psi}=\theta\ket{\psi}$. Requiring the residual to vanish against every member of the left block gives $\bra{L_i}(\mathcal{E}-\theta)\ket{\psi}=0$. The full eigenproblem is thereby reduced to the two $k\times k$ matrices shown in Figure~\ref{fig:blockpm}b,
\begin{equation}
  S_{ij}=\braket{L_i}{R_j},
  \qquad
  M_{ij}=\bra{L_i}\mathcal{E}\ket{R_j},
  \label{eq:blockpm_pencil}
\end{equation}
and the generalized eigenvalue problem
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j} M_{ij}\,v_j = \theta \sum_{j} S_{ij}\,v_j .
  \label{eq:blockpm_smallproblem}
\end{equation}
The overlap matrix $S$ measures the relative orientation of the left and right subspaces, while $M$ is the transfer matrix projected between them. The resulting Ritz values approximate $\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{k-1}$, and the coefficient matrices identify the corresponding combinations within each block. If $S$ becomes ill-conditioned, the oblique projection amplifies numerical errors, so we solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_smallproblem} with the pseudoinverse regularization described in Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm}.

The Ritz coefficient matrices are then used to de-mix the propagated blocks, assigning each member to one approximate eigenpair. The resulting states are truncated back to $\chi$ and normalized before the next iteration. We stop when the leading Ritz values remain stable within the prescribed tolerance.

The computational cost is dominated by the tensor-network operations rather than the $k\times k$ eigenproblem. Each iteration requires $2k$ applications of the \ac{tMPO}. De-mixing is usually more expensive still, because it first combines $k$ \acp{tMPS}, which multiplies the bond dimension by $k$, and only then compresses the result. Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm:cost} quantifies this breakdown and the measures that reduce it.

When only the eigenvalues are needed, we do not assign individual block members to separate eigenvectors at all. The blocks are kept in an orthonormal Schur basis of the leading invariant subspace, updated by a QR decomposition of the coefficient matrices (Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm:schur}). No accuracy is lost, because the Ritz values do not depend on the basis chosen inside the blocks. Recombining the blocks by any invertible $B$ and $C$ sends $S\to B^{\mathsf T}SC$ and $M\to B^{\mathsf T}MC$, which leaves the solutions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_smallproblem} unchanged. The two modes read the same spectrum from the same pair of subspaces, and differ only in how well conditioned the basis is when it is truncated. That favours the orthonormal one, since the eigenvector coefficients become ill-conditioned where the block is near-degenerate. We use this mode for the late-time spectral measurements in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

It is worth mentioning that the truncation at each iteration uses only the current states, not the columns of the network still to be absorbed, so a component discarded now could in principle matter later. Translation invariance keeps this harmless: every subsequent column applies the same operator $\mathcal{E}$, which regenerates any part of the leading invariant subspace that has been trimmed away.

Finally, we validate the implementation at bond dimensions for which $\mathcal{E}$ can be constructed explicitly and diagonalized exactly. Across the models tested, the block method reproduces the leading dense spectrum with errors between $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-13}$. The corresponding tests are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm:validation}.

\subsection{Selection of the physical branch}
\label{sec:numerics:practical}

At each evolution time the block returns $k$ Ritz values, and the physical $\mu_0$ has to be identified among them. Its phase winds with $T$ at a rate set by the sound velocity. At $p\neq0$ the block also holds partner eigenvalues of similar modulus but widely separated phase, as we will see in the results (Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}). Selecting the value nearest the previous one in the complex plane could therefore drift off the winding branch onto a more stationary partner. Each rung is anchored on the one before it, so a single wrong choice propagates down the rest of the sweep.

We select the eigenvalue of largest modulus. Continuity with the preceding point is used only when the two largest moduli differ by less than one per cent. We also flag a point if the selected $|\mu_0|$ changes by more than a prescribed fraction relative to the previous trusted value, since such a jump indicates unreliable convergence or branch assignment. Flagged points are excluded from the fits.

Modulus dominance holds only while the two moduli can be told apart. Deep in the dual-unitary regime the physical branch and its displaced partner approach a common modulus, and once their splitting falls below the accuracy of the iteration the ordering between them loses meaning---the partner can even overtake the physical branch. The phase separates them where the modulus cannot. The physical branch winds at a nearly constant rate, so its phase at the next rung can be predicted from the rungs already accepted, and the block member closest to that prediction is selected whatever its modulus rank. A rung whose block holds no member near the predicted phase is discarded. Appendix~\ref{app:selector} gives the implementation.

The flagged rungs are not discarded outright, and their recovery is what makes the later sweeps usable. A rung can fail to converge and still carry the physical branch: the spurious value typically sits far above the physical band, while the true $\mu_0$ survives further down the block. A post-processing selector therefore restricts the candidates to a window of five per cent around the last trusted modulus and applies dominance inside that window. A rung with no candidate in the window is dropped, and the reference is chained from the last accepted point. This repairs affected rungs from the stored spectra alone, with no recomputation; on the one rung where an independently converged run exists for comparison, the repaired eigenvalue agrees with it to $3\times10^{-5}$ relative (Appendix~\ref{app:selector}).

\subsection{Convergence and conditioning}
\label{sec:reach}

The numerical diagnostics must distinguish failure of the iteration from the intrinsic sensitivity of a non-normal eigenproblem. We use the following quantities to determine the validity of our numerical results for the measurements we aim to obtain in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

The first diagnostic is the spectral ratio $|\mu_1/\mu_0|$, whose powers control the suppression of the first subleading contribution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:contraction}. It is not a convergence criterion for the block, which is governed instead by the separation of the whole leading subspace from the rest of the spectrum. The two differ near a degeneracy inside the block, where $|\mu_1/\mu_0|$ may approach one while the subspace stays well separated and its eigenvalues keep converging.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors respond differently as this internal gap closes. To first order, the change in an eigenvalue under a perturbation $\delta\mathcal{E}$ is
\begin{equation}
  \delta\mu_i=
  \frac{\bra{L_i}\delta\mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}}
       {\braket{L_i}{R_i}},
  \label{eq:eigenvalue_perturbation}
\end{equation}
which contains no spectral gap: an eigenvalue remains accurate under truncation as long as its biorthogonal overlap stays finite. The first-order change of an eigenvector instead contains the remaining eigenvectors with coefficients proportional to $1/(\mu_i-\mu_j)$, so it degrades as neighbouring eigenvalues approach and the de-mixing that separates the block members becomes correspondingly harder.

The denominator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:eigenvalue_perturbation} is the rigidity of the pair, so the same expression also bounds how far this protection extends: near an exceptional point the overlap tends to zero, and an eigenvalue tied to an individual eigenvector loses accuracy with it. Close to a defective point the individual eigenvalues are in fact fragile objects, splitting under a perturbation of size $\varepsilon$ as $\varepsilon^{1/n}$ rather than $\varepsilon$, where $n$ is the size of the coalescing cluster~\cite{moro1997}. What stays well conditioned is the leading subspace as a whole, whose separation from the rest of the spectrum is what governs the block iteration (Section~\ref{sec:blockpm}). Our eigenvalue-only mode reads the spectrum at that level: it never assigns block members to separate eigenvectors, but carries an orthonormal basis of the subspace and takes the Ritz values from the projected pencil. The ordering is confirmed in our own data, where the leading eigenvalues stay smooth and mutually consistent far past the point at which the entropy built from the individual pair fails (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The eigenvalues behave better than the eigenvectors not because they are immune, but because the subspace-level read avoids the ill-conditioned de-mixing that the eigenvector route cannot do without.

The most severe loss of conditioning occurs near an exceptional point. In a non-normal matrix, eigenvectors associated with approaching eigenvalues can coalesce; their bilinear overlap then tends to zero even though neither vector vanishes, and the matrix becomes defective at the exceptional point~\cite{rotter2009}. We monitor this behaviour through the phase rigidity of each biorthogonal pair,
\begin{equation}
    r_j = \frac{|\braket{L_j}{R_j}|}{\lVert L_j\rVert\,\lVert R_j\rVert},
    \label{eq:rigidity}
\end{equation}
which is close to one for a well-conditioned, near-Hermitian pair and tends to zero as the eigenvectors coalesce. Two eigenvalues can approach one another with their eigenvectors staying independent, or by coalescing. The eigenvalues alone do not distinguish these two cases, whereas $r_j$ does. Appendix~\ref{app:wall} treats the distinction in more detail.

Finally, we test whether an observed limitation is physical or resolution dependent by repeating representative calculations at half the bond dimension, with a tighter truncation cutoff, and with \ac{RDM} rather than \ac{RTM} truncation. We attribute a feature to the transfer matrix only if it persists under all three changes. The fits in Section~\ref{sec:results} also include the expected finite-simulation corrections: the $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$ boundary correction to the rate in Eq.~\eqref{eq:rate} and the finite-time corrections controlled by $\beta_0/T$ (Section~\ref{sec:cft:boundary}). Together, these checks determine the time interval over which each route to the conformal observables remains reliable.
