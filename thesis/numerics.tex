\section{Extracting the transfer-matrix spectrum}
\label{sec:numerics}
\label{sec:nonhermitian}

Section~\ref{sec:transverse} reduced the dynamical calculation to the problem of determining the spectrum of the transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$. We use two iterative methods, chosen according to the spectral information required by each observable and their computational cost.

The right and left eigenvectors of $\mathcal{E}$, written $\ket{r_i}$ and $\bra{\ell_i}$ as in Section~\ref{sec:transverse}, satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}\ket{r_i}=\mu_i\ket{r_i},
  \qquad
  \bra{\ell_i}\mathcal{E}=\mu_i\bra{\ell_i}.
  \label{eq:nonhermitian_eigenproblem}
\end{equation}
Since $\mathcal{E}$ is non-normal (Section~\ref{sec:mporep:fsm}), its right and left eigenvectors differ and must be computed separately. They form a biorthogonal set, such that $\braket{\ell_i}{r_j}\propto\delta_{ij}$. Throughout, the bra notation does not imply complex conjugation: $\bra{\ell_i}$ denotes a row vector, and $\braket{\ell_i}{r_j}$ is the direct contraction of the two temporal networks. We implement the second equation by propagating the left states with $\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf T}$.

\subsection{The single-vector iteration}
\label{sec:svpm}

The first option we use is the power method implemented in \texttt{ITransverse.jl}~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}. Starting from a random complex \ac{tMPS}, we repeatedly apply the transfer matrix and normalise after each step. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:apply}, every application multiplies the temporal bond dimension by the \ac{tMPO} bond dimension $D$, so we use either truncation scheme of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation} to cap the bond dimension to $\chi$, keeping the cost per iteration fixed. The reason why we use random initial states is because a structured product state may have little overlap with the dominant eigenvector and may therefore converge to a subdominant fixed point.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{imgs/apply.pdf}%
  \caption{\textbf{One application of the transfer matrix to a temporal state.} The \ac{tMPS} forms a boundary column, while $\mathcal{E}$ is the corresponding column of the space--time network. Applying it raises the temporal bond dimension from $\chi$ to $\chi D$, where $D$ is the \ac{tMPO} bond dimension, and the state is then truncated back to $\chi$. The left state is treated in the same way using the transpose.}
  \label{fig:apply}
\end{figure}

The iteration converges to the dominant right and left fixed points, from which we obtain the leading eigenvalue as
\begin{equation}
  \mu_0=\frac{\bra{L}\mathcal{E}\ket{R}}{\braket{L}{R}} .
  \label{eq:svpm_mu0}
\end{equation}
Therefore, we use this single-vector method in Section~\ref{sec:results} for computing the leading eigenvalue and the associated dominant pair ($\bra{L}$ and $\ket{R}$). Obtaining the boundary dimensions $x_i$, on the other hand, requires access to the subleading eigenvalues of the spectrum to compute the gaps between them.

\subsection{The block iteration}
\label{sec:blockpm}

To resolve the subleading eigenvalues, we propagate blocks of $k$ right and left states, denoted by $\ket{R_j}$ and $\bra{L_j}$. Repeated application of $\mathcal{E}$ drives each block towards the subspace spanned by the $k$ leading eigenvectors, although its individual states do not generally converge to particular eigenvectors and must be separated afterwards. The convergence rate is controlled by the spectral separation between the retained subspace and the discarded eigenvalues, rather than by the gaps within the retained spectrum. The block can therefore converge even as the leading eigenvalues approach the common-modulus structure described in Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}.

The individual eigenvalues are then separated by projecting the eigenproblem onto the converged subspace. We approximate a right eigenvector as a linear combination of the current block states,
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\psi} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} v_j \ket{R_j}.
  \label{eq:blockpm_ansatz}
\end{equation}
Inserting this approximation into $\mathcal{E}\ket{\psi}=\theta\ket{\psi}$ produces a residual which we require to vanish when contracted with every member of the left block, $\bra{L_i}(\mathcal{E}-\theta)\ket{\psi}=0$. This two-sided projection is appropriate for a non-normal operator~\cite{saad1992} and reduces the full eigenproblem to two $k\times k$ matrices,
\begin{equation}
  S_{ij}=\braket{L_i}{R_j},
  \qquad
  M_{ij}=\bra{L_i}\mathcal{E}\ket{R_j},
  \label{eq:blockpm_pencil}
\end{equation}
and to the generalised eigenvalue problem
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j} M_{ij}\,v_j = \theta \sum_{j} S_{ij}\,v_j .
  \label{eq:blockpm_smallproblem}
\end{equation}
The overlap matrix $S$ describes the relative orientation of the left and right subspaces, while $M$ is the transfer matrix projected between them. Solving this reduced problem yields approximations $\theta_j$ to $\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{k-1}$ and hence the phase gaps that encode the boundary operator content.

The block iteration can also reconstruct the individual eigenvectors, but this step is computationally expensive. Each eigenvector is formed as a linear combination of the propagated block states, which increases the temporal bond dimension and requires a further truncation to $\chi$. This repeated enlargement and truncation dominates the cost of the eigenvector mode. The single-vector method avoids this operation and provides the dominant eigenvectors at a lower cost, so we use it for determining the dominant pair and the block method only for the subleading eigenvalues. Appendix~\ref{app:blockpm} describes the implementation details of this block power method.

\subsection{Selecting and tracking the eigenvalue}
\label{sec:reach}

The block iteration returns $k$ unlabelled Ritz values. At each time, we must identify $\mu_0$ and assign the remaining values to the conformal tower. Selecting the value of largest modulus is unreliable because, for a non-normal operator, an unconverged subspace can produce spurious Ritz values outside the spectrum, and their number may increase with the block size~\cite{saad1992}. We instead identify the physical branch through continuity in time. We retain candidates whose modulus lies within a few per cent of the previously accepted $\mu_0$ and select the nearest of these in the complex plane. If no candidate satisfies this criterion, we discard the time point. The remaining values are ordered by increasing phase distance from $\mu_0$, as predicted for the conformal tower. We compute each ladder at increasing times and initialise every block with the converged result from the preceding time. Appendix~\ref{app:selector} specifies the thresholds and reports the cases in which reselection changed the result.

The single-vector iteration returns one eigenvalue per run, so its reliability is assessed across independent seeds rather than among several Ritz values. At short times, all seeds converge to the same left and right fixed points. At longer times, their overlap $\braket{L}{R}$ can decrease, making the pair ill-conditioned and allowing different runs to converge to different fixed points. Appendix~\ref{app:conditioning} examines the onset of this behaviour. We accept an entropy time point only when two independent runs agree and the resulting profile varies smoothly between neighbouring times. To continue a leading-eigenvalue ladder, we apply the continuity criterion across seeds and retain the run whose phase follows the progression established at earlier times. We include the new point only if it does not increase the residual of the fit to Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3}. Appendix~\ref{app:errors} describes how the fitted ladders are assembled, while Appendix~\ref{app:failures} reports the failure statistics of both iterations.
