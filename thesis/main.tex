\begin{abstract}
The universal properties of a critical many-body system---its central charge and operator content---can be extracted directly from its dynamics, without ever preparing a low-energy state. At a conformally invariant critical point the Loschmidt echo of a quench from a simple product state maps onto the partition function of a boundary conformal field theory on a strip, whose universal data can be read from a transfer matrix built by contracting the space-time tensor network along the spatial direction. This transverse contraction treats time as a spatial dimension, and its cost is set by a temporal entanglement that grows only logarithmically at criticality, sidestepping the entanglement barrier that limits conventional methods. Established so far for integrable chains, we ask whether this route to universal data survives the loss of integrability. We study an ANNNI-type model---the transverse-field Ising chain extended by next-nearest-neighbour interactions of strength $p$---which stays critical and in the Ising universality class while becoming non-integrable and longer-ranged. Encoding its evolution as a translation-invariant finite-state-machine MPO and contracting it with a left--right block power method adapted to the non-Hermitian transfer matrix, we recover the conformal signatures at $p=0$ and find that they persist for $p\neq0$: the temporal central charge stays consistent with the Ising value $c=\tfrac12$, and the boundary operator content is reproduced, including an odd-parity tower displaced by momentum $\pi$ that the interaction generates. Throughout, we separate carefully the universal quantities from the non-universal lattice constants that accompany them, and we map the limits of the approach. \textit{This is a working draft.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro}

How a closed quantum system evolves after being driven far from equilibrium is one of the central questions of modern many-body physics. Simulating such dynamics is difficult, but it can also be revealing: at criticality, the real-time evolution of a simple initial state can expose universal information normally associated with low-energy physics. Our aim with this thesis is to determine how far this dynamical route can be pushed once the system is no longer exactly solvable.

This question is particularly relevant for non-integrable systems, which lack exact analytical solutions. Their dynamics is also intrinsically harder to simulate over long times: after a quench these systems are generically expected to thermalize, acting as their own heat bath so that local observables relax to values fixed only by the energy---the content of the \ac{ETH}---whereas integrable models, protected by extensively many conserved charges, retain memory of their initial state instead. Analytical results are largely confined to those integrable limits, so almost everything we know about thermalization, scrambling and the onset of chaos in non-integrable models rests on being able to follow their dynamics numerically.

Exact numerical simulations are severely limited by the exponential scaling of the Hilbert space, which makes representing a generic many-body wavefunction intractable. To circumvent this bottleneck, the community has turned to \acp{TN}, a framework that efficiently compresses quantum states by using entanglement as the organizing principle. For one-dimensional systems in equilibrium, \acp{MPS} combined with algorithms such as the \ac{DMRG} have become the state-of-the-art approach~\cite{schollwock2011}. Their efficiency comes from the area law of entanglement: the entanglement entropy across a bipartition of a gapped 1D system saturates to a constant rather than growing with subsystem size~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}. Since the required MPS bond dimension is controlled by this entanglement, the ansatz efficiently targets the low-entanglement corner of Hilbert space where the relevant equilibrium states lie.

Simulating dynamics, however, runs into the so-called entanglement barrier. After a quantum quench, the entanglement entropy typically grows linearly in time, approaching volume-law behaviour. Although highly optimized algorithms such as \ac{TEBD} and the \ac{TDVP} are very effective at short times, faithfully representing this growth requires a bond dimension that increases exponentially with time, making standard MPS simulations exponentially costly at late times.

To overcome this barrier, transverse contraction adopts a different perspective. Rather than evolving a state forward in the Schr\"odinger picture, it represents the full dynamics as a two-dimensional tensor network and contracts it along the original spatial direction, treating time as a second spatial dimension. The computational cost is then controlled by the temporal entanglement entropy, defined through a Schmidt decomposition across a cut in time rather than in space. Although its physical interpretation remains an open question, it is computationally well defined. For a generic quench it may grow as strongly as spatial entanglement; nevertheless, the transverse viewpoint can provide a useful way to tackle specific dynamical quantities, such as the Loschmidt echo studied here, as we will see.

The central quantity we compute throughout is the Loschmidt echo, the amplitude for a state prepared in product form to return to itself after evolving for a time $T$. Viewed as a function of complex time, it is the dynamical analogue of a partition function: the evolution operator acts as a Boltzmann weight, the logarithm of the echo per site defines a dynamical free-energy density, and its zeros are the dynamical counterparts of the Fisher zeros of equilibrium statistical mechanics. The echo therefore probes how quickly the evolved state departs from, and how coherently it returns towards, its initial condition, providing a natural measure of relaxation after a quench. It is also an ideal target for the transverse method: as a scalar amplitude with no operator insertions, its tensor network is the bare space-time grid, so its contraction isolates the cost of the method itself.

At a conformally invariant critical point, the echo provides direct access to universal data such as the central charge of the underlying \ac{CFT}. In the transverse picture, it is governed by a spatial transfer matrix whose spectrum is predicted by the CFT and which becomes effectively unitary at long times---a phenomenon known as emergent dual unitarity. As a consequence, the generalized temporal entropies grow only logarithmically, allowing the corresponding quenches to be simulated with polynomial resources. This behaviour was demonstrated explicitly for the critical Ising and three-state Potts chains in Ref.~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}, while Ref.~\cite{boucomas2026} sharpened the connection between the echo and the full conformal data, including the finite-time corrections used later in this thesis. Crucially, these are critical-point statements: the argument requires conformal invariance, not exact solvability.

The central question is therefore whether these conformal signatures survive the loss of integrability. To address it, we study an ANNNI-type model: a self-dual quantum spin chain that extends the transverse-field Ising model with \ac{NNN} interactions controlled by a coupling $p$~\cite{alcaraz2016}. The model provides a deliberate stress test: for any $p>0$ it is non-integrable and longer-ranged, making its dynamics costly for conventional methods, yet it remains critical and in the Ising universality class over a wide range of $p$, so the CFT predictions retain a clear target. We prepare the system in the fully polarized paramagnet $\ket{\Psi_0}=\ket{X+}^{\otimes N}$ (its ground state in the $\lambda\to\infty$ limit), quench it to the critical point $\lambda=1$, and use transverse contraction to compute the Loschmidt echo and the generalized temporal entropies, which we compare with the Ising CFT predictions.


The rest of this thesis is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:cft} defines the Loschmidt echo and develops the CFT framework that relates the transfer-matrix spectrum and temporal entropy to the central charge and operator content, while separating universal predictions from lattice-dependent constants. Section~\ref{sec:annni} introduces the ANNNI-type model, establishes its critical Ising universality, and examines the breaking of integrability and the frustration-dependent sound velocity. Sections~\ref{sec:transverse} and~\ref{sec:mporep} develop the transverse-contraction framework and temporal entropies, followed by the finite-state-machine construction of the time-evolution MPO and its non-Hermitian transfer matrix. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} presents the block power method and the diagnostics of its limitations, while Section~\ref{sec:results} validates the Ising predictions at the integrable point, tests their survival under frustration, and maps the method's range of applicability. Supporting technical derivations are collected in the appendices.

\section{Conclusions and Outlook}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\textit{This section is a provisional summary: the numerical campaign it draws on is complete for $p=0$ and $p=0.1$, while the sweep to stronger frustration is still in progress, and its conclusions should be read as current rather than final.}

The question that motivated this work was whether the universal, conformal signatures that make transverse contraction efficient at a critical point---emergent dual unitarity and the logarithmic growth of the generalized temporal entropies---survive when the model is no longer integrable. Our answer, for next-nearest-neighbour frustration up to $p=0.1$, is that they do. We established this in three steps. At the integrable point $p=0$ the transverse pipeline reproduces the known Ising conformal predictions along four independent axes---the R\'enyi-2 entropy chord, its imaginary plateau, the free-boundary exponent $x_1=\tfrac12$, and the $\mu_0$ circle with its eigenvalue extraction of the central charge---which calibrates the method and fixes our conventions. Switching on frustration, the temporal central charge at $p=0.1$ comes out $c=0.47\pm0.05$ from the entropy slope and $0.50$--$0.53$ from the independent eigenvalue route, both consistent with the Ising value $c=\tfrac12$, and the boundary content is unchanged: once the odd-parity boundary tower---which the next-nearest-neighbour interaction displaces in phase by momentum $\pi$---is read on the correct side of the spectrum, the free-boundary exponent $x_1=\tfrac12$ is recovered at $p=0.1$ as well. Integrability, in other words, is not what the conformal structure of these quenches requires; criticality is.

Beyond this physics result, the work makes a few methodological contributions. The finite-state-machine construction of the time-evolution MPO, combined with the second-order Van~Damme exponentiation, gives a genuinely translation-invariant, second-order propagator for a next-nearest-neighbour Hamiltonian---the ingredient that lets the transverse method reach the thermodynamic limit for a model outside the usual nearest-neighbour setting. To resolve the non-Hermitian transfer matrix through the gap closing we developed a left--right block power method with exact biorthogonal pairing and a Schur-stable subspace basis, which extends the usable window well past the single-vector iteration. Reading the transfer spectrum correctly was itself part of the work: the near-$\pm$ pairing of the leading eigenvalues that had looked like a nuisance turned out to be the odd-parity boundary tower displaced by momentum $\pi$, and recognizing it fixed both the boundary-exponent extraction and the modulus-dominant branch-selection rule on which the stronger-frustration data depend. And we mapped, quantitatively, where and why the method ultimately fails: the entanglement barrier is a near-exceptional point of the transfer matrix, diagnosed by the geometric collapse of the phase rigidity, at which the eigenvalues remain well-conditioned but the individual eigenvectors---and therefore the temporal entropy---do not. We further verified that this barrier is set by the physics rather than by our numerical resolution: neither the bond dimension, nor the truncation cutoff, nor the choice between the two truncation schemes moves it. Characterizing that limit, rather than merely hitting it, is itself part of the contribution.

Several threads remain open, and are the natural continuation of this project. The most immediate is completing the sweep to stronger frustration, spanning $p=0.3$ through $p=1.5$ and extended to longer times: the low-frustration arms already confirm that temporal Ising universality persists with a monotonically shrinking reach, and finishing the higher-$p$ arms---which require the automatic block-size escalation and the corrected branch selector developed here---will turn the picture into a full $c(p)$ curve and pin down the strong-frustration edge, where an exact top-spectrum degeneracy finally stops the method. The resolution of the momentum-$\pi$ displaced boundary tower leaves one theory question behind it: a first-principles selection of which sector carries the $\pm i$ half-step index that quantizes the displacement at exactly $\pi$. Smaller open questions include a sub-leading boundary state at $p\gtrsim0.15$ that matches no Ising boundary dimension, and a systematic study of the Trotter-step dependence of the wall. Looking further out, the same framework applies unchanged to models whose quench carries an exact symmetry degeneracy or a larger central charge---for instance the XXZ chain quenched from a N\'eel state, or the tricritical Ising point---where preliminary work indicates that the barrier arrives markedly earlier, for reasons tied to the symmetry content of the quench and to the value of $c$; a systematic account of how these dials set the barrier's position is a promising direction. Finally, the finite-$\ell$ boundary-weighted construction that survives the wall as a diagnostic hints that the physically meaningful object past the degeneracy may be a genuinely finite-width temporal entropy rather than its thermodynamic limit---a reframing worth pursuing in its own right.
