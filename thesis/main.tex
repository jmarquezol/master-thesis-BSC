\begin{abstract}
The universal properties of a critical many-body system---its central charge and operator content---can be extracted directly from its dynamics, without ever preparing a low-energy state. At a conformally invariant critical point the Loschmidt echo of a quench from a simple product state maps onto the partition function of a boundary conformal field theory on a strip, whose universal data can be read from a transfer matrix built by contracting the space-time tensor network along the spatial direction. This transverse contraction treats time as a spatial dimension, and its cost is set by a temporal entanglement that grows only logarithmically at criticality, sidestepping the entanglement barrier that limits conventional methods. Established so far for integrable chains, we ask whether this route to universal data survives the loss of integrability. We study an ANNNI-type model---the transverse-field Ising chain extended by next-nearest-neighbour interactions of strength $p$---which stays critical and in the Ising universality class while becoming non-integrable and longer-ranged. Encoding its evolution as a translation-invariant finite-state-machine MPO and contracting it with a left--right block power method adapted to the non-Hermitian transfer matrix, we recover the conformal signatures at $p=0$ and find that they persist for $p\neq0$: the temporal central charge stays consistent with the Ising value $c=\tfrac12$, and the boundary operator content is reproduced, including an odd-parity tower displaced by momentum $\pi$ that the interaction generates. Throughout, we separate carefully the universal quantities from the non-universal lattice constants that accompany them, and we map the limits of the approach. \textit{This is a working draft.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro}

The study of out-of-equilibrium dynamics in many-body quantum systems remains one of the most active areas of research in quantum physics, holding the key to understanding and characterizing fundamental phenomena arising from these complex systems.

A fundamental question in contemporary physics is how a closed quantum system relaxes after a sudden perturbation, such as a quantum quench. In generic, non-integrable systems, this relaxation is expected to be governed by the \ac{ETH}: the system effectively acts as its own heat bath, scrambling information so that local observables eventually reach thermal equilibrium values that depend only on macroscopic conserved quantities such as the energy. Integrable systems---which possess an extensive set of local conserved charges---do not follow this mechanism because they retain memory of their initial conditions and relax instead to a \ac{GGE}. Because exact analytical solutions are largely restricted to these integrable limits, exploring the true mechanisms of thermalization, information scrambling and the emergence of chaos in non-integrable models relies almost entirely on our ability to track their long-time dynamics numerically.

Exact numerical simulations are severely limited by the exponential scaling of the Hilbert space, which makes representing a generic many-body wavefunction intractable. To circumvent this bottleneck, the community has turned to \acp{TN}, a framework that efficiently compresses quantum states by using entanglement as the organizing principle. For one-dimensional systems in equilibrium, \acp{MPS} combined with algorithms such as the \ac{DMRG} have become the state-of-the-art approach. Their success comes from the area law of entanglement: the entanglement entropy across a bipartition scales with the size of the boundary between the two halves. In 1D, the boundary is just a single point, so the entropy saturates to a constant instead of scaling with the volume of the chain (which corresponds to volume law behaviour). Since the virtual bond dimension required by an MPS is controlled by precisely this entanglement, a low-entanglement state can be represented faithfully and efficiently. By restricting the variational manifold to these low-entanglement states, the MPS ansatz isolates the exact corner of the Hilbert space where equilibrium physics actually lives.

Extracting dynamics, however, remains a challenge because of the so-called entanglement barrier. During time evolution---for instance, after a quantum quench---the entanglement entropy of the state typically grows linearly in time, exhibiting volume-law behaviour. While highly optimized algorithms such as \ac{TEBD} or the \ac{TDVP} are very successful at short times, this linear growth forces an exponentially increasing bond dimension to keep the state representation faithful, which bottlenecks any standard computation at late times.

To overcome this barrier, recent work has explored a radically different perspective: transverse contraction. Instead of evolving the state forward in time in the usual Schr\"odinger picture, the full dynamical evolution of a 1D system is encoded as a 2D tensor network in which time plays the role of a second spatial dimension. By contracting this network transversally---along the original spatial direction---one can, in favourable cases, bypass the entanglement barrier and reach late-time observables. In this picture, a new quantity appears naturally: the temporal entanglement entropy, obtained from the standard Schmidt decomposition taken across a cut in the time domain rather than across the spatial lattice. Its physical interpretation is still an open question, but it is computationally well defined and it controls the cost of the transverse contraction.

The central quantity we compute throughout this work is the Loschmidt echo: the amplitude for a system prepared in a given initial state to return to it after evolving for a time $T$. Despite its simple appearance, the echo is a rich object, and it deserves a proper introduction because everything in this thesis is ultimately organized around it. Read as a function of complex time, it is the dynamical analogue of a partition function: the evolution operator plays the role of a Boltzmann weight, the logarithm of the echo per site defines a dynamical free-energy density, and its zeros in the complex-time plane are the dynamical counterpart of the Fisher zeros of equilibrium statistical mechanics. As a probe, it captures how fast the evolved state departs from---and how coherently it returns towards---its initial condition, which is precisely the information one needs to characterize relaxation after a quench. And as a computational target it is the natural first object for the transverse method: being a scalar amplitude, with no operator insertions, its tensor network is the bare space-time grid, so its contraction isolates the cost of the method itself. Its precise definition, and the way the conformal theory organizes it, are developed in Section~\ref{sec:cft}.

The cost of that contraction turns out to be dictated by criticality, not by integrability. When a system is quenched to a conformally invariant critical point, universal data---such as the central charge of the underlying \ac{CFT}---can be read directly from the echo. In the transverse picture, the echo is governed by a spatial transfer matrix whose spectrum is predicted by the CFT, and at long times that transfer matrix becomes effectively unitary---a phenomenon known as emergent dual unitarity, whose direct consequence is that the generalized temporal entropies grow only logarithmically, so the corresponding quenches can be simulated with polynomial resources. Carignano and Tagliacozzo~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025} demonstrated this explicitly for the critical Ising and three-state Potts models. These are critical-point (CFT) statements: nothing in the argument requires the model to be exactly solvable. We develop this conformal machinery---the central charge, the transfer-matrix spectrum, and the universal formulas we will fit---in Section~\ref{sec:cft}, since it is what motivates and organizes everything that follows.

This raises naturally the central question of the present work: does emergent dual unitarity, and with it the logarithmic scaling of the generalized temporal entropies, survive when the model is no longer integrable? To answer it, we study an ANNNI-type model: a self-dual quantum spin chain that extends the transverse-field Ising model with \ac{NNN} interactions controlled by a single coupling $p$. The model serves as a deliberate stress-test for the framework. It is non-integrable for any $p>0$, yet (as we verify in Section~\ref{sec:annni}) it remains critical and stays in the Ising universality class over a wide range of $p$, so the CFT predictions still have a well-defined target. At the same time, its longer-range couplings are precisely what makes it expensive for conventional dynamical methods. In particular, we prepare the system in the fully polarized paramagnet $\ket{\Psi_0}=\ket{X+}^{\otimes N}$ (the ground state of the model in the $\lambda\to\infty$ limit), quench it to the critical point $\lambda=1$, and use transverse contraction to compute the Loschmidt echo and the generalized temporal entropies. Comparing them against the Ising CFT prediction lets us test whether universal signatures and emergent dual-unitary behaviour withstand the breaking of integrability and the introduction of NNN interactions.


The rest of this thesis is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:cft} sets up how universal data is read out of the dynamics: it maps the Loschmidt echo onto a boundary conformal field theory, shows how the central charge and the operator content appear in the spectrum of the transfer matrix and in the temporal entropy, makes explicit which constants are universal and which are lattice artifacts, and collects the predictions we will later fit. Section~\ref{sec:annni} introduces the ANNNI-type model, establishes in equilibrium that it stays critical and in the Ising universality class, and examines what the next-nearest-neighbour coupling changes---the breaking of integrability and the growth of the sound velocity with frustration. Section~\ref{sec:transverse} develops the transverse-contraction framework and the temporal entropies it gives access to, and Section~\ref{sec:mporep} details the finite-state-machine construction of the time-evolution MPO that makes the method work for next-nearest-neighbour couplings, together with the non-Hermitian transfer matrix it produces. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} presents the numerical machinery we developed for this task---the block power method, its practical infrastructure, and the figures of merit that diagnose its limits. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results: we first reproduce the known Ising conformal predictions at the integrable point, then switch on frustration and test whether the universal signatures survive, and finally map the limits of the method itself. Technical derivations that support---but are not essential to follow---this main line are collected in the appendices, to which each section points.

\section{Conclusions and Outlook}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\textit{This section is a provisional summary: the numerical campaign it draws on is complete for $p=0$ and $p=0.1$, while the sweep to stronger frustration is still in progress, and its conclusions should be read as current rather than final.}

The question that motivated this work was whether the universal, conformal signatures that make transverse contraction efficient at a critical point---emergent dual unitarity and the logarithmic growth of the generalized temporal entropies---survive when the model is no longer integrable. Our answer, for next-nearest-neighbour frustration up to $p=0.1$, is that they do. We established this in three steps. At the integrable point $p=0$ the transverse pipeline reproduces the known Ising conformal predictions along four independent axes---the R\'enyi-2 entropy chord, its imaginary plateau, the free-boundary exponent $x_1=\tfrac12$, and the $\lambda_0$ circle with its eigenvalue extraction of the central charge---which calibrates the method and fixes our conventions. Switching on frustration, the temporal central charge at $p=0.1$ comes out $c=0.47\pm0.05$ from the entropy slope and $0.50$--$0.53$ from the independent eigenvalue route, both consistent with the Ising value $c=\tfrac12$, and the boundary content is unchanged: once the odd-parity boundary tower---which the next-nearest-neighbour interaction displaces in phase by momentum $\pi$---is read on the correct side of the spectrum, the free-boundary exponent $x_1=\tfrac12$ is recovered at $p=0.1$ as well. Integrability, in other words, is not what the conformal structure of these quenches requires; criticality is.

Beyond this physics result, the work makes a few methodological contributions. The finite-state-machine construction of the time-evolution MPO, combined with the second-order Van~Damme exponentiation, gives a genuinely translation-invariant, second-order propagator for a next-nearest-neighbour Hamiltonian---the ingredient that lets the transverse method reach the thermodynamic limit for a model outside the usual nearest-neighbour setting. To resolve the non-Hermitian transfer matrix through the gap closing we developed a left--right block power method with exact biorthogonal pairing and a Schur-stable subspace basis, which extends the usable window well past the single-vector iteration. Reading the transfer spectrum correctly was itself part of the work: the near-$\pm$ pairing of the leading eigenvalues that had looked like a nuisance turned out to be the odd-parity boundary tower displaced by momentum $\pi$, and recognizing it fixed both the boundary-exponent extraction and the modulus-dominant branch-selection rule on which the stronger-frustration data depend. And we mapped, quantitatively, where and why the method ultimately fails: the entanglement barrier is a near-exceptional point of the transfer matrix, diagnosed by the geometric collapse of the phase rigidity, at which the eigenvalues remain well-conditioned but the individual eigenvectors---and therefore the temporal entropy---do not. We further verified that this barrier is set by the physics rather than by our numerical resolution: neither the bond dimension, nor the truncation cutoff, nor the choice between the two truncation schemes moves it. Characterizing that limit, rather than merely hitting it, is itself part of the contribution.

Several threads remain open, and are the natural continuation of this project. The most immediate is completing the sweep to stronger frustration, spanning $p=0.3$ through $p=1.5$ and extended to longer times: the low-frustration arms already confirm that temporal Ising universality persists with a monotonically shrinking reach, and finishing the higher-$p$ arms---which require the automatic block-size escalation and the corrected branch selector developed here---will turn the picture into a full $c(p)$ curve and pin down the strong-frustration edge, where an exact top-spectrum degeneracy finally stops the method. The resolution of the momentum-$\pi$ displaced boundary tower leaves one theory question behind it: a first-principles selection of which sector carries the $\pm i$ half-step index that quantizes the displacement at exactly $\pi$. Smaller open questions include a sub-leading boundary state at $p\gtrsim0.15$ that matches no Ising boundary dimension, and a systematic study of the Trotter-step dependence of the wall. Looking further out, the same framework applies unchanged to models whose quench carries an exact symmetry degeneracy or a larger central charge---for instance the XXZ chain quenched from a N\'eel state, or the tricritical Ising point---where preliminary work indicates that the barrier arrives markedly earlier, for reasons tied to the symmetry content of the quench and to the value of $c$; a systematic account of how these dials set the barrier's position is a promising direction. Finally, the finite-$\ell$ boundary-weighted construction that survives the wall as a diagnostic hints that the physically meaningful object past the degeneracy may be a genuinely finite-width temporal entropy rather than its thermodynamic limit---a reframing worth pursuing in its own right.
