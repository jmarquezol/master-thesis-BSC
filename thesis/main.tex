\begin{abstract}
The universal properties of a critical many-body system---its central charge and operator content---can be extracted directly from its dynamics, without ever preparing a low-energy state. At a conformally invariant critical point the Loschmidt echo of a quench from a simple product state maps onto the partition function of a boundary conformal field theory on a strip, whose universal data can be read from a transfer matrix built by contracting the space-time tensor network along the spatial direction. This transverse contraction treats time as a spatial dimension, and its cost is set by a temporal entanglement that grows only logarithmically at criticality, sidestepping the entanglement barrier that limits conventional methods. Established so far for integrable chains, we ask whether this route to universal data survives the loss of integrability. We study an ANNNI-type model---the transverse-field Ising chain extended by next-nearest-neighbour interactions of strength $p$---which stays critical and in the Ising universality class while becoming non-integrable and longer-ranged. Encoding its evolution as a translation-invariant finite-state-machine MPO and contracting it with a left--right block power method adapted to the non-Hermitian transfer matrix, we recover the conformal signatures at $p=0$ and find that they persist for $p\neq0$: the temporal central charge stays consistent with the Ising value $c=\tfrac12$, and the boundary operator content is reproduced, including an odd-parity tower displaced by momentum $\pi$ that the interaction generates. Throughout, we separate carefully the universal quantities from the non-universal lattice constants that accompany them, and we map the limits of the approach. \textit{This is a working draft.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro}

How a closed quantum system evolves after being driven far from equilibrium is one of the central questions of modern many-body physics. Simulating such dynamics is difficult, but it can also be revealing: at criticality, the real-time evolution of a simple initial state can expose universal information normally associated with low-energy physics. Our aim with this thesis is to determine how far this dynamical route can be pushed once the system is no longer exactly solvable.

This question is particularly relevant for non-integrable systems, which lack exact analytical solutions. Their dynamics is also intrinsically harder to simulate over long times: after a quench these systems are generically expected to thermalize, acting as their own heat bath so that local observables relax to values fixed only by the energy---the content of the \ac{ETH}---whereas integrable models, protected by extensively many conserved charges, retain memory of their initial state instead. Analytical results are largely confined to those integrable limits, so almost everything we know about thermalization, scrambling and the onset of chaos in non-integrable models rests on being able to follow their dynamics numerically.

Exact numerical simulations are severely limited by the exponential scaling of the Hilbert space, which makes representing a generic many-body wavefunction intractable. To circumvent this bottleneck, the community has turned to \acp{TN}, a framework that efficiently compresses quantum states by using entanglement as the organizing principle. For one-dimensional systems in equilibrium, \acp{MPS} combined with algorithms such as the \ac{DMRG} have become the state-of-the-art approach~\cite{schollwock2011,banuls2023}. Their efficiency comes from the area law of entanglement: the entanglement entropy across a bipartition of a gapped 1D system saturates to a constant rather than growing with subsystem size~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}. Since the required MPS bond dimension is controlled by this entanglement, the ansatz efficiently targets the low-entanglement corner of Hilbert space where the relevant equilibrium states lie.

Simulating dynamics, however, runs into the so-called entanglement barrier. After a quantum quench, the entanglement entropy typically grows linearly in time, approaching volume-law behaviour. Although highly optimized algorithms such as \ac{TEBD} and the \ac{TDVP} are very effective at short times, faithfully representing this growth requires a bond dimension that increases exponentially with time, making standard MPS simulations exponentially costly at late times. Several strategies address this barrier, including sampling the space-time network amplitude by amplitude~\cite{carignano2025sampled}.

To overcome this barrier, transverse contraction adopts a different perspective. Rather than evolving a state forward in the Schr\"odinger picture, it represents the full dynamics as a two-dimensional tensor network and contracts it along the original spatial direction, treating time as a second spatial dimension. The computational cost is then controlled by the temporal entanglement entropy, defined through a Schmidt decomposition across a cut in time rather than in space. Although its physical interpretation remains an open question, it is computationally well defined. For a generic quench it may grow as strongly as spatial entanglement; nevertheless, the transverse viewpoint can provide a useful way to tackle specific dynamical quantities, such as the Loschmidt echo studied here, as we will see.

The central quantity we compute throughout is the Loschmidt echo, the amplitude for a state prepared in product form to return to itself after evolving for a time $T$. Viewed as a function of complex time, it is the dynamical analogue of a partition function: the evolution operator acts as a Boltzmann weight, the logarithm of the echo per site defines a dynamical free-energy density, and its zeros are the dynamical counterparts of the Fisher zeros of equilibrium statistical mechanics. The echo therefore probes how quickly the evolved state departs from, and how coherently it returns towards, its initial condition, providing a natural measure of relaxation after a quench. It is also an ideal target for the transverse method: as a scalar amplitude with no operator insertions, its tensor network is the bare space-time grid, so its contraction isolates the cost of the method itself.

At a conformally invariant critical point, the echo provides direct access to universal data such as the central charge of the underlying \ac{CFT}. In the transverse picture, it is governed by a spatial transfer matrix whose spectrum is predicted by the CFT and which becomes effectively unitary at long times---a phenomenon known as emergent dual unitarity. As a consequence, the generalized temporal entropies grow only logarithmically, allowing the corresponding quenches to be simulated with polynomial resources. This behaviour was demonstrated explicitly for the critical Ising and three-state Potts chains in Ref.~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}, while Ref.~\cite{boucomas2026} sharpened the connection between the echo and the full conformal data, including the finite-time corrections used later in this thesis. Crucially, these are critical-point statements: the argument requires conformal invariance, not exact solvability.

The central question is therefore whether these conformal signatures survive the loss of integrability. To address it, we study an ANNNI-type model: a self-dual quantum spin chain that extends the transverse-field Ising model with \ac{NNN} interactions controlled by a coupling $p$~\cite{alcaraz2016}. The model provides a deliberate stress test: for any $p>0$ it is non-integrable and longer-ranged, making its dynamics costly for conventional methods, yet it remains critical and in the Ising universality class over a wide range of $p$, so the CFT predictions retain a clear target. We prepare the system in the fully polarized paramagnet state, quench it to the critical point $\lambda=1$, and use transverse contraction to compute the Loschmidt echo and the generalized temporal entropies, which we compare with the Ising CFT predictions.


The rest of this thesis is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:cft} defines the Loschmidt echo and develops the CFT framework that relates the transfer-matrix spectrum and temporal entropy to the central charge and operator content, while separating universal predictions from lattice-dependent constants. Section~\ref{sec:annni} introduces the ANNNI-type model, establishes its critical Ising universality, and examines the breaking of integrability and the frustration-dependent sound velocity. Sections~\ref{sec:transverse} and~\ref{sec:mporep} develop the transverse-contraction framework and temporal entropies, followed by the construction of the time-evolution MPO and its non-Hermitian transfer matrix. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} presents the block power method and the diagnostics of its limitations, while Section~\ref{sec:results} validates the Ising predictions at the integrable point, tests their survival under frustration, and maps the method's range of applicability. Supporting technical derivations are collected in the appendices.

\section{Conclusions and Outlook}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This work asked whether the conformal structure that makes transverse contraction efficient at a critical point survives when the model is no longer integrable. For the self-dual ANNNI-type chain, up to the frustration we can resolve, it does. The temporal central charge at $p=0.1$ is $c=0.47\pm0.05$ from the calibrated entropy slope and $0.54$ from the independent eigenvalue route, both consistent with the Ising value. The free-boundary exponent $x_1\approx\tfrac12$ is recovered at both couplings, once the spectrum is read correctly. Emergent dual unitarity persists at every coupling in the sweep: each arm settles onto its own constant modulus while the phase winds. What the conformal predictions require is criticality---not integrability.

The two families of measurements behave very differently along the way. The eigenvalues are robust. At the integrable point they carry the central charge and the boundary exponents out to $T=20$, far past the point where the entropy is gone. Over that window the branch constant of the phase fit also settles at its exact value $-\pi$. Under frustration they still give dual unitarity, the boundary content and the central charge. The fit even announces where its window ends, since the branch constant drifts off $-\pi$ exactly where the tracking starts to lose the physical eigenvalue. The temporal entropy instead ends at a wall. Its onset is the approach to an exceptional point, announced by the geometric collapse of the phase rigidity. It is set by the physics rather than by the resolution: neither the bond dimension, nor the truncation cutoff, nor the choice of truncation scheme moves it.

Frustration makes the simulation genuinely harder than the integrable case. The sound velocity is the main reason. Every boundary dimension is read from a phase gap proportional to $1/v$, so a larger velocity compresses the whole spectrum into a narrower window, and the displaced tower crowds a second family into it. It is not the only reason: comparing the two chains at equal $vT$ shows the integrable one still running clean where the frustrated one has already walled, so the displaced family costs reach in its own right by occupying half of the leading block. The rigidity then collapses faster and the usable windows shrink accordingly. The entropy route is quantitative to $T=9$ at $p=0.1$ and closes almost at once beyond it, while the eigenvalue route reaches $T=11$ at $p=0.1$ and loses the branch after a few rungs at stronger frustration. Completing the $c(p)$ table therefore needs finer time ladders at $p\ge0.3$---not more of the same ones.

Reading the spectrum carefully also turned up a genuine feature of the interacting model. For any $p\neq0$ the entire odd-parity boundary tower, which contains the lightest boundary operator, is displaced in phase by momentum $\pi$. The displacement originates in the parity-dressed hopping generated by the NNN interaction, is absent for free fermions, and is what makes a naive boundary-exponent estimator fail. Recognizing it fixed the measurement and dictated the branch-selection rules on which the frustrated data depend.

The work makes three methodological contributions. The first is a translation-invariant second-order propagator for a next-nearest-neighbour Hamiltonian, built from the \ac{FSM} representation and the Van Damme exponentiation. The second is a left--right block power method with exact biorthogonal pairing, a Schur-stable subspace basis, and a phase-predicted branch tracker for the near-degenerate regime. The third is a quantitative map of where and why the approach fails, with the phase rigidity as the practical diagnostic.

Two directions follow naturally. The first is algorithmic. The de-mixing step dominates the cost of every iteration, the stagnation of late rungs dominates their number, and the branch tracking is limited by the spacing of the ladder in $T$. Progress on any of the three extends the reach at fixed frustration, and finer ladders alone would already unlock the eigenvalue route at $p\ge0.3$. The second is physical. The mechanism selecting which sector carries the momentum-$\pi$ displacement is not yet derived from the lattice model. The eigenvalue-route window at $p\neq0$ is also still short enough that the Casimir coefficient depends on it, and the $c(p)$ curve across the critical range remains to be completed. The same framework applies unchanged to quenches with exact symmetry degeneracies or larger central charge, where the barrier arrives earlier. How it depends on the symmetry content of the quench is worth a systematic study of its own.
