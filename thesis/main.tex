\begin{abstract}
  The Loschmidt echo---the amplitude for a quantum state to return to itself---provides a dynamical route to obtain universal properties of a critical many-body system. Analytically continuing from real to imaginary time, the echo becomes the partition function of a conformal field theory on a strip. The central charge and operator content of the corresponding conformal-field theory are then encoded in the spectrum of a transfer matrix, obtained by contracting the space--time tensor network along the space direction. The cost of this transverse contraction is governed by the temporal entanglement, which grows only logarithmically at criticality, allowing the method to reach times where conventional evolution algorithms are limited by the entanglement barrier. These results were established for integrable chains, and this thesis extends them to the non-integrable regime. We study a transverse-field Ising model extended by next-nearest-neighbour interactions, which remains critical and in the Ising universality class at weak frustration. We encode its evolution as a matrix product operator, and we resolve the leading spectrum of the resulting non-Hermitian transfer matrix with a block power method that evolves its left and right eigenvectors together. Where the method allows, we recover the conformal signatures: the central charge remains consistent with the Ising value $c=\tfrac12$, and the boundary operator content of the Ising class is reproduced. Throughout, we separate the universal quantities from the non-universal lattice constants that accompany them, and we map where and why the method reaches its limits. \textit{This is a working draft.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro}

Following a closed quantum system after being driven far from equilibrium is one of the central questions of modern many-body physics. Simulating such dynamics is difficult, but it can also be revealing: at criticality, the real-time evolution of a simple initial state can expose universal information normally associated with low-energy physics. Our aim with this thesis is to determine how far this dynamical route can be pushed once the system is no longer exactly solvable.

This question is particularly relevant for non-integrable systems, which lack exact analytical solutions. Their dynamics is also intrinsically harder to simulate over long times: after a quench these systems are generically expected to thermalize, acting as their own heat bath so that local observables relax to values fixed only by the energy---the content of the \ac{ETH}~\cite{deutsch1991,srednicki1994,dalessio2016}---whereas integrable models, protected by extensively many conserved charges, retain memory of their initial state instead~\cite{rigol2007}. Analytical results are largely confined to those integrable limits, so almost everything we know about thermalization, scrambling of the quantum information and the onset of chaos in non-integrable models depends on our ability to simulate their dynamics numerically.

Exact numerical simulations are severely limited by the exponential scaling of the Hilbert space, which makes representing a generic many-body wavefunction intractable. To circumvent this bottleneck, the community has turned to \acp{TN}, a framework that efficiently compresses quantum states by using entanglement as the organizing principle. For one-dimensional systems in equilibrium, \acp{MPS} combined with algorithms such as the \ac{DMRG} have become the state-of-the-art approach~\cite{white1992,schollwock2011,banuls2023}. Their efficiency comes from the area law of entanglement, which holds for ground states of one-dimensional systems with local gapped Hamiltonians: the entanglement entropy across a bipartition saturates to a constant rather than growing with the subsystem size~\cite{hastings2007}. Since the required \ac{MPS} bond dimension is controlled by this entanglement, the ansatz efficiently targets the low-entanglement corner of Hilbert space where these ground states lie.

Simulating dynamics, however, runs into the so-called entanglement barrier. After a quantum quench, the entanglement entropy typically grows linearly in time, approaching volume-law behaviour~\cite{calabrese_cardy2005}. Although highly optimized algorithms such as \ac{TEBD}~\cite{vidal2004} and the \ac{TDVP}~\cite{haegeman2011} are very effective at short times, faithfully representing this growth requires a bond dimension that increases exponentially with time, making standard simulations exponentially costly at late times~\cite{schuch2008,paeckel2019}.

To overcome this barrier, transverse contraction is a framework that adopts a different perspective~\cite{banuls2009,muller_hermes2012}. Rather than evolving a state forward in the Schr\"odinger picture, it represents the full dynamics as a two-dimensional tensor network and contracts it along the original spatial direction. The computational cost is then controlled by the temporal entanglement entropy, defined through a Schmidt decomposition across a cut in time rather than in space. Although its physical interpretation remains an open question, it is computationally well defined. For a generic quench it may grow as strongly as spatial entanglement; nevertheless, the transverse viewpoint can provide a useful way to tackle specific dynamical quantities.

The central quantity we compute throughout is the Loschmidt echo for an infinite, translation-invariant system: the amplitude for a state to return to itself after evolving for a time $T$~\cite{gorin2006}. It measures how quickly the evolved state departs from, and how coherently it returns towards, its initial condition, providing a natural measure of relaxation after a quench. Given its structure, transverse contraction offers an efficient way to compute it by contracting the space--time network directly with a power method~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}.

At a conformally invariant critical point, the echo also provides direct access to universal data such as the central charge of the underlying \ac{CFT}. In the transverse picture, it is governed by a spatial transfer matrix whose spectrum is predicted by the \ac{CFT} and which becomes effectively unitary at long times---a phenomenon known as emergent dual unitarity~\cite{bertini2019}. This behaviour has been demonstrated explicitly for the critical Ising and three-state Potts chains~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}, together with the finite-time corrections~\cite{boucomas2026} that we use in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

The central question is therefore whether these conformal signatures survive the loss of integrability of the model. To address it, we study a self-dual quantum spin chain in the thermodynamic limit that extends the transverse-field Ising model with \ac{NNN} interactions controlled by a coupling $p$~\cite{alcaraz2016}. For any $p>0$ it is non-integrable and longer-ranged, making its dynamics costly for conventional methods, yet it remains critical and in the Ising universality class over a wide range of $p$~\cite{alcaraz2016}, so the \ac{CFT} predictions provide a clear target for numerical simulations. We prepare the system in the fully polarized paramagnet state, quench it to the critical point, and use the transverse contraction machinery to compute the Loschmidt echo and the generalized temporal entropies, which we compare with the \ac{CFT} predictions.


The rest of this thesis is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:cft} defines the Loschmidt echo and develops the CFT framework that relates the transfer-matrix spectrum and generalized temporal entropy to the central charge and operator content. Section~\ref{sec:transverse} develops the transverse-contraction framework: the rotated picture, the temporal states, and the transition matrix from which the generalized temporal entropies are built. Section~\ref{sec:annni} introduces the ANNNI-type model, establishes its critical Ising universality, and measures the frustration-dependent sound velocity. Section~\ref{sec:mporep} constructs the time-evolution MPO and its non-Hermitian transfer matrix. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} presents the block power method, and Section~\ref{sec:results} validates the numerical results with the \ac{CFT} predictions.

\section{Conclusions and Outlook}
\label{sec:conclusions}


The question that motivated this thesis was whether the conformal signatures accessible through transverse contraction persist when integrability is broken. For the self-dual ANNNI-type chain and the dynamical couplings studied here, the results remain consistent with the Ising universality class. The leading transfer-matrix eigenvalue has an approximately time-independent modulus and a winding phase, as predicted by emergent dual unitarity. Its phase curvature gives a central charge compatible with $c=\tfrac12$ within the resolution of the fits, while the leading phase gaps reproduce the Ising boundary dimensions (Table~\ref{tab:cp}). These results indicate that the conformal structure used by the transverse method is not restricted to the integrable point. Within the critical regime considered, the \ac{NNN} interaction modifies the non-universal scales without changing the resolved universal content.

Obtaining these results required a transfer-matrix construction suitable for longer-range interactions. We represent the Hamiltonian as a \ac{FSM}, exponentiate it with a second-order scheme, and rotate the resulting \ac{MPO} into a column of the space--time network. The standard second-order construction for \ac{NN} Hamiltonians becomes first order in the presence of \ac{NNN} terms, whereas the \textit{VD2} construction retains second-order accuracy by including the corresponding three-site clusters. We then determine the leading spectrum with a block power method that propagates left and right temporal states together and preserves their biorthogonal pairing. An eigenvalue-only variant replaces the individual eigenvectors by well-conditioned bases of the leading subspaces when the calculation does not require the eigenvectors themselves.

The equilibrium analysis provides the non-universal input and an independent reference for the dynamical results. \ac{DMRG} calculations give a central charge consistent with the Ising value up to $p=1.5$, while exact diagonalisation of finite rings determines the sound velocity at each coupling (Section~\ref{sec:annni}). This velocity converts the transfer-matrix phase gaps into scaling dimensions and must therefore be determined independently. The equilibrium and dynamical estimates of the central charge are consistent within the numerical resolution of the dynamical fits, although the latter deviate from $\tfrac12$ by up to approximately ten per cent over the reported range.

The entropy and spectral routes have different numerical ranges because they require different parts of the eigensystem. First-order perturbation theory shows that eigenvector mixing is inversely proportional to the separation between neighbouring eigenvalues, whereas the corresponding eigenvalue shift contains no spectral-gap denominator. As the conformal phase gaps decrease with increasing $T$, separating individual eigenvectors within the converged block becomes ill conditioned. The entropy calculation requires the dominant left and right eigenvectors and consequently loses reliability at earlier times. The spectral calculation requires only the Ritz values of the leading subspace and remains usable over a wider range.

This conditioning argument explains the greater stability of the eigenvalues, but it does not fully determine when an entropy calculation fails. The phase rigidity decreases with evolution time and more rapidly at stronger coupling, yet its value at the last reliable time varies by four orders of magnitude across the couplings studied. Failures are also intermittent and depend on the random initial states: a calculation can fail at one time, converge at the next, or be recovered by repeating it with a different seed. Moreover, the bond dimension, truncation cutoff, and eigenvalue-selection rule were chosen through internal consistency tests rather than a systematic optimisation. The reported time windows should therefore be understood as the reach of the present numerical configuration, not as fundamental limits of transverse contraction.

The sound velocity provides a useful indicator of this numerical reach. Boundary dimensions are extracted from phase gaps proportional to $1/(vT)$, so increasing $v$ compresses the spectrum and raises the resolution required at a fixed evolution time. Since $v$ can be measured independently in equilibrium, this compression can be estimated before performing the dynamical calculation.

Several improvements follow directly from these limitations. A systematic study of the bond dimension, truncation cutoff, and eigenvalue selector is needed to determine which parameter controls convergence in each regime. The computational cost is dominated by the de-mixing and compression of the propagated block states, so reducing this cost would extend the accessible evolution times. With these improvements, the dynamical central charge $c(p)$ and leading boundary dimension $x_1(p)$ could be followed towards the boundary of the critical phase, where the equilibrium calculations remain consistent with Ising criticality but the present dynamical windows are too short for reliable fits.
