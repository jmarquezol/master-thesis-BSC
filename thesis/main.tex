\begin{abstract}
Simulating the long-time dynamics of quantum many-body systems is obstructed by the entanglement barrier: after a quench the spatial entanglement grows linearly in time, forcing an exponential cost on standard tensor-network methods. Transverse contraction sidesteps this by treating time as a spatial dimension and contracting the resulting network along the original space direction, where the relevant cost is set instead by a temporal entanglement that, for quenches to a conformally invariant critical point, grows only logarithmically. In this work we take the conformal-field-theory predictions established for the integrable Ising chain by Carignano and Tagliacozzo and test whether they survive the breaking of integrability. We study an ANNNI-type model---the transverse-field Ising chain extended by next-nearest-neighbour interactions of strength $p$---which stays critical and in the Ising universality class while becoming non-integrable and longer-ranged. Encoding its time evolution as a translation-invariant finite-state-machine MPO and contracting it with a left--right block power method adapted to the resulting non-Hermitian transfer matrix, we reproduce the known conformal signatures at $p=0$ and find that they persist at $p=0.1$, where the temporal central charge remains consistent with the Ising value $c=\tfrac12$. We further characterize the limits of the approach: the entanglement barrier reappears as a near-exceptional point of the transfer matrix, at which the eigenvalues stay accurate but the eigenvectors---and hence the temporal entropy---become ill-defined. \textit{This is a working draft; the sweep to stronger frustration is ongoing.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Motivation}

The study of out-of-equilibrium dynamics in many-body quantum systems remains one of the most active areas of research in quantum physics, holding the key to understanding and characterizing fundamental phenomena arising from these complex systems.

A fundamental question in contemporary physics is how a closed quantum system relaxes
after a sudden perturbation, such as a quantum quench. In generic, non-integrable systems this relaxation is expected to be governed by the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH): the system effectively acts as its own heat bath, scrambling information so that local observables eventually reach thermal equilibrium values that depend only on macroscopic conserved quantities such as the energy. Integrable systems---which possess an extensive set of local conserved charges---do not follow this mechanism because they retain memory of their initial conditions and relax instead to a Generalized Gibbs Ensemble (GGE). Because exact analytical solutions are largely restricted to these integrable limits, exploring the true mechanisms of thermalization, information scrambling and the emergence of chaos in non-integrable models relies almost entirely on our ability to track their long-time dynamics numerically.

Exact numerical simulations are severely limited by the exponential scaling of the Hilbert space, which makes representing a generic many-body wavefunction intractable. To circumvent this bottleneck, the community has turned to Tensor Networks (TN), a framework that efficiently compresses quantum states by using entanglement as the organizing principle. For one-dimensional systems in equilibrium, Matrix Product States (MPS) combined with algorithms such as the Density Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG) have become the state-of-the-art approach. Their success comes from the area law of entanglement: the entanglement entropy across a bipartition scales with the size of the boundary between the two halves. In 1D, the boundary is just a single point, so the entropy saturates to a constant instead of scaling with the volume of the chain (which corresponds to volume law behaviour). Since the virtual bond dimension required by an MPS is controlled by precisely this entanglement, a low-entanglement state can be represented faithfully and efficiently. By restricting the variational manifold to these low-entanglement states, the MPS ansatz isolates the exact corner of the Hilbert space where equilibrium physics actually lives.

Extracting dynamics, however, remains a challenge because of the so-called entanglement barrier. During time evolution---for instance, after a quantum quench---the entanglement entropy of the state typically grows linearly in time, exhibiting volume-law behaviour. While highly optimized algorithms such as Time-Evolving Block Decimation (TEBD) or the Time-Dependent Variational Principle (TDVP) are very successful at short times, this linear growth forces an exponentially increasing bond dimension to keep the state representation faithful, which bottlenecks any standard computation at late times.

To overcome this barrier, recent work has explored a radically different perspective: transverse contraction. Instead of evolving the state forward in time in the usual Schr\"odinger picture, the full dynamical evolution of a 1D system is encoded as a 2D tensor network in which time plays the role of a second spatial dimension. By contracting this network transversally---along the original spatial direction---one can, in favourable cases, bypass the entanglement barrier and reach late-time observables. In this picture a new quantity appears naturally: the temporal entanglement entropy, obtained from the standard Schmidt decomposition taken across a cut in the time domain rather than across the spatial lattice. Its physical interpretation is still an open question, but it is computationally well defined and it controls the cost of the transverse contraction.

It is worth mentioning that this cost is dictated by criticality, not by integrability. When a system is quenched to a conformally invariant critical point, universal data---such as the central charge of the underlying Conformal Field Theory (CFT)---can be read directly from the Loschmidt echo, the return amplitude of the initial state after the quench. In the transverse picture this echo is governed by a spatial transfer matrix whose spectrum the CFT predicts, and at long times that transfer matrix becomes effectively unitary---a phenomenon known as emergent dual unitarity, whose direct consequence is that the generalized temporal entropies grow only logarithmically, so the corresponding quenches can be simulated with polynomial resources. Carignano and Tagliacozzo~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025} demonstrated this explicitly for the critical Ising and three-state Potts models. These are critical-point (CFT) statements: nothing in the argument requires the model to be exactly solvable. We develop this conformal machinery---the central charge, the transfer-matrix spectrum, and the universal formulas we will fit---in Section~\ref{sec:cft}, since it is what motivates and organizes everything that follows.

This raises naturally the central question of the present work: does emergent dual unitarity, and with it the logarithmic scaling of the generalized temporal entropies, survive when the model is no longer integrable? To answer it, we study an ANNNI-type model: a self-dual quantum spin chain that extends the transverse-field Ising model with next-nearest-neighbour (NNN) interactions controlled by a single coupling $p$. The model serves as a deliberate stress-test for the framework. It is non-integrable for any $p>0$, yet (as we verify in Section~\ref{sec:annni}) it remains critical and stays in the Ising universality class over a wide range of $p$, so the CFT predictions still have a well-defined target. At the same time, its longer-range couplings are precisely what makes it expensive for conventional dynamical methods. In particular, we prepare the system in the fully polarized paramagnet $\ket{\Psi_0}=\ket{X+}^{\otimes N}$ (the ground state of the model in the $\lambda\to\infty$ limit), quench it to the critical point $\lambda=1$, and use transverse contraction to compute the Loschmidt echo and the generalized temporal entropies. Comparing them against the Ising CFT prediction lets us test whether universal signatures and emergent dual-unitary behaviour withstand the breaking of integrability and the introduction of NNN interactions.


The rest of this thesis is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:cft} reviews the conformal-field-theory background that makes all of this quantitative: what the central charge is, how it fixes the spectrum of the transfer matrix and the growth of the temporal entropy, and the handful of universal formulas we will later fit. Section~\ref{sec:annni} introduces the ANNNI-type model and establishes, in equilibrium, that it stays critical and in the Ising universality class. Section~\ref{sec:transverse} develops the transverse-contraction framework and the temporal entropies it gives access to; Section~\ref{sec:mporep} details the finite-state-machine construction of the time-evolution MPO that makes the method work for next-nearest-neighbour couplings; and Section~\ref{sec:nonhermitian} discusses the non-Hermitian transfer matrix, its emergent dual unitarity, and the block power method we use to resolve its leading spectrum. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results: we first reproduce the known Ising conformal predictions at the integrable point, then switch on frustration and test whether the universal signatures survive, and finally map the limits of the method itself.

\section{Conclusions and Outlook}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\textit{This section is a provisional summary: the numerical campaign it draws on is complete for $p=0$ and $p=0.1$, while the sweep to stronger frustration is still in progress, and its conclusions should be read as current rather than final.}

The question that motivated this work was whether the universal, conformal signatures that make transverse contraction efficient at a critical point---emergent dual unitarity and the logarithmic growth of the generalized temporal entropies---survive when the model is no longer integrable. Our answer, for next-nearest-neighbour frustration up to $p=0.1$, is that they do. We established this in three steps. At the integrable point $p=0$ the transverse pipeline reproduces the known Ising conformal predictions along four independent axes---the R\'enyi-2 entropy chord, its imaginary plateau, the free-boundary exponent $x_1=\tfrac12$, and the $\lambda_0$ circle with its eigenvalue extraction of the central charge---which calibrates the method and fixes our conventions. Switching on frustration, the temporal central charge at $p=0.1$ comes out $c=0.47\pm0.05$ from the entropy slope and $0.50$--$0.53$ from the independent eigenvalue route, both consistent with the Ising value $c=\tfrac12$, and the boundary content is unchanged. Integrability, in other words, is not what the conformal structure of these quenches requires; criticality is.

Beyond this physics result, the work makes a few methodological contributions. The finite-state-machine construction of the time-evolution MPO, combined with the second-order Van~Damme exponentiation, gives a genuinely translation-invariant, second-order propagator for a next-nearest-neighbour Hamiltonian---the ingredient that lets the transverse method reach the thermodynamic limit for a model outside the usual nearest-neighbour setting. To resolve the non-Hermitian transfer matrix through the gap closing we developed a left--right block power method with exact biorthogonal pairing and a Schur-stable subspace basis, which extends the usable window well past the single-vector iteration. And we mapped, quantitatively, where and why the method ultimately fails: the entanglement barrier is a near-exceptional point of the transfer matrix, diagnosed by the geometric collapse of the phase rigidity, at which the eigenvalues remain well-conditioned but the individual eigenvectors---and therefore the temporal entropy---do not. Characterizing that limit, rather than merely hitting it, is itself part of the contribution.

Several threads remain open, and are the natural continuation of this project. The most immediate is the sweep to stronger frustration, $p=0.3$ and $p=0.5$, which will turn the single $p=0.1$ result into a $c(p)$ curve and determine whether temporal Ising universality persists across the whole critical range or eventually departs from it; the observation that the barrier arrives earlier as $p$ grows already suggests that frustration systematically compresses the usable window, a trend that sweep will quantify. Smaller open questions include the anomalous boundary exponent seen in one estimator at $p=0.1$, the origin of the near-$\pm$ pairing of the leading transfer eigenvalues, and a systematic study of the Trotter-step dependence of the wall. Looking further out, the same framework applies unchanged to models whose quench carries an exact symmetry degeneracy or a larger central charge---for instance the XXZ chain quenched from a N\'eel state, or the tricritical Ising point---where preliminary work indicates that the barrier arrives markedly earlier, for reasons tied to the symmetry content of the quench and to the value of $c$; a systematic account of how these dials set the barrier's position is a promising direction. Finally, the finite-$\ell$ boundary-weighted construction that survives the wall as a diagnostic hints that the physically meaningful object past the degeneracy may be a genuinely finite-width temporal entropy rather than its thermodynamic limit---a reframing worth pursuing in its own right.
