\section{Optimal Truncation and the Transition Matrix}
\label{app:tensor_networks}

For the general framework of tensor networks---diagrammatic notation, matrix product states and operators, canonical forms and the standard algorithms---we refer to the reviews of Refs.~\cite{schollwock2011,banuls2023}. This appendix records only the linear algebra behind the truncation choice of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}: why truncating on Schmidt values is optimal for a single state, why gauge freedom decides which spectra are physical, and how the construction generalizes to the \ac{RTM}.

\subsection{Gauge freedom and canonical forms}
\label{app:sub:canonical_forms}

The \ac{MPS} representation of a state is not unique. On any virtual bond one may insert $\mathbb{1}=XX^{-1}$ and absorb the two factors into the neighbouring tensors, $A^{[n]}\to A^{[n]}X$ and $A^{[n+1]}\to X^{-1}A^{[n+1]}$, changing the tensors while leaving the state untouched. The canonical form fixes this freedom: sweeping QR or SVD decompositions inward from both ends makes every tensor an isometry, $A^\dagger A=\mathbb{1}$ from the left and $BB^\dagger=\mathbb{1}$ from the right, with one centre tensor carrying the non-trivial weight.

Gauge freedom draws the line between physical and representational quantities. Anything that depends on the individual tensors rather than on the state they encode can be changed at will by a gauge transformation, and is therefore not meaningful to truncate on. In the single-state setting this causes no trouble, because the canonical form fixes the gauge and the Schmidt values below are automatically the physical ones. In the transverse picture it becomes the deciding issue, as Section~\ref{app:sub:rtm_truncation} shows.

\subsection{Schmidt values and optimal truncation}
\label{app:sub:truncation}

Consider a cut dividing the chain into a block $\mathcal{A}$ and its complement $\mathcal{B}$. Grouping the physical indices on each side into one composite index turns the wavefunction into a matrix, whose \ac{SVD} gives the Schmidt decomposition
\begin{equation}
  \ket\psi = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{\chi} s_\alpha\,
    \ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal A}\otimes\ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal B},
  \qquad s_\alpha\ge0,\quad \sum_\alpha s_\alpha^2=1,
\end{equation}
with orthonormal bases on the two sides. In mixed canonical form this structure sits on the cut bond: the centre object is the diagonal matrix of Schmidt coefficients, and the \ac{RDM} of the block is diagonal in the same basis with eigenvalues $p_\alpha=s_\alpha^2$, the probabilities that fix the entanglement across the cut.

Truncating a bond from $\chi$ to $\chi'<\chi$ means keeping the $\chi'$ largest Schmidt coefficients. This is optimal in a precise sense: by the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem, among all states of Schmidt rank at most $\chi'$ the truncated state is the closest to $\ket\psi$ in norm, with an error equal to the discarded weight,
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = \big\lVert \ket\psi - \ket{\tilde\psi}\big\rVert_2^2
    = \sum_{\alpha>\chi'} s_\alpha^2.
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:truncation} shows why the truncation can be performed locally in canonical form.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{imgs/truncation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Why the \ac{RDM} truncation is local.} (a) The canonical condition: a tensor
  contracted with its own conjugate over the physical index and one virtual index reduces to the
  identity on the remaining one. A tensor and its conjugate are drawn as the same shape in a lighter
  and a darker shade. (b) The reduced density matrix at a cut, with the left block open and the
  right block traced. In mixed canonical form the isometries either side of the orthogonality centre
  collapse by (a), so the eigenvalues of the whole object are the squared singular values of the
  single tensor $M$, and the optimal truncation never touches the rest of the chain.}
  \label{fig:truncation}
\end{figure}

Two features of this scheme matter later, because the transverse picture keeps one and loses the other. First, the truncated object is Hermitian and positive, so its eigenvalues are the squared Schmidt values and dropping the smallest is unambiguous. Second, the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem provides a variational guarantee and bounds the error. Both are properties of the Hermitian, single-state setting, and neither survives the move to a non-Hermitian transition matrix.

\subsection{The reduced transition matrix and its truncation}
\label{app:sub:rtm_truncation}

In the transverse picture the transfer matrix is non-Hermitian, so its dominant left and right fixed points are distinct states, and the object to truncate is the \ac{RTM} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rtm}, built from both. Its eigenvalues can be complex, so the Schmidt interpretation above does not carry over. Figure~\ref{fig:rdm_rtm} contrasts the reduced density matrix with the transition matrix at the same temporal cut.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{imgs/rdm_rtm.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The two candidate objects at a temporal cut.} Sites after the cut are traced over, so their
  physical legs are contracted between the two rows; the legs before the cut stay open and carry the
  indices of the resulting matrix. (a) The reduced density matrix of a single temporal boundary pairs
  $\ket{L}$ with its own conjugate, and is Hermitian and positive. (b) The \ac{RTM} instead pairs the
  two distinct fixed points $\ket{R}$ and $\bra{L}$, so it is not Hermitian and its eigenvalues need
  not be real or positive.}
  \label{fig:rdm_rtm}
\end{figure}

This is where the gauge argument becomes decisive, and it is the derivation promised in Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}. Insert a gauge transformation $X$ on the cut bond and the two candidate objects respond in opposite ways. The boundary RDM transforms by a congruence, $\rho_L\to X^\dagger\rho_L X$, which for the non-unitary $X$ generic here distorts its spectrum. The transition matrix transforms by a similarity, $\mathcal{T}_t\to X^{-1}\mathcal{T}_t X$, which leaves its eigenvalues untouched. Figure~\ref{fig:gauge} shows the difference at the level of the network. The Schmidt values of a single temporal boundary are therefore not physical, while the RTM spectrum is, and truncating on the former can discard exactly the weight the overlap needs.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{imgs/gauge.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Why the \ac{RTM} spectrum is the gauge-invariant one.} (a) A pair $X^{-1}X$ may be inserted
  on every bond at no cost: the two factors cancel, so the contraction equals the ungauged one and
  the Loschmidt echo is untouched, while the individual tensors change. In (b) and (c) only the
  lower time site is contracted; the upper one carries the open indices, so each open index keeps
  one factor and the contracted bond receives both. (b) The \ac{RDM} pairs $\ket{R}$ with its own
  conjugate, the factors meet as $XX^{\dagger}\neq\mathbb{1}$, and the spectrum moves under the
  congruence $X\rho X^{\dagger}$. (c) The \ac{RTM} pairs $\ket{R}$ with $\bra{L}$, the factors meet
  as $XX^{-1}=\mathbb{1}$, and the similarity $X\mathcal{T}_t X^{-1}$ leaves the spectrum unchanged.}
  \label{fig:gauge}
\end{figure}

The canonical form still helps, but in a generalized version, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rtm_truncation}: the condition of Figure~\ref{fig:truncation}(a) is imposed on the pair $\bra{L},\ket{R}$ rather than on a state and its own conjugate. The tensors away from the cut then contract away exactly as before, and the singular values of the \ac{RTM} again follow from a local decomposition, so the two routes cost the same to evaluate and differ only in which object they compress.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{imgs/rtm_truncation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The \ac{RTM} counterpart of Figure~\ref{fig:truncation}.} (a) The generalized canonical
  condition: the contraction pairs a tensor of $\bra{L}$ with the corresponding tensor of $\ket{R}$,
  not with its own conjugate. (b) Imposed along the chain, this condition makes everything before
  the cut contract away, so the singular values of the \ac{RTM} are those of the two tensors at the
  cut alone. The construction is as local as the \ac{RDM} one; what it loses is the variational
  guarantee, since the object is no longer Hermitian.}
  \label{fig:rtm_truncation}
\end{figure}

The logic of truncation changes accordingly. In the ordinary case one keeps the directions that best preserve the norm of a state. Here the relevant quantities are bilinear matrix elements $\bra{L}\mathcal O\ket{R}$. A component of $\ket R$ with little overlap with $\bra L$ contributes little, however large it looks from $\ket R$ alone, and a component with small weight in $\ket R$ can still matter if its projection onto $\bra L$ is large. The scheme of Ref.~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024} therefore performs a biorthogonal decomposition of $\ket R\bra L$ and keeps the eigenvalues of largest modulus, which are the directions that dominate the physical contraction. Because relevance is measured through the overlap, this can retain fewer states than an RDM truncation at the same accuracy.

The stability caveat of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation} can now be stated sharply. The singular values of $\mathcal{T}_t$ and its eigenvalues are different quantities, and it is the eigenvalues that enter the contraction, since the trace is their sum. A component with a large singular value can therefore still fail to contribute: an operator with eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$ has both at unit modulus, so an SVD-based truncation keeps both, yet they cancel exactly in the trace. Truncating the RTM on singular values is thus a motivated heuristic rather than a bound.

A second approximation enters through the environment. The truncation at each step uses the local environment available at that point of the sweep, not the full remaining network, because the contraction is built column by column. For a translation-invariant problem this is benign, since every bulk column is the same operator and weight discarded too early is regenerated by the next application. It remains an approximation, and it is one reason the RDM route behaves more predictably when the RTM output turns noisy.

\section{Conformal-field-theory derivations}
\label{app:cft}

This appendix derives the three results quoted without proof in Section~\ref{sec:cft}: the chord form of the two-point function, the transfer-matrix spectrum, and the entropy coefficients. For everything else we refer to Ref.~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}.

\subsection{Conformal maps and the two-point function}
\label{app:cft:maps}

In two dimensions the Euclidean coordinates are packaged into $z=x+i\tau$ and $\bar z=x-i\tau$, and physical quantities factorize into a holomorphic piece depending on $z$ and an antiholomorphic one depending on $\bar z$, which is where the separate weights $h$ and $\bar h$ come from. Since every analytic map $z\mapsto f(z)$ is a conformal symmetry, a correlation function known on the plane is known on any geometry such a map reaches, simply by substitution.

A primary operator $\phi$ of weights $(h,\bar h)$ has on the plane the two-point function
\begin{equation}
    \langle \phi(z_1)\phi(z_2)\rangle
      = \frac{1}{(z_1-z_2)^{2h}\,(\bar z_1-\bar z_2)^{2\bar h}},
    \label{eq:cft:plane2pt}
\end{equation}
whose exponent is the scaling dimension $x=h+\bar h$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:dim}, and under a map $\omega=f(z)$ it picks up the Jacobian factors $\prod_i f'(z_i)^{-h}\bar f'(\bar z_i)^{-\bar h}$. The map used throughout is the plane-to-cylinder logarithm $\omega=\tfrac{\beta}{2\pi}\log z$, which wraps the plane onto a cylinder of circumference $\beta$ and turns Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:plane2pt} into
\begin{equation}
    \langle\phi(\omega_1)\phi(\omega_2)\rangle \;\propto\;
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\omega_1-\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2h}
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\bar\omega_1-\bar\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2\bar h}.
    \label{eq:cft:cyl2pt}
\end{equation}
Continuing to real time, with $\beta\to iT$ and $\sinh\to\sin$, produces the strip geometry of the quench, and the time-like version of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:cyl2pt} is the origin of the chord variable of Eq.~\eqref{eq:chord}.

\subsection{The transfer-matrix spectrum from the stress tensor}
\label{app:cft:spectrum}

The central charge is carried by the stress-energy tensor, whose modes generate the Virasoro algebra. One mode is special: $L_0$ generates scaling, and its eigenvalues on the primaries are their weights, so its spectrum is the operator content.

Quantizing on a strip of transverse size $\beta$, the generator of translations along the strip is an integral of the stress tensor, up to the velocity $v$. The conformal map from the plane leaves behind one extra, purely geometric term, the Casimir anomaly proportional to $c$, which gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir}. Since the transfer matrix is essentially $\mathcal{E}=e^{-\delta t\,\hat H}$, this fixes its whole spectrum in the form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer}: a dominant eigenvalue carrying the Casimir term, and a tower of subleading ones whose ratios and phases carry the operator dimensions.

\subsection{Twist fields and the entropy coefficients}
\label{app:cft:twist}

The entanglement entropy of an interval $\mathcal A$ follows from the R\'enyi moments $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}$, continued to $n\to1$. The replica trick~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004} computes those moments as a path integral on $n$ copies of the plane sewn cyclically along $\mathcal A$, which is equivalent to inserting a pair of twist operators at the endpoints of $\mathcal A$.

The twist operator is a primary of dimension $\Delta_n=\tfrac{c}{12}(n-1/n)$, so its two-point function is a power law of the form~\eqref{eq:cft:plane2pt}. This gives $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}\sim\ell^{-4\Delta_n}$ for an interval of length $\ell$, and hence the logarithm
\begin{equation}
    S_n = \frac{c}{6}\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)\log\ell + \text{const},
    \qquad
    S_{n\to1} = \frac{c}{3}\log\ell + \text{const}.
    \label{eq:cft:cc}
\end{equation}
For a boundary cut, which is the temporal situation of this thesis, the coefficient is halved, giving $\tfrac{c}{12}\tfrac{n+1}{n}$: that is $c/6$ at $n\to1$ and $c/8$ at $n=2$, rather than the bulk value $c/3$.

\section{Construction of the time-evolution MPO}
\label{app:mpo}

This appendix records the construction details behind Section~\ref{sec:mporep}: the standard route and why it fails here, the schemes that exponentiate the \ac{FSM} blocks directly, the closed-form second-order tensor used in production, and the measurement that justifies it.

\subsection{Standard time-evolution algorithms}
\label{app:mpo:standard}

The conventional way of building a time-evolution operator as a tensor network is the Trotter--Suzuki decomposition~\cite{vidal2004, schollwock2011}. The evolution is split into short steps, and each step $e^{-iH\delta t}$ is factorized into two-site gates by separating the Hamiltonian into terms acting on even and on odd bonds. Within each group the terms commute, so each group exponentiates exactly, and the error comes only from the non-commutation between groups: $\mathcal{O}(\delta t^2)$ per step for the first-order splitting, $\mathcal{O}(\delta t^3)$ for the symmetric second-order one. Splitting each gate by an SVD and regrouping the factors site by site turns a gate layer into a uniform MPO, which is exactly the translation invariance the transverse contraction needs. Ref.~\cite{paeckel2019} reviews these and other time-evolution schemes.

The construction fails as soon as an interaction connects sites that are not adjacent, since such a term does not act on a single bond of the brick-wall pattern and cannot be written as one of its gates. That is the situation here, and the reason for the \ac{FSM} route.

\subsection{The auxiliary-boson trick and the time-step split}
\label{app:mpo:boson}

The simplest construction on the overlap logic of Section~\ref{sec:mporep:exp} is $W^{\mathrm{I}}$, which keeps every disconnected term and discards all overlaps. It distributes the time step across the initiators $C$, the terminators $B$ and the on-site block $D$, keeps the virtual bond at $1+\chi$, and is first order. Improving it to $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ means additionally keeping the single-site overlaps, and the overlaps need care: $H^2$ sums over every ordered pair, so both orderings $CD$ and $DC$ of an initiator and a field on the same site must be kept, or the operator loses the Hermiticity it inherits from $H$.

Writing out the correct symmetric combinations by hand is awkward, and Zaletel et al.~\cite{zaletel2015} avoid it with an auxiliary-space device. The four blocks are encoded in the dynamics of two hard-core bosons; a small effective Hamiltonian built from $A,B,C,D$ acting on those bosons is exponentiated, and the new MPO blocks are read off by projecting onto the boson occupations. Since a hard-core boson can be occupied at most once, the exponential truncates by itself and produces exactly the required symmetric cross terms. The Van Damme construction~\cite{vandamme2024} generalizes this idea to arbitrary order.

All these schemes share the time-step split, which is what keeps them stable. The complex step $\tau=-i\delta t$ is divided unevenly: a real factor $t_C=\sqrt{\delta t}$ goes to the initiator block and the phase $t_B=\tau/t_C$ to the terminator block, so the product $C\otimes B$ still carries the correct factor while the intermediate memory channels stay well behaved.

\subsection{The second-order VD2 blocks}
\label{app:mpo:vd2}

We record the explicit local tensor of the second-order MPO (VD2) used throughout this work. These are the closed-form expressions of Van Damme et al.~\cite{vandamme2024}, specialized to our \ac{FSM} blocks; they are what the exponentiation routine assembles for a given memory dimension $\chi$.

We start from the four blocks $A,B,C,D$ of the Hamiltonian MPO $W_H$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:Wannni}): $D$ is the on-site term, $C$ initiates an interaction, $B$ terminates one, and $A$ propagates it. Following the time-step split above, we scale them as
\begin{equation}
  \tilde D = \tau D, \qquad \tilde C = t_C\, C, \qquad \tilde B = t_B\, B, \qquad \tilde A = A,
  \qquad t_C = \sqrt{\delta t},\ \ t_B = \tau/t_C,\ \ \tau=-i\delta t .
\end{equation}
The construction organizes the exponential $e^{\tau W_H}$ not by powers of $W_H$, but by the symmetric products of overlapping blocks that its Taylor series generates. We write $\{X_1\dots X_m\}$ for the symmetrized product, i.e.\ the sum over all distinct orderings of the operator-valued blocks (for example $\{XY\}=X\otimes Y + Y\otimes X$, and $\{XXY\}=XXY+XYX+YXX$), where $\otimes$ is the Kronecker product on the virtual memory index and the ordinary product on the physical index. The multinomial weights $1/2!,\,1/3!,\dots$ of the exponential are carried by the explicit prefactors below.

The resulting local tensor is a $3\times3$ block object (indices $1,2,3$ label the scalar, single- and doubled-memory sectors, which is the origin of the enlarged $1+\chi+\chi^2$ virtual bond),
\begin{equation}
  W^{\mathrm{VD2}} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    W_{11} & W_{12} & W_{13} \\
    W_{21} & W_{22} & W_{23} \\
    W_{31} & W_{32} & W_{33}
  \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
  W_{11} &= \mathbbm{1} + \tilde D + \tfrac12\{\tilde D\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde D\tilde D\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{12} &= \tilde C + \tfrac12\{\tilde C\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde C\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{13} = \{\tilde C\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{21} &= \tilde B + \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{31} = \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{22} &= \tilde A + \tfrac12\big(\{\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\}\big)
            + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde C\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\tilde D\}\big), \\
  W_{23} &= \{\tilde A\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde C\tilde D\}+\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde B\}\big), \qquad
  W_{32} = \tfrac12\{\tilde A\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde C\}\big), \\
  W_{33} &= \{\tilde A\tilde A\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde A\tilde D\}\big).
\end{align}
The scalar track $W_{11}$ carries the on-site evolution to third Taylor order; the $W_{12},W_{13}$ entries dress an outgoing memory leg (single and doubled), $W_{21},W_{31}$ the incoming one, and the lower-right block dresses the through-wire $\tilde A$ with all of its symmetric companions. Keeping the doubled-leg (index-3) sector is exactly what reproduces the connected clusters spanning three sites---the next-nearest-neighbour overlaps that $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ misses---and is why VD2 is genuinely second order for our model at the cost of the enlarged virtual bond.

\subsection{Comparing the two constructions in practice}
\label{app:mpo:comparison}

The claim that $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ degrades to first order for a next-nearest-neighbour Hamiltonian, while VD2 remains second order, can be checked directly on the model of this thesis. A convenient diagnostic is the loss of unitarity, since the exact $e^{-iH\delta t}$ preserves the norm of any state: applying successive layers of the approximate operator to $\ket{X+}^{\otimes N}$ and monitoring $\lVert\psi\rVert$ isolates the Trotter error, because a perfect construction would leave the norm at one.

Applying eight layers at $\delta t=0.05$ to a chain of $N=100$ sites at $p=0.1$ gives the following norms:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
 & $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ & $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ & VD2 \\
\hline
virtual bond & $4$ & $4$ & $13$ \\
$\lVert\psi\rVert$ after 1 layer & $1.3514$ & $1.1341$ & $0.99995$ \\
$\lVert\psi\rVert$ after 8 layers & $12.6146$ & $3.0181$ & $0.99923$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The separation is unambiguous. The first-order $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ inflates the norm by an order of magnitude within eight steps, and $W^{\mathrm{II}}$, which would be effectively second order for a strictly \ac{NN} chain, still triples it: the connected clusters generated by the \ac{NNN} bridge are precisely the ones it fails to capture. VD2 holds the norm to within $8\times10^{-4}$ over the same interval. The same ordering appears in the Schr\"odinger-picture benchmark of the notebooks, where VD2 reproduces the TDVP evolution at every time while $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ agrees only at short times before drifting.

This is what justifies paying for the larger virtual bond. Since the transverse contraction needs long temporal chains, and reducing $\delta t$ to compensate a first-order error would lengthen them further and add truncation error at every extra site, a genuinely second-order operator at a moderate time step is the configuration that makes the calculation feasible at all.

\section{Implementation details}
\label{app:blockpm}

This appendix documents the implementation of the block power method of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm} for a reader who wants to know how it works internally or to run it. The core routine is \texttt{block\_transfer\_eigs}, built on top of \texttt{ITransverse.jl}~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}; all code, notebooks and cluster drivers are in the public repository of Ref.~\cite{marquezolguin2026code}. The subsections cover, in order: the numerical solve of the projected pencil, the eigenvalue-only mode, the validation against exact diagonalization, the cost profile, the options that control a production run, and the classifier that assigns the block members.

\subsection{The regularized solve and the left pairing}
\label{app:blockpm:pairing}

Near the degeneracy the columns of the right block begin to coincide and the metric $S$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_pencil} becomes nearly singular, so the pencil $Mv=\theta\,Sv$ is not handed to a generalized eigensolver, which would return infinite or wildly scattered values. It is regularized with the pseudoinverse instead,
\begin{equation}
  W = S^{+} M, \qquad W = V\,\Theta\,V^{-1},
  \tag{D1}
\end{equation}
where $S^{+}$ carries a relative tolerance of $10^{-12}$: directions that have effectively collapsed onto each other are projected out rather than inverted. The eigenvalues $\Theta$, sorted by modulus, estimate $\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{k-1}$, and the columns of $V$ give the right mixing coefficients.

The left block needs its own coefficients, and the obvious route hides a trap. Diagonalizing the transposed pencil in a second call gives no guarantee that its eigenvalues come out in the same order, and inside a quasi-degenerate cluster, matching each left eigenvalue to the nearest right one is ambiguous exactly when it matters: a greedy match picks the wrong partner, the paired left and right vectors stop describing the same eigenvector, and the block oscillates instead of converging. No second diagonalization is needed. Writing $y^{\mathsf T}=u^{\mathsf T}S$, the left problem becomes $y^{\mathsf T}W=\theta\,y^{\mathsf T}$, so the $y$ are the rows of $V^{-1}$ and
\begin{equation}
  u_j = \big(S^{+}\big)^{\mathsf T}\big(V^{-1}\big)^{\mathsf T} e_j ,
  \tag{D2}
\end{equation}
paired with $\theta_j$ by construction and biorthogonal through the metric, $u_i^{\mathsf T}S\,v_j=\delta_{ij}$.

One implementation note concerns the de-mixing. The MPS linear combinations of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm} are formed by exact direct-sum addition and compressed once at the end, because the default density-matrix addition is fragile for the near-parallel vectors that a tight cluster produces.

\subsection{The eigenvalue-only mode}
\label{app:blockpm:schur}

The eigenvalue-only mode of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm} replaces the de-mixing coefficients by orthonormal ones. After the modulus sorting, the coefficient matrices are QR-decomposed,
\begin{equation}
  V \to Q_V, \qquad U \to Q_U, \qquad Q^\dagger Q = \mathbb{1},
  \tag{D3}
\end{equation}
and the blocks are rotated with $Q_V$ and $Q_U$ instead of $V$ and $U$. The leading $j$ columns of each $Q$ span the same leading subspaces as those of the sorted coefficient matrices, so this is an ordered-Schur-like choice, and the Ritz values are unchanged for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:blockpm}. What the rotation does change is the conditioning of what is truncated next: $\mathrm{cond}(V)$ grows like the inverse of the gap inside the block, while $Q_V$ and $Q_U$ are unitary by construction. The two modes can therefore drift apart only through the truncation, and in practice they barely do. Two independent ladders at $p=0.1$ and $\chi=64$, one carried in each basis, agree on the two tracked eigenvalues to between $10^{-7}$ and $10^{-4}$ in relative modulus over $T=2$ to $8$; the untracked members agree only to $10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$, which is expected since the convergence test never monitors them.

Three safeguards complete the iteration in either mode. Every block member is renormalized after each de-mixing, and a member whose norm underflows or overflows is replaced by a fresh random vector. The condition number of $S$ is monitored: a large value means two block directions have effectively merged, and the last member is then replaced by a random vector bi-orthogonalized against the rest with the bilinear form. Convergence is measured on the leading Ritz values (the leading two in production) after matching each value to its nearest predecessor from the previous iteration, since the frequent modulus-order swaps between near-degenerate eigenvalues would otherwise register as spurious jumps; the iteration stops when the matched movement falls below $10^{-6}$, or when it has stopped improving for a set number of iterations.

\subsection{Validation against exact diagonalization}
\label{app:blockpm:validation}

At evolution times short enough that the rotated transfer operator stays below a few thousand dimensions, $\mathcal{E}$ can be contracted into a dense matrix and diagonalized exactly. This tests the whole pipeline, builder, rotation, block iteration and pencil, against a reference with no tensor-network approximation in it. Table~\ref{tab:blockpm_validation} shows the outcome at $k=4$ for three operators: the block method reproduces the exact leading pair to between $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-13}$.

  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \hline
    operator & dense dimension & exact $|\mu_0|,\,|\mu_1|$ & error on the leading pair \\
    \hline

\subsection{Computational cost}
\label{app:blockpm:cost}

One iteration of the block method contains three kinds of tensor-network work. The application step performs $2k$ \ac{tMPO}--\ac{tMPS} products, each growing the temporal bond from $\chi$ to $D\chi$ before compression. The projection step evaluates the $k^2$ bilinear overlaps of $S$ and $M$; no state grows there, and the $k\times k$ dense algebra is negligible. The de-mixing step forms $2k$ linear combinations of $k$ states each. Every combination is built as an exact direct sum, which raises the bond dimension to $k\chi$ before the compression sweep, so both the memory peak of an iteration and most of its work sit in this step. Profiling a production rung ($\chi=64$, $k=4$, $p=0.1$) attributes roughly $85\%$ of the iteration time to the de-mixing sums and their compression sweeps.

Two adjustments reduce this cost without changing what the method converges to. The running direct sum is compressed progressively, each partial sum truncated back to the production bond dimension, with the final addition left exact. And the stagnation patience is shortened, since at late rungs the iteration spends long plateaus below the convergence threshold before the stop triggers. Together the two halved the wall-clock time of a $T=18$ rung, from $9500$ to $4900$ seconds, while moving $|\mu_0|$ by $4\times10^{-6}$ relative. The mechanisms that reduce the number of iterations, rather than the cost of each one, are described next.

\subsection{Production options}
\label{app:library:build}
\label{app:library:cluster}
\label{app:library:adaptive}

A thin, model-agnostic builder sits above the solver. It assembles the one-step propagator as an MPO (Appendix~\ref{app:mpo}), performs the $90^\circ$ rotation that maps the spatial virtual bond onto the temporal physical index, and returns the transfer-matrix MPO with a seed tMPS carrying the right index skeleton. The seed is always overwritten with random complex tensors before the iteration, because a symmetry-special seed converges to a subdominant sector. The temporal physical dimension is read from the operator rather than hard-coded, which is what lets the same code path serve the $W^{\mathrm{I}}$, $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ and VD2 kernels and every model without modification.

The options that control a production run are the following; none changes what the method converges to.

\textit{Warm starting.} Along a ladder of evolution times, the converged block at one time is re-indexed onto the longer time-site set of the next and padded with a small random tail, so the iteration only has to relax the newly added sites. The bond cap and the cutoff follow a schedule that is loose early and tight near convergence: the cap grows in steps of two up to $\chi$, and the cutoff holds at $10^{-8}$ for the first forty iterations before tightening to $10^{-10}$. This is more than a speed-up: near a degeneracy a cold random restart has nothing biasing it onto the physical branch, so warm continuity is also what keeps the tracker on the correct eigenvector family as the spectrum tightens.

\textit{Block-size escalation.} At stronger frustration the leading $k=4$ block can fill with members of a single near-degenerate cluster, pushing the subleading eigenvalue needed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq4} outside the captured window. The driver detects this, since the tower gap of the classifier below returns no value when no tower member was captured, and escalates the block in steps ($k=4\to6\to8$), warm-seeding each larger solve with the converged smaller block, so the extra cost is paid only where the spectrum demands it.

\textit{Truncation scheme.} The truncated object is selectable between \ac{RTM} and \ac{RDM} (Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}), with RTM the production default and RDM the well-conditioned fallback.

\textit{Crash safety.} Each $(p,T)$ point is written to a per-label cache as soon as it converges, keyed by its parameters, so an interrupted run loses at most the point in flight and a completed point is never recomputed. A separate checkpoint stores the last converged block, so a resubmitted run resumes warm.

\subsection{The branch classifier}
\label{app:selector}

The classifier assigns the block members relative to a candidate $\mu_0$. Writing $\mathrm{phase}(z)=\arg(-z)$ for the convention of Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}, it splits the block into a tower and a set of partners: the tower is every eigenvalue within $\pi/2$ in phase, the conformal descendants of $\mu_0$, and the partners are the momentum-$\pi$ displaced states of Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}. The tower gap is $|\mu_1|/|\mu_0|$ computed from the largest-modulus tower member only, never a partner, and it returns no value when the block holds no tower member, which is the trigger for the escalation above.

The selection rules themselves---modulus dominance with a continuity tie-break, the flagging of suspect rungs, and the five-per-cent repair window---are stated in Section~\ref{sec:numerics:practical}; here we record what a nearest-value rule does wrong, since it is the natural first attempt. The physical branch rotates between rungs by an amount that grows with the velocity and hence with $p$, while its displaced partners of similar modulus stand still in phase, so a nearest-value rule latches onto a partner and, re-anchoring on its own pick, corrupts the ladder that follows. At $p=0.5$ this manifests as the tracked $|\mu_0|$ collapsing at one rung and following partners ever after: output that looks plausible but is an artifact of the selector.

The phase-predicted tracking that takes over past the modulus crossing deserves three technical notes. First, a crossing does not lose the branch: the physical eigenvalue survives inside the block as a subleading member, and where an independently tracked ladder exists for comparison the phases agree to four decimals. Second, the prediction must be seeded, because the phase advance per rung grows with the velocity and can exceed $\pi$, making the wrapped step ambiguous; the first step is chosen by testing every candidate advance against the next few rungs and keeping the most consistent one. Third, each accepted rung records the distance between the prediction and the matched phase, and the ladder is cut at the first rung where this distance jumps, which is where the block stops resolving the branch.

\section{The entanglement-barrier wall}
\label{app:wall}

This appendix supports the diagnosis of Section~\ref{sec:results:wall}, where the temporal-entropy dome inflates at a well-defined time and stops being conformal. It has two parts: what an exceptional point is and why it spoils the eigenvectors while sparing the eigenvalues, and the controls that establish the wall as a property of the physics rather than a resolution limit.

\subsection{Exceptional points of a non-normal matrix}
\label{app:wall:ep}

Section~\ref{sec:reach} established how eigenvalues and eigenvectors respond differently as a gap inside the block closes. The limiting configuration is what this subsection adds. As two eigenvalues of a non-normal matrix approach each other, their left and right eigenvectors need not stay apart: they can rotate towards a common direction, so the pairing $\braket{L}{R}$ of the coalescing branch tends to zero even though neither vector vanishes. Where two eigenvalues and their eigenvectors merge at once the matrix is at an exceptional point: it is defective, has no complete eigenbasis, and the sensitivity of the individual eigenvector diverges.

This is the origin of the wall. The dome breaks not because the solver fails to converge, but because the object it is asked to converge to becomes ill-defined. The order parameter is the phase rigidity of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rigidity}, which equals one for a near-Hermitian pair and collapses to zero at the exceptional point, and its measured collapse lands exactly where the dome inflates, which is what identifies the two as one event. Complex symmetry does not help, when a model has it: every matrix is similar to a complex-symmetric one, so symmetry can relabel the collapse but not forbid it, and inside an exactly degenerate subspace the individual eigenvector is undefined rather than merely ill-conditioned.

\subsection{What the broken dome looks like}
\label{app:wall:shape}

Section~\ref{sec:results:wall} reads the wall from the chord plot, where the profiles past it appear as a detached arc rather than as points scattered about the line. The shape has a simple origin, and it makes a sharper diagnostic than the height of the dome.

The chord variable of Eq.~\eqref{eq:chord} is not monotonic in the cut position. It grows as the cut moves in from either edge and is largest at mid-strip, so a profile plotted against $W$ is traversed twice: once for cuts in the first half, once for cuts in the second. A conformal profile depends on the cut only through $W$, which is what Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} states, so the two traversals give the same value at the same $W$ and land on top of one another. The dome then collapses onto a single straight line, and the fold is invisible. This is what makes the chord plot a test rather than a change of variables: the collapse only happens if the profile really is a function of $W$.

Past the wall that ceases to hold. The two halves of the profile no longer agree at equal $W$, the fold opens, and the two branches trace out the arc seen in Figure~\ref{fig:wall}. At $p=0.1$ the transition is abrupt: between $T=9$ and $T=10$ the peak of the dome jumps from $0.34$ to $0.72$ and a second maximum appears, so a single evolution step separates a profile that collapses cleanly from one that does not. The hump count and the failure to collapse are two views of the same failure, and either can be used to locate the wall without fitting anything.

We do not plot the broken profiles in the main text. Once the dome carries two maxima its height and its slope have no conformal meaning, so a figure of them would document the failure of the estimator rather than any property of the model. The rigidity of Figure~\ref{fig:wall} says the same thing in a quantity that remains well defined on both sides of the wall, which is why it is the diagnostic we report.

\subsection{The wall is gap-limited, not resolution-limited}
\label{app:wall:eps}

An exceptional point is a property of the transfer matrix, so its location must not move when we change how finely that matrix is represented. We checked this along three independent knobs, holding the physics fixed at $p=0.1$ and varying only the numerical resolution.

Halving the bond dimension from $\chi=64$ to $32$ leaves the onset of the inflation at the same time, so the wall is not a matter of keeping too few states. Tightening the truncation cutoff by orders of magnitude leaves it unchanged as well. Switching the truncated object itself, from the cheap RTM scheme to the Hermitian RDM one, also moves nothing: the RDM run breaks at the same time, at several times the cost. Three qualitatively different refinements leaving the onset fixed is what pins the wall to the spectral gap rather than to any shortfall of the representation. Phase rigidity supports the same reading, since it is a Rayleigh-quotient-like quantity that stays accurate even from a cold start.

The regulator $\beta_0$ is a fourth knob, and the most promising one, because the modulus gaps grow linearly with it and it is those gaps closing that ends the route. The measurement behind the statement in Section~\ref{sec:results:wall} is the following. Across $\beta_0=0.1$ to $0.8$ the rate at which the rigidity collapses is unchanged, the ratio $r(T{=}6)/r(T{=}2)$ staying between $7.35$ and $7.40\times10^{-3}$ over the whole range, so the curves are parallel and the wall sits at the same time. The rigidity itself is in fact lower at larger $\beta_0$, by about $40\%$ from one end of that range to the other, so widening the regulator makes the conditioning slightly worse rather than better.

These controls also settle which truncation to use in production. Where both converge, RTM and RDM agree on every eigenvalue and on the rigidity to four to five digits, so they agree on the physics, and RDM is simply slower for the same answer, by factors that grow with $T$ up to an order of magnitude. RTM is therefore the production choice, with RDM kept as the well-conditioned fallback for the rare points where RTM output turns noisy before the wall.

\section{The momentum-\texorpdfstring{$\pi$}{pi} displaced boundary tower}
\label{app:pairing}

This appendix derives the effect behind the boundary-exponent result of Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}: why the odd-parity boundary tower is displaced in phase by momentum $\approx\pi$ for any $p\neq0$, and why free-fermion models never show it. In short, the next-nearest-neighbour coupling is a four-fermion interaction of a particular kind, and only such a term can attach a momentum-$\pi$ phase to the odd-parity sector.

\subsection{Jordan--Wigner and the four-fermion interaction}
\label{app:pairing:jw}

Spins are algebraically awkward, since two Pauli operators anticommute on the same site but commute on different ones, so a spin is neither a boson nor a fermion. The Jordan--Wigner transformation rewrites the chain exactly as fermions at the cost of a non-local string. In the convention matched to our model,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^x_j = 1 - 2n_j, \qquad
    \sigma^z_j = (c_j + c_j^\dagger)\,K_j, \qquad
    K_j = \prod_{k<j}(1-2n_k),
    \tag{F1}
\end{equation}
with $n_j=c_j^\dagger c_j$, and the string $K_j$ supplying the signs that make the $c$ anticommute.

Using $(c+c^\dagger)(1-2n)=c^\dagger-c$ and $K_j^2=1$, the terms fermionize as follows. The transverse field becomes a chemical potential. The \ac{NN} Ising bond telescopes its strings away and leaves a quadratic hopping-and-pairing term,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^z_j\sigma^z_{j+1} = (c_j^\dagger-c_j)(c_{j+1}+c_{j+1}^\dagger).
    \tag{F2}
\end{equation}
Those two terms make up the pure Ising chain, which is why it is a free-fermion theory, exactly solvable, flowing to a free Majorana CFT.

The two extra terms of the ANNNI-type model are both quartic. The self-dual $XX$ term is a plain density--density interaction, diagonal in the occupation basis, which moves nothing. The next-nearest-neighbour bond is the interesting one: its strings no longer cancel, and it becomes a parity-dressed hop,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^z_i\sigma^z_{i+2} = (c_i^\dagger-c_i)\,\underbrace{(1-2n_{i+1})}_{(-1)^{n_{i+1}}}\,(c_{i+2}+c_{i+2}^\dagger),
    \tag{F3}
\end{equation}
a hop from $i$ to $i+2$ whose sign is set by the occupation of the site skipped over. This single term is the origin of the effect.

\subsection{Why free fermions cannot displace, and the quantization at \texorpdfstring{$\pi$}{pi}}
\label{app:pairing:kw}

A free-fermion transfer matrix has the normal form $\mathcal{E}=\mu_0\exp(-\sum_m \varepsilon_m \hat n_m)$: independent modes whose moduli and phases are both additive over occupations. An eigenvalue near the top can only occupy modes with $\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon_m\approx0$, and in an Ising-class critical chain those are the long-wavelength modes, whose phases $\pi x/(vT)$ are small. Building a phase of exactly $\pi$ at full modulus would need a mode that neither decays nor stops oscillating, that is a gapless branch at lattice momentum $\pi$, which the uniform Ising-class free chain does not have. A free chain therefore cannot produce a displaced tower. This is what the free-fermion controls show: the critical Ising chain at two evolution times, and the anisotropic XY chain at $\gamma=0.5,\,1,\,1.5$, return no displaced state at all.

What the argument requires is a source of momentum-$\pi$ structure, and in this model that source is an interaction. Single-term probes identify which one. Deforming the critical Ising chain by the \ac{NNN} term alone, at strength $0.1$ and $T=4$, produces a displaced family at $x\approx0.37,\,1.22,\,2.17$; deforming it by the $\sigma^x\sigma^x$ term alone produces none. It is therefore the parity-dressed hop of Eq.~(F3), not the density--density term, that carries the displacement. Scanning down to small couplings, the displaced family is present at every $p\neq0$ tested, down to $p=0.01$, with no threshold: the effect switches on with the interaction rather than at some finite strength.

One consequence of the single-term probe is worth recording. The \ac{NNN}-only deformation is not self-dual, and it displaces all the same. Self-duality is therefore not needed for the displacement to exist; the argument below concerns why it is pinned at exactly $\pi$ rather than at a coupling-dependent value.

The quantization at exactly $\pi$, rather than at a coupling-dependent value, has a natural home in the self-duality of the model. In Majorana variables the $XX$ and next-nearest $ZZ$ terms are both products of four consecutive Majoranas, offset by one Majorana site. That one-site shift is the lattice Kramers--Wannier duality exchanging order and disorder, and it maps the two terms onto each other, which is why a self-dual Hamiltonian must carry both. At the self-dual point the network admits a half-step transfer operation with $\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{1/2}^{2}$, so a sector whose half-step eigenvalue carries a factor $\pm i$ lands after squaring at phase exactly $\pi$. This is a discrete index rather than a tunable phase, consistent with the displacement being identical across the couplings probed. Which sector carries the index is not settled from first principles, and we note it in the outlook. What the main text needs is established here: the displacement exists for every $p\neq0$, is absent for free fermions, and is pinned at $\pi$.

\subsection{The sound velocity from the equilibrium dispersion}
\label{app:velocity}

Every temporal formula that turns a transfer-matrix phase into a scaling dimension carries the sound velocity $v(p)$, so it has to be known independently. We take it from equilibrium, using Alcaraz's dispersion relation for this chain: the periodic ring Hamiltonian is diagonalized exactly at small size and the velocity is read off the lowest excitation carrying momentum.

The construction is validated in four ways. It recovers $v=2$ exactly at $p=0$. The from-scratch ring Hamiltonian matches the operator used elsewhere in the code to machine precision. The sector-resolved and sector-blind reads agree. And, decisively, feeding our measured $v(p)$ into Alcaraz's separate Casimir formula reproduces his published ground-state central charge $c(p)\approx\tfrac12$. The measured values grow steadily with frustration,
\begin{equation}
    v(0)=1.98,\quad v(0.1)=2.60,\quad v(0.2)=3.21,\quad
    v(0.3)=3.80,\quad v(0.4)=4.37,\quad v(0.5)=4.93,
    \tag{G1}
\end{equation}
and it is these, rather than a fixed $v=2$, that make the boundary tower of Section~\ref{sec:results:tower} read as clean half-integers at every $p$. Figure~\ref{fig:velocity} shows them against $p$.

\subsection{The branch constant in the eigenvalue central-charge fit}
\label{app:eq3}

The eigenvalue route to $c$ has a subtlety that ruins the fit if ignored. We work with $\lambda_0=\log(-\mu_0)$, following Ref.~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}, and since $\log(-\mu_0)=\log(\mu_0)+i\pi$ the imaginary part carries a constant $-\pi$ branch offset that the bare fit form omits. Leaving it in dumps a spurious $-\pi/T$ contribution into the $1/T^2$ Casimir slot and returns a central charge of order $10^3$.

The corrected procedure fits the four-term form $\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0 = A\,T + B + C/T + D/T^3$ on the unwrapped phase and reads $c=24\,v\,|C|/\pi$. We leave $B$ free rather than pinning it at $-\pi$. On a short window $B$ and $C$ trade off against each other, and pinning hides that trade-off instead of removing it: the fit then returns a Casimir coefficient whose error has nowhere to go. Left free, $B$ becomes the diagnostic. Where the window is long enough to separate the two, the fit recovers $B=-3.141$ on its own and the central charge can be read absolutely; where it is not, $B$ drifts off $-\pi$ and the window is not yet trustworthy. This is what limits the absolute read at $p\neq0$, where the branch can be followed over fewer rungs.
