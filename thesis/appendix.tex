\section{Conformal-field-theory derivations}
\label{app:cft}

This appendix summarises the standard \ac{CFT} results used in Section~\ref{sec:cft}: the chord form of the two-point function, the strip quantisation underlying the transfer-matrix spectrum, and the entropy coefficients. Full derivations are given in Refs.~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004,difrancesco1997}; here we focus on their application to the present calculation.

\subsection{Conformal maps, the two-point function and the entropy coefficients}
\label{app:cft:maps}

In two dimensions, the Euclidean coordinates are written as $z=x+i\tau$ and $\bar z=x-i\tau$. A primary operator $\phi$ has conformal weights $(h,\bar h)$, which determine its transformation under the two chiral sectors. Their sum,
\begin{equation}
    x = h + \bar h
    \label{eq:cft:dim}
\end{equation}
is the scaling dimension used in the main text. On the plane, the two-point function has the power-law form
\begin{equation}
    \langle \phi(z_1)\phi(z_2)\rangle
      = \frac{1}{(z_1-z_2)^{2h}\,(\bar z_1-\bar z_2)^{2\bar h}}.
    \label{eq:cft:plane2pt}
\end{equation}
The transformation law of a primary field then determines the correlator on conformally related geometries. Under the logarithmic map from the plane to a cylinder of circumference $\beta$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:plane2pt} becomes~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004,difrancesco1997}
\begin{equation}
    \langle\phi(\omega_1)\phi(\omega_2)\rangle \;\propto\;
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\omega_1-\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2h}
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\bar\omega_1-\bar\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2\bar h}.
    \label{eq:cft:cyl2pt}
\end{equation}
Analytic continuation to real time, $\beta\to iT$, replaces the hyperbolic sine by a sine and produces the strip geometry relevant to the quench. The resulting time-dependent correlator gives the chord variable in Eq.~\eqref{eq:chord}.

The entropy coefficients follow from the same two-point function. The R\'enyi entropy of an interval $\mathcal A$ is obtained from the moments $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}$, with the von Neumann entropy recovered by analytic continuation to $n\to1$. In the replica construction, each endpoint of $\mathcal A$ in the interior of the system introduces a twist operator with scaling dimension~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_n = \frac{c}{12}\Big(n - \frac{1}{n}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:twist}
\end{equation}
For a bulk interval, both endpoints lie in the interior. The moments therefore follow the two-point scaling $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}\sim\ell^{-4\Delta_n}$, which gives
\begin{equation}
    S_n = \frac{c}{6}\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)\log\ell + \text{const},
    \qquad
    S_{n\to1} = \frac{c}{3}\log\ell + \text{const}.
    \label{eq:cft:cc}
\end{equation}
The temporal bipartitions used in this thesis instead terminate at an edge of the strip and have only one endpoint in the interior. The replica construction then contains a single twist operator, reducing the logarithmic coefficient by a factor of two. The coefficient is therefore $\tfrac{c}{12}(1+\tfrac1n)$, which becomes $c/6$ as $n\to1$ and $c/8$ at $n=2$, rather than the bulk value $c/3$. Using the bulk coefficient would consequently overestimate the fitted central charge by a factor of two.




\subsection{The transfer-matrix spectrum from the stress tensor}
\label{app:cft:spectrum}

The stress-energy tensor determines the response of the theory to coordinate transformations. In two dimensions, its modes $L_n$ generate conformal transformations and satisfy the Virasoro algebra, whose central term is proportional to $c$~\cite{difrancesco1997}. The generator $L_0$ controls scale transformations, and its eigenvalues on primary operators are their conformal weights. Its spectrum therefore encodes the operator content of the theory.

Quantisation on a strip of transverse width $\beta$ makes the conformal anomaly explicit. The generator of translations along the strip is obtained by integrating the stress tensor. Under the conformal map from the plane, it acquires a geometric contribution proportional to $c$~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
    \hat H = \frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(L_0 - \frac{c}{24}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:casimir}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir} contains both forms of universal information. The constant $-c/24$ is the Casimir contribution and determines the central-charge term, while the spectrum of $L_0$ gives the scaling dimensions. Since the transfer matrix advances the network by one spatial column, $\mathcal{E}=e^{-a\hat H}$, where $a$ is the lattice spacing. Substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir} gives the spectrum in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer}, with $g=a/\beta$ and $a=1$ in lattice units. The dominant eigenvalue contains the Casimir term, whereas ratios and phase differences involving the subleading eigenvalues encode the operator dimensions.

The effective strip width contains both imaginary- and real-time contributions. The regulator supplies imaginary-time intervals at the two boundaries, the quench supplies the intervening real-time interval, and the velocity converts these durations into lengths:
\begin{equation}
    \beta = v\,(2\beta_0 + i\,T),
    \qquad
    \frac{1}{\beta} \;\simeq\; \frac{1}{v}\Big(\frac{2\beta_0}{T^2} - \frac{i}{T}\Big),
    \label{eq:cft:continuation}
\end{equation}
where the second expression is expanded to first order in $\beta_0/T$. Substitution into Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} gives
\begin{equation}
    \log\mu_i \;\simeq\; \frac{i\pi}{v\,T}\Big(x_i-\frac{c}{24}\Big)
      \;-\; \frac{2\pi\beta_0}{v\,T^2}\Big(x_i-\frac{c}{24}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:contexpand}
\end{equation}
The phases contain the universal data at order $1/T$, whereas the moduli depend on the regulator at order $\beta_0/T^2$. Setting $x_i=0$ for the identity gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3}, and subtracting two eigenvalues gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq4}. For small $\beta_0$, the moduli therefore vary weakly with time, producing the emergent dual unitarity tested in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Universal versus non-universal constants}
\label{app:universal}
\label{sec:numerics:universal}

The predictions of Section~\ref{sec:cft} apply in the continuum limit, whereas the simulations use a microscopic lattice at finite evolution time $T$, bond dimension $\chi$, Trotter step $\delta t$, and regulator $\beta_0$. The measured quantities therefore contain universal and non-universal contributions. The central charge $c$ and scaling dimensions $x_i$ are fixed by the universality class and determine the coefficients in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cft:chord}--\eqref{eq:gapclose}. By contrast, the regulator and associated entropy offset $s_0$, the Trotter step, the sound velocity $v$, and the overall transfer-matrix normalisation depend on the lattice construction or numerical method. Separating these contributions determines which conformal predictions can be tested directly.

The sound velocity is determined independently. We use the extrapolated value $v_\infty(p)$ from Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity} in all subsequent conformal expressions. Since the eigenvalue phases depend on $\pi x/(vT)$, this velocity converts each measured phase gap into a scaling dimension $x_i$.

Universal and non-universal contributions affect the spectrum differently. The overall normalisation of the transfer matrix is fixed by the lattice construction rather than the continuum theory. Each network column contains one Trotter layer and the cooling rows, so this scale depends on $\delta t$, $\beta_0$, and the normalisation convention for the local \ac{MPO} tensor. Its explicit form is unnecessary because it multiplies all eigenvalues equally, rescaling their moduli without changing their phases. We therefore extract scaling dimensions from phase differences and use the moduli only through ratios or asymptotic trends.

A ratio of phase gaps also removes the velocity and regulator dependence:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{Im}(\lambda_2-\lambda_0)}{\mathrm{Im}(\lambda_1-\lambda_0)}
      = \frac{x_2-x_0}{x_1-x_0},
    \label{eq:cft:gapratio}
\end{equation}
The normalisation, $\beta_0$, and $v$ cancel between numerator and denominator. This ratio therefore probes the relative operator content without external calibration.

The entropy slope in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} cannot be reduced to such a ratio. At finite $(T,\chi,\delta t)$, it differs from its continuum value:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{slope}(p) = \frac{c(p)}{8}\,K(T,\chi,\delta t,\beta_0) + R(p;T),
  \label{eq:cft:calib}
\end{equation}
Here $K$ collects finite-time, truncation, and Trotter corrections shared by two models in the same universality class when evaluated with matched parameters. The term $R$ contains model-dependent subleading corrections whose amplitudes depend on the operator content and boundary condition~\cite{boucomas2026}. At the integrable point, where $c=\tfrac12$, the measured $\mathrm{slope}(0)$ determines the shared factor $K$. Taking the ratio then cancels $K$, but not $R$:
\begin{equation}
  c(p) \approx \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{slope}(p)}{\mathrm{slope}(0)}.
  \label{eq:cft:calibratio}
\end{equation}
This calibration assumes that the two models share a universality class, which is itself under investigation. The residual term $R$ sets the uncertainty, so the calibrated value provides a consistency check rather than an independent determination. Alternatively, the \ac{CFT} fixes the functional form of the leading corrections~\cite{boucomas2026}, allowing them to be included explicitly and $c$ to be extracted without a reference model. This absolute method requires a sufficiently long time window to separate the correction from the leading term, whereas calibration can be applied at shorter times but requires an integrable reference.

\section{Truncation schemes}
\label{app:tensor_networks}

The general \ac{TN} framework, including diagrammatic notation, matrix product representations, canonical forms, and standard algorithms, is reviewed in Refs.~\cite{schollwock2011,banuls2023}. Here we present the linear algebra underlying the truncation choice in Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}. We explain why Schmidt-value truncation is optimal for a single state, how gauge freedom distinguishes physical from representation-dependent spectra, and how the construction extends to the \ac{RTM}.

\subsection{Gauge freedom and canonical forms}
\label{app:sub:canonical_forms}

An \ac{MPS} representation is not unique. On any virtual bond, one may insert $\mathbb{1}=XX^{-1}$ and absorb the two factors into neighbouring tensors, $A^{[n]}\to A^{[n]}X$ and $A^{[n+1]}\to X^{-1}A^{[n+1]}$, without changing the represented state. A canonical form fixes this freedom. Successive QR or \ac{SVD} decompositions make the tensors isometric from the left, $A^\dagger A=\mathbb{1}$, or from the right, $BB^\dagger=\mathbb{1}$, leaving a single centre tensor that carries the non-trivial weight. The full construction is given in Refs.~\cite{schollwock2011,banuls2023}.

A physical quantity must be invariant under this gauge transformation. Quantities that depend on individual tensors rather than on the represented state are gauge dependent and cannot define an invariant truncation criterion. For a single state, the canonical form fixes the gauge and exposes the physical Schmidt values. In the transverse construction, the distinction becomes essential because the left and right states are different.

\subsection{Schmidt values and optimal truncation}
\label{app:sub:truncation}

Consider a cut dividing the chain into a block $\mathcal{A}$ and its complement $\mathcal{B}$. Grouping the physical indices on each side into one composite index turns the wavefunction into a matrix, whose \ac{SVD} gives the Schmidt decomposition
\begin{equation}
  \ket\psi = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{\chi} s_\alpha\,
    \ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal A}\otimes\ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal B},
  \qquad s_\alpha\ge0,\quad \sum_\alpha s_\alpha^2=1,
\end{equation}
where the states on each side form orthonormal bases. In mixed canonical form, this decomposition is represented on the cut bond by a diagonal matrix of Schmidt coefficients. The corresponding \ac{RDM} is diagonal in the same basis, with eigenvalues $p_\alpha=s_\alpha^2$ that determine the entanglement across the cut.

Reducing a bond dimension from $\chi$ to $\chi'<\chi$ retains the $\chi'$ largest Schmidt coefficients. By the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem, this gives the closest state to $\ket\psi$ among all states with Schmidt rank at most $\chi'$. The squared norm error equals the discarded weight,
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = \big\lVert \ket\psi - \ket{\tilde\psi}\big\rVert_2^2
    = \sum_{\alpha>\chi'} s_\alpha^2.
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:truncation} shows why the truncation can be performed locally in canonical form.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{imgs/truncation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Local \ac{RDM} truncation in canonical form.} (a) Contracting a canonical tensor with its conjugate over the physical index and one virtual index gives the identity on the remaining index. The tensor and its conjugate are shown in lighter and darker shades. (b) Reduced density matrix at a cut, with the left block open and the right block traced. The isometries on either side of the orthogonality centre contract to identities, so the eigenvalues of the full \ac{RDM} are the squared singular values of the centre tensor $M$.}
  \label{fig:truncation}
\end{figure}

This construction relies on two properties of the single-state problem. The truncated object is Hermitian and positive, so its eigenvalues are the squared Schmidt values and their ordering is unambiguous. In addition, the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem provides a variational error bound. Neither property extends directly to a non-Hermitian transition matrix.

\subsection{The reduced transition matrix and its truncation}
\label{app:sub:rtm_truncation}

In the transverse picture, the transfer matrix is non-Hermitian and its dominant left and right fixed points are distinct. The corresponding truncation object is the \ac{RTM} in Eq.~\eqref{eq:rtm}, which is constructed from both states. Its eigenvalues can be complex and do not admit the Schmidt interpretation above. Gauge transformations can also redistribute weight between components that contribute to $\braket{L}{R}$ and those that do not~\cite{tang2025}. Figure~\ref{fig:rdm_rtm} compares the \ac{RDM} and \ac{RTM} at the same temporal cut.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{imgs/rdm_rtm.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Candidate truncation objects at a temporal cut.} Sites after the cut are traced over, while the physical legs before the cut remain open and form the matrix indices. (a) The \ac{RDM} pairs a temporal state with its conjugate and is Hermitian and positive. (b) The \ac{RTM} pairs the distinct fixed points $\ket{R}$ and $\bra{L}$; it is generally non-Hermitian and can have complex eigenvalues.}
  \label{fig:rdm_rtm}
\end{figure}

The two objects transform differently under a gauge transformation $X$ on the cut bond. The boundary \ac{RDM} transforms by a congruence, $\rho_L\to X^\dagger\rho_LX$, which changes its spectrum for a non-unitary $X$. The \ac{RTM} transforms by a similarity, $\mathcal{T}_t\to X^{-1}\mathcal{T}_tX$, and its eigenvalues are therefore invariant. Figure~\ref{fig:gauge} shows these transformations at the network level. Consequently, the spectrum of a single temporal-state \ac{RDM} depends on the representation, whereas the \ac{RTM} spectrum does not.

The \ac{RDM} scheme remains well defined in a fixed canonical gauge and can still be used as a numerical truncation method. The gauge argument instead identifies which spectrum is representation independent and can therefore be constrained by the \ac{CFT}.

Both schemes must extract their truncation data from a local $\chi\times\chi$ environment matrix $M_A$, rather than diagonalising the exponentially large global object. For the Hermitian case, a bipartite cut gives
\begin{equation}
  \rho_B = U_B\,M_A\,U_B^{\dagger},
  \label{eq:app:rdm_env}
\end{equation}
where $U_B$ is unitary because it is constructed from left-orthogonal tensors. Equation~\eqref{eq:app:rdm_env} is a similarity transformation, so $\mathrm{eig}(\rho_B)=\mathrm{eig}(M_A)$. The local environment therefore contains the eigenvalues of the full \ac{RDM}. For an \ac{RTM} built from two different states, the same cut gives
\begin{equation}
  \tau_B = U_B\,M_A\,V_B,
  \qquad U_B \neq V_B^{\dagger},
  \label{eq:app:rtm_env}
\end{equation}
which is not a similarity transformation. Its eigenvalues are therefore not preserved: $\mathrm{eig}(\tau_B)\neq\mathrm{eig}(M_A)$. However, $U_B$ and $V_B$ remain isometries and preserve singular values,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{sv}(\tau_B) = \mathrm{sv}(M_A).
  \label{eq:app:rtm_sv}
\end{equation}
Thus, the singular values of the local environment equal those of the full \ac{RTM}. They are the invariant quantities accessible from the local reduction and provide the practical truncation criterion.

Singular values and eigenvalues nevertheless encode different information. They satisfy $\mathrm{sv}(M)=\sqrt{\mathrm{eig}(MM^{\dagger})}$ and coincide for a Hermitian positive \ac{RDM}, but not for a non-Hermitian \ac{RTM}. The physical overlap is determined by the trace, $\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{T}_t=\sum_i\lambda_i$, which is a signed sum of eigenvalues and is not bounded directly by singular-value magnitudes. For example, eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$ both have unit magnitude but cancel exactly in the trace.

The \ac{RTM} truncation therefore lacks the variational bound available for an \ac{RDM}. Its success here is empirical and relies on the structure of transfer matrices generated by time evolution; the cancellations that could invalidate the singular-value criterion are not observed in the calculations studied. We retain the \ac{RDM} scheme as an independent numerical check.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{imgs/gauge.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Gauge transformation of the \ac{RDM} and \ac{RTM}.} (a) Inserting $X^{-1}X$ on a virtual bond changes the tensors but not the full contraction. In (b) and (c), the lower time site is contracted while the upper site remains open. (b) For the \ac{RDM}, the gauge factors combine as $XX^{\dagger}\neq\mathbb{1}$ and produce the congruence $X\rho X^{\dagger}$, which changes the spectrum. (c) For the \ac{RTM}, they combine as $XX^{-1}=\mathbb{1}$ and produce the similarity transformation $X\mathcal{T}_tX^{-1}$, which preserves the spectrum.}
  \label{fig:gauge}
\end{figure}

A generalised canonical form can be imposed on the pair $\bra{L},\ket{R}$ rather than on a state and its conjugate, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rtm_truncation}. Tensors away from the cut then contract to identities, and the singular values of the \ac{RTM} follow from a local decomposition. The \ac{RDM} and \ac{RTM} routes consequently have comparable computational cost and differ primarily in the object they compress.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{imgs/rtm_truncation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Local \ac{RTM} truncation in generalised canonical form.} (a) The contraction pairs a tensor of $\bra{L}$ with the corresponding tensor of $\ket{R}$ rather than with its conjugate. (b) Imposing this condition along the chain reduces the environment to the tensors at the cut, whose singular values equal those of the \ac{RTM}. The construction remains local but no longer has a variational error bound.}
  \label{fig:rtm_truncation}
\end{figure}

The transverse calculation depends on bilinear matrix elements rather than on the norm of either state separately. In particular, it involves quantities of the form $\bra{L}\mathcal O\ket{R}$. A component of $\ket R$ contributes weakly if its overlap with $\bra L$ is small, even when its weight in $\ket R$ is large, and the converse can also occur. The method therefore uses a biorthogonal decomposition of $\ket R\bra L$ and retains the components with largest-magnitude eigenvalues~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024}. Since relevance is measured through the left--right overlap, this criterion can require fewer states than an \ac{RDM} truncation at comparable accuracy.

The distinction between singular values and eigenvalues limits this argument. The contraction depends on the trace of $\mathcal{T}_t$, and hence on the signed sum of its eigenvalues, whereas the local reduction preserves its singular values. Large singular values do not preclude cancellation in the trace, as illustrated by eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$. Singular-value truncation of the \ac{RTM} is therefore a physically motivated approximation rather than a rigorous bound.

A further approximation arises because each truncation uses the local environment available at that stage of the sweep rather than the uncontracted remainder of the network. Translation invariance reduces the resulting error because each subsequent bulk column applies the same operator and can regenerate components of the leading subspace that were previously discarded. Nevertheless, this approximation contributes to the greater numerical stability of the \ac{RDM} route when the \ac{RTM} result becomes noisy.

\section{The fermionic form of the NNN interactions}
\label{app:pairing}
\label{sec:annni:jw}

This appendix derives the fermionic representation of the model and shows that the \ac{NNN} coupling generates genuine interactions for $p\neq0$, removing the free-fermion integrability of the Ising chain.


The interaction structure becomes explicit after mapping the spin chain to fermions. In the convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni_H}, with the transverse field along $\sigma^x$ and the order parameter along $\sigma^z$, the \ac{JW} transformation is~\cite{jordan_wigner1928,fradkin2013}
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^x_j = 1 - 2n_j, \qquad
    \sigma^z_j = \big(c_j + c_j^\dagger\big)\,K_j, \qquad
    K_j = \prod_{k<j}\big(1-2n_k\big),
    \label{eq:annni:jwmap}
\end{equation}
where $c_j$ annihilates a spinless fermion, $n_j=c^\dagger_j c_j$, and the string $K_j$ enforces the fermionic anticommutation relations.

The two terms present at $p=0$ remain quadratic. The transverse field becomes a chemical-potential term, while the strings in the \ac{NN} Ising coupling cancel and leave hopping and pairing terms. The resulting chain is a free-fermion model and can be diagonalised exactly~\cite{pfeuty1970}. At criticality, its continuum limit is the free Majorana \ac{CFT} with $c=\tfrac12$~\cite{difrancesco1997}.

The terms proportional to $p$ generate quartic fermionic contributions. The self-dual $\sigma^x_j\sigma^x_{j+1}$ term becomes the density--density factor $(1-2n_j)(1-2n_{j+1})$, which is diagonal in the occupation basis. For the \ac{NNN} bond, the Jordan--Wigner strings cancel only partially, leaving a parity factor on the intermediate site:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^z_i \sigma^z_{i+2}
    = \big(c_i^\dagger - c_i\big)\,\big(1-2n_{i+1}\big)\,\big(c_{i+2}+c_{i+2}^\dagger\big).
    \label{eq:annni:hop}
\end{equation}
This term describes hopping and pairing between sites $i$ and $i+2$, with a sign determined by the occupation of the intermediate site. We refer to this process as parity-dressed hopping. The resulting quartic terms allow fermion scattering, so Wick's theorem no longer reduces correlation functions to two-point functions~\cite{wick1950}. The model is therefore interacting and does not admit a free-fermion solution for $p\neq0$.

\subsection{Jordan--Wigner and the four-fermion interaction}
\label{app:pairing:jw}

We now derive Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:hop} from the transformation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:jwmap}. Pauli operators anticommute on the same site but commute on different sites, so local spin operators do not reproduce fermionic anticommutation at finite separation. The string $K_j$ supplies the parity of all sites to the left of $j$ and converts the spin algebra into the fermionic one. When the resulting Hamiltonian is quadratic, it can be diagonalised through a single-particle transformation into independent normal modes. This is the structure of the pure Ising chain and its free Majorana critical theory. The calculation below uses $(c+c^\dagger)(1-2n)=c^\dagger-c$ and $K_j^2=1$.

The transverse field becomes a chemical-potential term directly from Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:jwmap}. For the \ac{NN} Ising bond, the two strings overlap on all but one site and cancel telescopically, leaving the quadratic hopping-and-pairing term
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^z_j\sigma^z_{j+1} = (c_j^\dagger-c_j)(c_{j+1}+c_{j+1}^\dagger).
    \tag{F1}
\end{equation}
Together with the chemical-potential term, Eq.~(F1) gives the quadratic fermionic Hamiltonian of the pure Ising chain.

Both terms proportional to $p$ are quartic. The self-dual $\sigma^x\sigma^x$ term becomes the density--density factor $(1-2n_j)(1-2n_{j+1})$. For the \ac{NNN} bond, the strings cancel on the common sites, while the intermediate site contributes one uncancelled factor $(1-2n_{i+1})=(-1)^{n_{i+1}}$. This gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:hop}, in which the sign of hopping and pairing between $i$ and $i+2$ depends on the intermediate occupation.

\section{Extraction and equilibrium details}
\label{app:extraction}

This appendix gives the equilibrium measurements and fitting conventions used for the numerical values quoted in the main text. These include the finite-size test of the universality class, the sound velocity, and the branch constant in the eigenvalue fit.

\subsection{Finite-size scaling of the mid-chain entropy}
\label{app:fss}

Section~\ref{sec:annni:equilibrium} extracts the central charge from the entanglement profile at a fixed system size. An independent estimate follows from the size dependence of the entropy at the mid-chain cut. At this cut, the chord variable is maximal, $W(L/2,L)=\ln(2L/\pi)$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft_obc} reduces to
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{vN}}(L/2) = \frac{c}{6}\,\ln L + k'.
    \tag{G0}
    \label{eq:cft_mid}
\end{equation}
A linear fit of the mid-chain entropy against $\ln L$ therefore has slope $c/6$. For $N=40$--$200$, this procedure gives $c\approx0.51$ at both $p=0.1$ and $p=0.5$, consistent with the profile fit in the main text and with the Ising value. These calculations use fifteen \ac{DMRG} sweeps, a bond-dimension ramp up to $400$, and a truncation cutoff of $10^{-10}$. The $N=400$ chord fit in Section~\ref{sec:annni:equilibrium} uses fifty sweeps, a maximum bond dimension of $1000$, and a cutoff of $10^{-12}$. Both calculations begin with noise-assisted sweeps.

The same ground states provide a comparison with the R\'enyi-2 entropy used in the temporal calculations. Its mid-chain coefficient is $c/8$, but a linear fit gives $c\approx0.58$, above the Ising value. R\'enyi entropies with $n>1$ contain oscillatory finite-size corrections that decay algebraically with subsystem size~\cite{calabrese2010}; omitting them biases the fitted slope upwards. The corresponding temporal correction scales as $\Delta S_n\propto T^{-1/n}$~\cite{boucomas2026} and is included explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:results:wall}. We do not fit the equilibrium corrections because the von Neumann entropy already provides a stable estimate when the full Schmidt spectrum is available. Figure~\ref{fig:cft_L} compares the two finite-size results.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{imgs/cft_L.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Finite-size scaling of the mid-chain entropy.} Results for $p=0.1$ and $p=0.5$. The von Neumann entropy (solid lines), with slope $c/6$, gives $c\approx0.51$. The R\'enyi-2 entropy (dashed lines), computed from the same ground states and fitted with slope $c/8$, gives $c\approx0.58$ because of its larger finite-size corrections.}

\subsection{The sound velocity from the equilibrium dispersion}
\label{app:velocity}

We now give the construction and validation of the sound-velocity estimates used in Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity}.

We use finite periodic chains, for which momentum is a good quantum number, and diagonalise the Hamiltonian in Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni_H} exactly at $\lambda=1$. Each eigenstate is classified by its momentum, obtained from the one-site translation operator, and by its spin-flip parity. Since numerical diagonalisation returns arbitrary mixtures within degenerate subspaces, momentum and parity are resolved jointly before the states are labelled. In the magnetic, odd-parity sector, we identify the lowest states at momenta $0$ and $k$ and compute the velocity from their dispersion:
\begin{equation}
  v = \frac{N}{2\pi}\,\frac{E_1(k)-E_1(0)}{|k|},
  \label{eq:app:velocity}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the smallest non-zero momentum on the ring. Equation~\eqref{eq:app:velocity} is the finite-size form of the low-momentum dispersion $\omega=v|k|$. The allowed momenta are separated by $2\pi/N$, so the prefactor converts the energy difference between adjacent momentum sectors into the dispersion slope.

Four checks validate this construction. At $p=0$, the estimate approaches the exact value $v=2$ and reaches $1.990$ at $N=18$. The independently constructed ring Hamiltonian agrees with the operator used elsewhere in the code to within $7\times10^{-14}$ over the full spectrum at $N=8$. Sector-resolved and sector-independent calculations give the same velocity at every $p$. Finally, inserting the measured $v(p)$ into the Casimir formula of Ref.~\cite{alcaraz2016} gives $c=0.504$ at $p=0$ and $c=0.525$ at $p=0.5$ for $N=16$, consistent with the published ground-state results.

The accessible ring sizes remain far from the thermodynamic limit. At $p=0$, the estimate is $1.9899$ at $N=18$, compared with the exact value $2$. The finite-size error follows a regular $1/N^2$ dependence: multiplying it by $N^2$ gives values between $3.27$ and $3.28$ for $N=10$--$18$. Directly reaching three-digit accuracy would require approximately $N=57$, which is beyond exact diagonalisation, so we extrapolate this leading correction.

At each coupling, we fit $v(N)=v_\infty+C/N^2$ and use $v_\infty$ in the conformal analysis. Table~\ref{tab:velocity} lists the data and fit parameters, and Figure~\ref{fig:velocity_extrap} shows the extrapolations. The residuals remain below $2.8\times10^{-2}$ for velocities between approximately two and eleven. At $p=0$, the extrapolation gives $1.99994$, within $6\times10^{-5}$ of the exact value, although the $N=18$ result differs by $1.0\times10^{-2}$. The finite-size coefficient grows in magnitude from $C=-3.27$ at $p=0$ to $C=-140.7$ at $p=1.5$, making the extrapolation increasingly important with frustration. Using these coupling-dependent velocities, rather than fixing $v=2$, is necessary to recover the expected boundary dimensions.

\subsection{Fit windows and the controls behind the quoted numbers}
\label{app:errors}

The central charges in Table~\ref{tab:cp} are obtained from least-squares fits. We specify here the fitting windows, the fixed parameters, and the numerical controls used to assess their stability.

The start of the fitting window is not unique. Moving it to later times reduces the number of data points and increases the fitted central charge because the $1/T$ and $1/T^2$ contributions in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3} become difficult to distinguish over a short interval. Varying the starting time changes $c$ by $0.014$ at $p=0$, $0.024$ at $p=0.1$, and $0.21$ at $p=0.3$. We report the widest window that satisfies the consistency criterion, since it contains the most data and best separates the correction terms. The upper end is set by the loss of a constant phase increment in $\mu_0$. For example, at $p=0.5$ the increment is $1.8326$ with a spread of $0.0008$ over $T=2.2$--$4.5$, but becomes irregular at later times. This criterion determines the windows in Table~\ref{tab:cp}.

We fix the branch constant in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3} rather than fitting it. Its value follows from $\lambda_0=\log(-\mu_0)$, and unconstrained fits over sufficiently long windows give $B/\pi=-1.000$. Fixing it removes a parameter that short windows cannot determine. The phase is unwrapped over the complete time series before selecting a fitting window; otherwise the absolute branch information would be lost.

We use three numerical controls. Increasing the bond dimension to $\chi=128$ changes the imaginary plateau by approximately $2\%$ and $|\mu_0|$ by less than $10^{-4}$, but shifts the real-part chord slope by up to $0.12$. This motivates extracting the entropy result from the plateau. Halving the Trotter step while doubling $N_\beta$ to keep $\beta_0$ fixed changes the plateau by less than $1\%$ within the reliable window and by $18\%$ near its boundary. Repeating one time point with five independent random seeds produces a $3\%$ spread in the plateau and a $12\%$ spread in the chord slope, whereas $|\mu_0|$ agrees within $1.3\times10^{-4}$. The spectral estimates are therefore substantially less sensitive to the random seed. Finally, the sound velocity enters linearly, and its extrapolation gives $1.99994$ at the integrable point, compared with the exact value $2$.

The absolute entropy result in Eq.~\eqref{eq:res:c_entropy_p0} also depends on the finite-time correction model. We compare a constant, $a+bT^{-1/2}$, $a+b/T$, and $a+b/T+c/T^2$ using the corrected Akaike information criterion. Over $T=2$--$24$, the constant model is disfavoured by a difference of $21$. The $T^{-1/2}$ and two-term models differ by less than $1$ and give $c=0.506$ and $0.511$, respectively. Over the shorter interval $T\leq11$, the data do not distinguish the four models, demonstrating the need for the extended time series.

\section{Construction of the time-evolution MPO}
\label{app:mpo}

This appendix describes the time-evolution \ac{MPO} used in Section~\ref{sec:mporep}. We give the practical exponentiation procedure, outline the two available constructions, record the explicit second-order tensor, and compare their numerical accuracy.

\subsection{How the exponentiation is carried out}
\label{app:mpo:code}

We perform the exponentiation with \texttt{ITensorExpMPO.jl}~\cite{pokart2024expmpo}, which implements the construction of Ref.~\cite{zaletel2015} using \texttt{ITensors.jl}~\cite{itensor2022}. The Hamiltonian is supplied as a symbolic sum of operator strings, so the \ac{FSM} in Section~\ref{sec:mporep:fsm} need not be constructed manually. At each bond, the package collects the terms crossing that bond and compresses their coefficient matrix with an \ac{SVD}. The retained components form the minimal set of memory channels needed to represent the interactions. For Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni_H}, these are the three channels in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Wannni}. The resulting blocks $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ are then passed to the routine that constructs the local evolution tensor.

The original package provides the first- and second-order operators of Ref.~\cite{zaletel2015}. We added an implementation of the second-order construction of Ref.~\cite{vandamme2024}, including the required enlargement of the virtual bond from $1+\chi$ to $1+\chi+\chi^2$. This implementation is available in a fork of the package~\cite{marquezolguin2026expmpo}; the repositories contain the software documentation.

\subsection{The two constructions in outline}
\label{app:mpo:outline}

Both constructions address the symmetric cross terms generated at second order. Directly expanding $W_H^2$ includes every ordered pair of Hamiltonian blocks, so terms such as $CD$ and $DC$ must both be retained to preserve the Hermiticity inherited from $H$. In the construction of Ref.~\cite{zaletel2015}, the blocks are encoded in an auxiliary space of two hard-core bosons. The auxiliary exponential terminates once both modes are occupied, and projection onto the four bosonic states gives the blocks of the evolution \ac{MPO}. Retaining only disjoint-support terms gives the first-order operator $W^{\mathrm{I}}$. Adding single-site overlaps gives $W^{\mathrm{II}}$, which is second order for Hamiltonians containing only \ac{NN} interactions. Both use virtual bond dimension $1+\chi$.

The construction of Ref.~\cite{vandamme2024} instead organises the expansion into connected clusters of overlapping terms. Disjoint terms commute and factorise, so only connected clusters require an explicit approximation. Retaining clusters up to size $n$ gives a one-step error of order $\delta t^{n+1}$. The second-order operator used here introduces a doubled memory sector, increasing the virtual bond dimension to $1+\chi+\chi^2$. This sector represents the three-site connected clusters generated by the \ac{NNN} interaction. The resulting local tensor is given below.

In each scheme, the complex step $\tau=-i\delta t$ is distributed asymmetrically between the interaction blocks. The initiator $C$ is multiplied by $t_C=\sqrt{\delta t}$ and the terminator $B$ by $t_B=\tau/t_C$. Their product retains the required factor $\tau$, while the individual memory-channel amplitudes remain numerically balanced.

\subsection{The second-order \textit{VD2} blocks}
\label{app:mpo:vd2}

We now give the explicit local tensor of the second-order \ac{MPO}, denoted \textit{VD2}, used in the simulations. The expressions follow Ref.~\cite{vandamme2024} and are specialised to the \ac{FSM} blocks of the present Hamiltonian.

We start from the four blocks $A,B,C,D$ of the Hamiltonian MPO $W_H$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:Wannni}): $D$ is the on-site term, $C$ initiates an interaction, $B$ terminates one, and $A$ propagates it. Following the time-step split above, we scale them as
\begin{equation}
  \tilde D = \tau D, \qquad \tilde C = t_C\, C, \qquad \tilde B = t_B\, B, \qquad \tilde A = A,
  \qquad t_C = \sqrt{\delta t},\ \ t_B = \tau/t_C,\ \ \tau=-i\delta t .
\end{equation}
The expansion of $e^{\tau W_H}$ is expressed in terms of symmetrised products of overlapping blocks. We denote by $\{X_1\dots X_m\}$ the sum over all distinct orderings of the operator-valued blocks. For example, $\{XY\}=X\otimes Y+Y\otimes X$ and $\{XXY\}=XXY+XYX+YXX$. Here $\otimes$ denotes the Kronecker product on the virtual memory index and ordinary operator multiplication on the physical index. The explicit prefactors below include the factorial weights from the exponential series.

The local tensor has a $3\times3$ block structure. Indices $1$, $2$, and $3$ label the scalar, single-memory, and doubled-memory sectors, respectively, giving the virtual bond dimension $1+\chi+\chi^2$:
\begin{equation}
  W^{\textit{VD2}} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    W_{11} & W_{12} & W_{13} \\
    W_{21} & W_{22} & W_{23} \\
    W_{31} & W_{32} & W_{33}
  \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
  W_{11} &= \mathbbm{1} + \tilde D + \tfrac12\{\tilde D\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde D\tilde D\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{12} &= \tilde C + \tfrac12\{\tilde C\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde C\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{13} = \{\tilde C\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{21} &= \tilde B + \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{31} = \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{22} &= \tilde A + \tfrac12\big(\{\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\}\big)
            + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde C\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\tilde D\}\big), \\
  W_{23} &= \{\tilde A\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde C\tilde D\}+\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde B\}\big), \qquad
  W_{32} = \tfrac12\{\tilde A\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde C\}\big), \\
  W_{33} &= \{\tilde A\tilde A\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde A\tilde D\}\big).
\end{align}
The block $W_{11}$ contains the on-site evolution through third order in the Taylor expansion. The blocks $W_{12}$ and $W_{13}$ describe outgoing single- and doubled-memory sectors, while $W_{21}$ and $W_{31}$ describe the corresponding incoming sectors. The lower-right blocks combine propagation through $\tilde A$ with the required symmetrised products. In particular, the doubled-memory sector represents connected clusters spanning three sites. These are the \ac{NNN} overlaps omitted by $W^{\mathrm{II}}$, and their inclusion makes \textit{VD2} second order for the present Hamiltonian.

\subsection{Comparing the two constructions in practice}
\label{app:mpo:comparison}

We compare the two constructions through their violation of norm conservation. Exact real-time evolution preserves $\lVert\psi\rVert$, so the norm drift produced by successive approximate layers provides a direct measure of the propagation error. We apply each operator to $\ket{X+}^{\otimes N}$ and monitor the norm after each step.

Applying eight layers at $\delta t=0.05$ to a chain of $N=100$ sites at $p=0.1$ gives the following norms:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
 & $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ & $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ & \textit{VD2} \\
\hline
virtual bond & $4$ & $4$ & $13$ \\
$\lVert\psi\rVert$ after 1 layer & $1.3514$ & $1.1341$ & $0.99995$ \\
$\lVert\psi\rVert$ after 8 layers & $12.6146$ & $3.0181$ & $0.99923$ \\

The first-order operator $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ increases the norm by more than an order of magnitude after eight layers. The operator $W^{\mathrm{II}}$, which is second order for a strictly \ac{NN} Hamiltonian, increases it by a factor of three because it omits connected clusters generated by the \ac{NNN} interaction. Over the same interval, \textit{VD2} keeps the norm within $8\times10^{-4}$ of unity.

The larger virtual bond of \textit{VD2} is therefore required to control the time-step error. Reducing $\delta t$ sufficiently to compensate for a first-order propagator would increase the temporal chain length and introduce additional truncations. A second-order operator at moderate $\delta t$ provides the more efficient representation for the transverse calculation.

\section{Implementation of the block power method}
\label{app:blockpm}

This appendix describes the implementation of the block power method introduced in Section~\ref{sec:blockpm}. The core routine, \texttt{block\_transfer\_eigs}, is built on \texttt{ITransverse.jl}~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}. The code, analysis notebooks, and cluster drivers are available in the thesis repository~\cite{marquezolguin2026code}.

\subsection{The regularised solve and the left pairing}
\label{app:blockpm:pairing}

Near a degeneracy, the columns of the right block become nearly linearly dependent and the metric $S$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_pencil} becomes ill conditioned. Direct solution of the generalised eigenvalue problem $Mv=\theta Sv$ can then produce divergent or unstable values. We instead use the pseudoinverse of $S$:
\begin{equation}
  W = S^{+} M, \qquad W = V\,\Theta\,V^{-1},
  \tag{D1}
\end{equation}
The pseudoinverse uses a relative tolerance of $10^{-12}$, so directions that have become numerically dependent are projected out rather than inverted. After sorting by modulus, the diagonal entries of $\Theta$ estimate $\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{k-1}$, and the columns of $V$ give the right mixing coefficients.

The left block requires coefficients paired with the same eigenvalues. A separate diagonalisation of the transposed pencil is unreliable within a quasi-degenerate cluster because its eigenvalues can be returned in a different order, making the left--right pairing ambiguous. The coefficients can instead be obtained directly from the right decomposition. Defining $y^{\mathsf T}=u^{\mathsf T}S$ transforms the left problem into $y^{\mathsf T}W=\theta y^{\mathsf T}$, so the vectors $y$ are the rows of $V^{-1}$ and
\begin{equation}
  u_j = \big(S^{+}\big)^{\mathsf T}\big(V^{-1}\big)^{\mathsf T} e_j ,
  \tag{D2}
\end{equation}
is paired with $\theta_j$ by construction. The resulting coefficients are biorthogonal in the metric $S$, satisfying $u_i^{\mathsf T}Sv_j=\delta_{ij}$.

The \ac{MPS} linear combinations used for de-mixing are formed by exact direct-sum addition and compressed after the sum is complete. The default density-matrix addition becomes unstable when the vectors are nearly parallel, as occurs within a tightly clustered spectrum.

\subsection{The eigenvalue-only mode}
\label{app:blockpm:schur}

The eigenvalue-only mode replaces the de-mixing coefficients by orthonormal bases. After sorting the Ritz values by modulus, we apply QR decompositions to the coefficient matrices:
\begin{equation}
  V \to Q_V, \qquad U \to Q_U, \qquad Q^\dagger Q = \mathbb{1},
  \tag{D3}
\end{equation}
The blocks are then rotated with $Q_V$ and $Q_U$ rather than $V$ and $U$. The first $j$ columns of each orthonormal matrix span the same ordered subspaces as the corresponding coefficient matrix, giving an ordered-Schur-like basis. This basis change does not alter the Ritz values. Under invertible recombinations $B$ and $C$, the projected matrices transform as $S\to B^{\mathsf T}SC$ and $M\to B^{\mathsf T}MC$, leaving the solutions of the pencil unchanged. It does, however, improve the conditioning of the subsequent truncation: $\mathrm{cond}(V)$ increases as the internal spectral gap closes, whereas $Q_V$ and $Q_U$ remain unitary. The two modes can therefore differ only through truncation. At $p=0.1$ and $\chi=64$, independent time series in the two bases agree in the relative moduli of the two monitored eigenvalues to $10^{-7}$--$10^{-4}$ over $T=2$--$8$. The unmonitored eigenvalues agree to $10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$.

Three safeguards are applied in both modes. Each block member is normalised after de-mixing, and any vector whose norm underflows or overflows is replaced by a new random vector. We also monitor the condition number of $S$. When two block directions become numerically dependent, the final member is replaced and biorthogonalised against the remaining vectors using the bilinear form. Convergence is assessed from the leading Ritz values, with the first two monitored in production. Values are matched to their nearest predecessors before computing their change, preventing swaps in modulus order within a near-degenerate cluster from appearing as discontinuities. The iteration terminates when the matched change falls below $10^{-6}$ or ceases to improve for a prescribed number of iterations.

\subsection{Computational cost}
\label{app:blockpm:cost}

Each block-power iteration contains application, projection, and de-mixing steps. The application step performs $2k$ \ac{tMPO}--\ac{tMPS} products, increasing the temporal bond dimension from $\chi$ to $D\chi$ before compression. The projection step evaluates the $k^2$ bilinear overlaps defining $S$ and $M$; it does not increase the state dimensions, and the remaining $k\times k$ dense algebra is negligible. The de-mixing step forms $2k$ linear combinations of $k$ states. Exact direct-sum addition temporarily increases the bond dimension to $k\chi$ before compression, so this step determines both the peak memory and most of the runtime. For a representative production calculation with $\chi=64$, $k=4$, and $p=0.1$, de-mixing and compression account for approximately $85\%$ of each iteration.

We reduce this cost in two ways. First, partial direct sums are compressed progressively back to the production bond dimension, while the final addition remains exact. Second, the allowed stagnation period is shortened because late-time calculations can remain close to the convergence threshold for many iterations. Together, these changes reduce the runtime of the longest time points by approximately a factor of two and change $|\mu_0|$ by only $4\times10^{-6}$ relative.

Each truncation uses the current states without information from the network columns that remain to be contracted. A discarded component could therefore become relevant at a later step. In the translation-invariant bulk, however, each subsequent column applies the same operator $\mathcal{E}$ and can regenerate components belonging to the leading invariant subspace.

\subsection{Production options}
\label{app:library:build}
\label{app:library:cluster}
\label{app:library:adaptive}

A model-independent builder constructs the one-step propagator as an \ac{MPO} (Appendix~\ref{app:mpo}), performs the $90^\circ$ rotation from the spatial virtual bond to the temporal physical index, and returns the transfer-matrix \ac{MPO} together with a seed \ac{tMPS} carrying the required index structure. Before iteration, the seed tensors are replaced by random complex tensors because a symmetry-restricted seed can converge to a subdominant sector. The temporal physical dimension is inferred from the operator, allowing the same implementation to treat $W^{\mathrm{I}}$, $W^{\mathrm{II}}$, \textit{VD2}, and different models.

The following options modify the numerical procedure without changing its target fixed point.

\textit{Warm starting.} The converged block at one evolution time is re-indexed onto the longer temporal lattice of the next point and extended with a small random tail. The iteration then needs primarily to relax the new sites. The bond cap increases in steps of two up to $\chi$, while the truncation cutoff remains at $10^{-8}$ for the first forty iterations and then decreases to $10^{-10}$. Initialising consecutive times from the same leading subspace also improves continuity in the eigenvalue selection.

\textit{Block-size escalation.} At stronger frustration, a $k=4$ block can be occupied entirely by one near-degenerate cluster, leaving the subleading eigenvalue required by Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq4} outside the computed subspace. The driver then increases the block size as $k=4\to6\to8$, initialising each larger calculation from the converged smaller block. The additional cost is therefore incurred only when required by the spectrum.

\textit{Truncation scheme.} The calculation can use either \ac{RTM} or \ac{RDM} truncation (Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}). The \ac{RTM} is the default, while the better-conditioned \ac{RDM} provides a fallback and control.

\textit{Checkpointing.} Each converged $(p,T)$ point is stored immediately in a parameter-indexed cache. An interrupted calculation therefore loses at most the current point. A separate checkpoint stores the latest converged block and permits warm restarting after resubmission.

\subsection{Selecting the dominant eigenvalue}
\label{app:selector}

As stated in Section~\ref{sec:reach}, we select the eigenvalue of largest modulus and use continuity with the preceding time point to resolve differences below one per cent. If the selected $|\mu_0|$ changes by more than a prescribed fraction relative to the previous accepted value, the point is flagged as potentially unconverged. The stored spectrum is then reanalysed by restricting the candidates to a five-per-cent window around the previous modulus and selecting the dominant eigenvalue within that interval. The reference is updated sequentially from the last accepted point. For the case with an independently converged comparison, this correction agrees with the repeated calculation to $3\times10^{-5}$ relative. Subleading eigenvalues are ordered by their phase distance from $\mu_0$ and matched continuously to the conformal tower.

When a numerical limitation appears, we repeat representative calculations at half the bond dimension, with a tighter truncation cutoff, and with \ac{RDM} instead of \ac{RTM} truncation. We interpret a feature as intrinsic to the transfer matrix only when it persists under these changes.

\subsection{Why eigenvalues outlive eigenvectors}
\label{app:conditioning}

The different numerical stability of eigenvalues and eigenvectors follows from first-order perturbation theory for non-Hermitian matrices~\cite{wilkinson1965}. We perturb $\mathcal{E}$ by $\delta\mathcal{E}$ and expand the perturbed right eigenvector as $\ket{R_i}+\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}\ket{R_j}$. Projection onto $\bra{L_i}$ gives the eigenvalue shift,
\begin{equation}
  \delta\mu_i=
  \frac{\bra{L_i}\delta\mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}}
       {\braket{L_i}{R_i}},
  \label{eq:eigenvalue_perturbation}
\end{equation}
while projection onto $\bra{L_j}$ for $j\neq i$ gives the eigenvector-mixing coefficients,
\begin{equation}
  a_{ij}=
  \frac{\bra{L_j}\delta\mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}}
       {(\mu_i-\mu_j)\,\braket{L_j}{R_j}} .
  \label{eq:eigenvector_perturbation}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:eigenvalue_perturbation} contains no spectral gap, so the eigenvalue remains stable while the overlap $\braket{L_i}{R_i}$ remains finite. By contrast, Eq.~\eqref{eq:eigenvector_perturbation} contains the denominator $\mu_i-\mu_j$. As two eigenvalues approach, their eigenvectors become increasingly sensitive to perturbations and the de-mixing of individual block members becomes ill conditioned.

The overlap appearing in Eq.~\eqref{eq:eigenvalue_perturbation} is quantified by the phase rigidity of a biorthogonal pair,
\begin{equation}
    r_j = \frac{|\braket{L_j}{R_j}|}{\lVert L_j\rVert\,\lVert R_j\rVert},
    \label{eq:rigidity}
\end{equation}
which is the normalised overlap of the left and right eigenvectors associated with the same eigenvalue. It equals one for a Hermitian matrix and approaches zero as their biorthogonal overlap decreases. A small rigidity therefore increases the sensitivity of an eigenvalue associated with an individual eigenvector. The leading invariant subspace can nevertheless remain well conditioned when it is separated from the rest of the spectrum. The eigenvalue-only mode exploits this distinction: it propagates an orthonormal basis of the leading subspace and obtains the Ritz values from the projected pencil without assigning each basis vector to a particular eigenvector. Its improved stability arises from avoiding the ill-conditioned de-mixing required by the eigenvector calculation.

The ratio $|\mu_1/\mu_0|$ controls the suppression of the first subleading contribution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:contraction}, but it does not determine convergence of the block iteration. Convergence instead depends on the separation between the complete leading subspace and the remainder of the spectrum. Near a degeneracy within the retained block, $|\mu_1/\mu_0|$ can approach one while the subspace remains well separated and its Ritz values continue to converge.
