\section{Conformal-field-theory derivations}
\label{app:cft}

This appendix collects the standard conformal-field-theory results used in Section~\ref{sec:cft}---the chord form of the two-point function, the strip quantization behind the transfer-matrix spectrum, and the entropy coefficients---and sketches where each comes from. Full derivations are in Refs.~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004,difrancesco1997}; what is specific to this thesis is only how the results are used.

\subsection{Conformal maps, the two-point function and the entropy coefficients}
\label{app:cft:maps}

In two dimensions the Euclidean coordinates are packaged into $z=x+i\tau$ and $\bar z=x-i\tau$. A primary operator $\phi$ carries a pair of weights $(h,\bar h)$, its exponents under the two chiral halves of the conformal group, and the combination
\begin{equation}
    x = h + \bar h
    \label{eq:cft:dim}
\end{equation}
is its scaling dimension, the exponent quoted in the main text. On the plane the two-point function is the power law
\begin{equation}
    \langle \phi(z_1)\phi(z_2)\rangle
      = \frac{1}{(z_1-z_2)^{2h}\,(\bar z_1-\bar z_2)^{2\bar h}}.
    \label{eq:cft:plane2pt}
\end{equation}
Because every analytic map is a conformal symmetry, this fixes the correlator on any geometry such a map reaches. Mapping the plane onto a cylinder of circumference $\beta$ with the logarithm map turns the power law into~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004,difrancesco1997}
\begin{equation}
    \langle\phi(\omega_1)\phi(\omega_2)\rangle \;\propto\;
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\omega_1-\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2h}
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\bar\omega_1-\bar\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2\bar h}.
    \label{eq:cft:cyl2pt}
\end{equation}
Continuing to real time, with $\beta\to iT$ and $\sinh\to\sin$, produces the strip geometry of the quench, and the time-like version of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:cyl2pt} is the origin of the chord variable of Eq.~\eqref{eq:chord}.

The same two-point function fixes the entropy coefficients, which is why the two derivations belong together. The entanglement entropy of an interval $\mathcal A$ follows from the R\'enyi moments $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}$, continued to $n\to1$. The replica trick~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004} computes those moments by inserting one twist operator at each endpoint of $\mathcal A$ that lies in the interior of the system; the twist operator is a primary of dimension~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_n = \frac{c}{12}\Big(n - \frac{1}{n}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:twist}
\end{equation}
For a bulk interval both endpoints are interior, the moments follow the two-point power law $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}\sim\ell^{-4\Delta_n}$, and the entropy is
\begin{equation}
    S_n = \frac{c}{6}\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)\log\ell + \text{const},
    \qquad
    S_{n\to1} = \frac{c}{3}\log\ell + \text{const}.
    \label{eq:cft:cc}
\end{equation}
The cuts of this thesis are the other case. Each temporal region is anchored at an edge of the strip, so it shares only one border with its complement: a single interior endpoint, a single twist operator, and half the logarithmic coefficient. That gives $\tfrac{c}{12}(1+\tfrac1n)$---$c/6$ at $n\to1$ and $c/8$ at $n=2$---rather than the bulk $c/3$, and using the bulk coefficient by mistake would double every fitted central charge.




\subsection{The transfer-matrix spectrum from the stress tensor}
\label{app:cft:spectrum}

The central charge is carried by the stress-energy tensor, the operator that measures the response of the theory to a change of coordinates. In two dimensions those changes are the symmetry itself, so the modes $L_n$ of the stress tensor generate the conformal transformations; their commutators close into the Virasoro algebra, whose central term is proportional to $c$, which is where the central charge gets its name~\cite{difrancesco1997}. One mode is special: $L_0$ generates scalings, and its eigenvalues on the primary operators are their weights, so its spectrum is the operator content of the theory.

Quantizing on a strip of transverse size $\beta$ makes the anomaly of the main text explicit. Following Ref.~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}, the generator of translations along the strip is an integral of the stress tensor, and the conformal map from the plane leaves behind one extra, purely geometric term proportional to $c$,
\begin{equation}
    \hat H = \frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(L_0 - \frac{c}{24}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:casimir}
\end{equation}
This single expression contains both kinds of universal data: the constant $-c/24$ is the Casimir anomaly and carries the central charge, while the spectrum of $L_0$ lists the scaling dimensions. The transfer matrix advances one column of the network, so it is $\mathcal{E}=e^{-a\,\hat H}$ with $a$ the spatial lattice spacing, and substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir} fixes its whole spectrum in the form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer}, with $g=a/\beta$ and $a=1$ in lattice units: a dominant eigenvalue carrying the Casimir term, and a tower of subleading ones whose ratios and phases carry the operator dimensions.

The width entering on the lattice is not purely Euclidean, since the regulator supplies imaginary time at the two ends and the quench real time in between, and the velocity converts both durations into lengths,
\begin{equation}
    \beta = v\,(2\beta_0 + i\,T),
    \qquad
    \frac{1}{\beta} \;\simeq\; \frac{1}{v}\Big(\frac{2\beta_0}{T^2} - \frac{i}{T}\Big),
    \label{eq:cft:continuation}
\end{equation}
the expansion holding to first order in $\beta_0/T$. Substituting it into Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} gives the eigenvalues of the transfer matrix,
\begin{equation}
    \log\mu_i \;\simeq\; \frac{i\pi}{v\,T}\Big(x_i-\frac{c}{24}\Big)
      \;-\; \frac{2\pi\beta_0}{v\,T^2}\Big(x_i-\frac{c}{24}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:contexpand}
\end{equation}
The universal data therefore appear in the phases at order $1/T$, and in the moduli only through the regulator, at order $\beta_0/T^2$. Taking $x_i=0$ for the identity gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3}, and the difference between two eigenvalues gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq4}. The moduli are flat to the extent that $\beta_0$ is small, which is the emergent dual unitarity measured in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Optimal Truncation and the Transition Matrix}
\label{app:tensor_networks}

For the general framework of tensor networks---diagrammatic notation, matrix product states and operators, canonical forms and the standard algorithms---we refer to the reviews of Refs.~\cite{schollwock2011,banuls2023}. This appendix records only the linear algebra behind the truncation choice of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}: why truncating on Schmidt values is optimal for a single state, why gauge freedom decides which spectra are physical, and how the construction generalizes to the \ac{RTM}.

\subsection{Gauge freedom and canonical forms}
\label{app:sub:canonical_forms}

The \ac{MPS} representation of a state is not unique. On any virtual bond one may insert $\mathbb{1}=XX^{-1}$ and absorb the two factors into the neighbouring tensors, $A^{[n]}\to A^{[n]}X$ and $A^{[n+1]}\to X^{-1}A^{[n+1]}$, changing the tensors while leaving the state untouched. The canonical form fixes this freedom: sweeping QR or SVD decompositions inward from both ends makes every tensor an isometry, $A^\dagger A=\mathbb{1}$ from the left and $BB^\dagger=\mathbb{1}$ from the right, with one centre tensor carrying the non-trivial weight. Refs.~\cite{schollwock2011,banuls2023} give the construction in full.

Gauge freedom draws the line between physical and representational quantities. Anything that depends on the individual tensors rather than on the state they encode can be changed at will by a gauge transformation, and is therefore not meaningful to truncate on. In the single-state setting this causes no trouble, because the canonical form fixes the gauge and the Schmidt values below are automatically the physical ones. In the transverse picture it becomes the deciding issue, as Section~\ref{app:sub:rtm_truncation} shows.

\subsection{Schmidt values and optimal truncation}
\label{app:sub:truncation}

Consider a cut dividing the chain into a block $\mathcal{A}$ and its complement $\mathcal{B}$. Grouping the physical indices on each side into one composite index turns the wavefunction into a matrix, whose \ac{SVD} gives the Schmidt decomposition
\begin{equation}
  \ket\psi = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{\chi} s_\alpha\,
    \ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal A}\otimes\ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal B},
  \qquad s_\alpha\ge0,\quad \sum_\alpha s_\alpha^2=1,
\end{equation}
with orthonormal bases on the two sides. In mixed canonical form this structure sits on the cut bond: the centre object is the diagonal matrix of Schmidt coefficients, and the \ac{RDM} of the block is diagonal in the same basis with eigenvalues $p_\alpha=s_\alpha^2$, the probabilities that fix the entanglement across the cut.

Truncating a bond from $\chi$ to $\chi'<\chi$ means keeping the $\chi'$ largest Schmidt coefficients. This is optimal in a precise sense: by the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem, among all states of Schmidt rank at most $\chi'$ the truncated state is the closest to $\ket\psi$ in norm, with an error equal to the discarded weight,
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = \big\lVert \ket\psi - \ket{\tilde\psi}\big\rVert_2^2
    = \sum_{\alpha>\chi'} s_\alpha^2.
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:truncation} shows why the truncation can be performed locally in canonical form.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{imgs/truncation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Why the \ac{RDM} truncation is local.} (a) The canonical condition: a tensor
  contracted with its own conjugate over the physical index and one virtual index reduces to the
  identity on the remaining one. A tensor and its conjugate are drawn as the same shape in a lighter
  and a darker shade. (b) The reduced density matrix at a cut, with the left block open and the
  right block traced. In mixed canonical form the isometries either side of the orthogonality centre
  collapse by (a), so the eigenvalues of the whole object are the squared singular values of the
  single tensor $M$, and the optimal truncation never touches the rest of the chain.}
  \label{fig:truncation}
\end{figure}

Two features of this scheme matter later, because the transverse picture keeps one and loses the other. First, the truncated object is Hermitian and positive, so its eigenvalues are the squared Schmidt values and dropping the smallest is unambiguous. Second, the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem provides a variational guarantee and bounds the error. Both are properties of the Hermitian, single-state setting, and neither survives the move to a non-Hermitian transition matrix.

\subsection{The reduced transition matrix and its truncation}
\label{app:sub:rtm_truncation}

In the transverse picture the transfer matrix is non-Hermitian, so its dominant left and right fixed points are distinct states, and the object to truncate is the \ac{RTM} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rtm}, built from both. Its eigenvalues can be complex, so the Schmidt interpretation above does not carry over. The gauge freedom also controls how well the two fixed points can be represented at a given bond dimension, since it moves weight between the components that contribute to $\braket{L}{R}$ and those that do not~\cite{tang2025}. Figure~\ref{fig:rdm_rtm} contrasts the reduced density matrix with the transition matrix at the same temporal cut.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{imgs/rdm_rtm.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The two candidate objects at a temporal cut.} Sites after the cut are traced over, so their
  physical legs are contracted between the two rows; the legs before the cut stay open and carry the
  indices of the resulting matrix. (a) The reduced density matrix of a single temporal boundary pairs
  $\ket{L}$ with its own conjugate, and is Hermitian and positive. (b) The \ac{RTM} instead pairs the
  two distinct fixed points $\ket{R}$ and $\bra{L}$, so it is not Hermitian and its eigenvalues need
  not be real or positive.}
  \label{fig:rdm_rtm}
\end{figure}

The choice between the two objects is settled by their behaviour under a gauge transformation. Insert $X$ on the cut bond: the reduced density matrix and the transition matrix respond differently. The boundary RDM transforms by a congruence, $\rho_L\to X^\dagger\rho_L X$, which for the non-unitary $X$ generic here distorts its spectrum. The transition matrix transforms by a similarity, $\mathcal{T}_t\to X^{-1}\mathcal{T}_t X$, which leaves its eigenvalues untouched. Figure~\ref{fig:gauge} shows the difference at the level of the network. The Schmidt values of a single temporal boundary are therefore not physical, while the RTM spectrum is, and truncating on the former can discard exactly the weight the overlap needs.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{imgs/gauge.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Why the \ac{RTM} spectrum is the gauge-invariant one.} (a) A pair $X^{-1}X$ may be inserted
  on every bond at no cost: the two factors cancel, so the contraction equals the ungauged one and
  the Loschmidt echo is untouched, while the individual tensors change. In (b) and (c) only the
  lower time site is contracted; the upper one carries the open indices, so each open index keeps
  one factor and the contracted bond receives both. (b) The \ac{RDM} pairs $\ket{R}$ with its own
  conjugate, the factors meet as $XX^{\dagger}\neq\mathbb{1}$, and the spectrum moves under the
  congruence $X\rho X^{\dagger}$. (c) The \ac{RTM} pairs $\ket{R}$ with $\bra{L}$, the factors meet
  as $XX^{-1}=\mathbb{1}$, and the similarity $X\mathcal{T}_t X^{-1}$ leaves the spectrum unchanged.}
  \label{fig:gauge}
\end{figure}

The canonical form still helps, but in a generalized version, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rtm_truncation}: the condition of Figure~\ref{fig:truncation}(a) is imposed on the pair $\bra{L},\ket{R}$ rather than on a state and its own conjugate. The tensors away from the cut then contract away exactly as before, and the singular values of the \ac{RTM} again follow from a local decomposition, so the two routes cost the same to evaluate and differ only in which object they compress.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{imgs/rtm_truncation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The \ac{RTM} counterpart of Figure~\ref{fig:truncation}.} (a) The generalized canonical
  condition: the contraction pairs a tensor of $\bra{L}$ with the corresponding tensor of $\ket{R}$,
  not with its own conjugate. (b) Imposed along the chain, this condition makes everything before
  the cut contract away, so the singular values of the \ac{RTM} are those of the two tensors at the
  cut alone. The construction is as local as the \ac{RDM} one; what it loses is the variational
  guarantee, since the object is no longer Hermitian.}
  \label{fig:rtm_truncation}
\end{figure}

The logic of truncation changes accordingly. In the ordinary case one keeps the directions that best preserve the norm of a state. Here the relevant quantities are bilinear matrix elements $\bra{L}\mathcal O\ket{R}$. A component of $\ket R$ with little overlap with $\bra L$ contributes little, however large it looks from $\ket R$ alone, and a component with small weight in $\ket R$ can still matter if its projection onto $\bra L$ is large. The scheme of Ref.~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024} therefore performs a biorthogonal decomposition of $\ket R\bra L$ and keeps the eigenvalues of largest modulus, which are the directions that dominate the physical contraction. Because relevance is measured through the overlap, this can retain fewer states than an RDM truncation at the same accuracy.

The stability caveat of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation} can now be made precise. The singular values of $\mathcal{T}_t$ and its eigenvalues are different quantities, and it is the eigenvalues that enter the contraction, since the trace is their sum. A component with a large singular value can therefore still fail to contribute: an operator with eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$ has both at unit modulus, so an SVD-based truncation keeps both, yet they cancel exactly in the trace. Truncating the RTM on singular values is thus a motivated heuristic rather than a bound.

A second approximation enters through the environment. The truncation at each step uses the local environment available at that point of the sweep, not the full remaining network, because the contraction is built column by column. For a translation-invariant problem this is benign, since every bulk column is the same operator and weight discarded too early is regenerated by the next application. It remains an approximation, and it is one reason the RDM route behaves more predictably when the RTM output turns noisy.

\section{Construction of the time-evolution MPO}
\label{app:mpo}

This appendix records the construction details behind Section~\ref{sec:mporep}: the schemes that exponentiate the \ac{FSM} blocks directly, the closed-form second-order tensor used in production, and the measurement that justifies it.

\subsection{The auxiliary-boson trick and the time-step split}
\label{app:mpo:boson}

The simplest construction on the overlap logic of Section~\ref{sec:mporep:exp} is $W^{\mathrm{I}}$, which keeps every disconnected term and discards all overlaps. It distributes the time step across the initiators $C$, the terminators $B$ and the on-site block $D$, keeps the virtual bond at $1+\chi$, and is first order. Improving it to $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ means additionally keeping the single-site overlaps, and the overlaps need care: $H^2$ sums over every ordered pair, so both orderings $CD$ and $DC$ of an initiator and a field on the same site must be kept, or the operator loses the Hermiticity it inherits from $H$.

Writing out the correct symmetric combinations by hand is impractical. Ref.~\cite{zaletel2015} generates them with an auxiliary-space construction, encoding the blocks in the dynamics of two hard-core bosons whose exponential truncates by itself, and Ref.~\cite{vandamme2024} generalizes the idea to arbitrary order; the second-order member of that family is the VD2 operator below.

All these schemes share the time-step split, which is what keeps them stable. The complex step $\tau=-i\delta t$ is divided unevenly: a real factor $t_C=\sqrt{\delta t}$ goes to the initiator block and the phase $t_B=\tau/t_C$ to the terminator block, so the product $C\otimes B$ still carries the correct factor while the intermediate memory channels stay well behaved.

\subsection{The second-order VD2 blocks}
\label{app:mpo:vd2}

We record the explicit local tensor of the second-order MPO (VD2) used throughout this work. These are the closed-form expressions of Van Damme et al.~\cite{vandamme2024}, specialized to our \ac{FSM} blocks; they are what the exponentiation routine assembles for a given memory dimension $\chi$.

We start from the four blocks $A,B,C,D$ of the Hamiltonian MPO $W_H$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:Wannni}): $D$ is the on-site term, $C$ initiates an interaction, $B$ terminates one, and $A$ propagates it. Following the time-step split above, we scale them as
\begin{equation}
  \tilde D = \tau D, \qquad \tilde C = t_C\, C, \qquad \tilde B = t_B\, B, \qquad \tilde A = A,
  \qquad t_C = \sqrt{\delta t},\ \ t_B = \tau/t_C,\ \ \tau=-i\delta t .
\end{equation}
The construction organizes the exponential $e^{\tau W_H}$ not by powers of $W_H$, but by the symmetric products of overlapping blocks that its Taylor series generates. We write $\{X_1\dots X_m\}$ for the symmetrized product, i.e.\ the sum over all distinct orderings of the operator-valued blocks (for example $\{XY\}=X\otimes Y + Y\otimes X$, and $\{XXY\}=XXY+XYX+YXX$), where $\otimes$ is the Kronecker product on the virtual memory index and the ordinary product on the physical index. The multinomial weights $1/2!,\,1/3!,\dots$ of the exponential are carried by the explicit prefactors below.

The resulting local tensor is a $3\times3$ block object (indices $1,2,3$ label the scalar, single- and doubled-memory sectors, which is the origin of the enlarged $1+\chi+\chi^2$ virtual bond),
\begin{equation}
  W^{\mathrm{VD2}} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    W_{11} & W_{12} & W_{13} \\
    W_{21} & W_{22} & W_{23} \\
    W_{31} & W_{32} & W_{33}
  \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
  W_{11} &= \mathbbm{1} + \tilde D + \tfrac12\{\tilde D\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde D\tilde D\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{12} &= \tilde C + \tfrac12\{\tilde C\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde C\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{13} = \{\tilde C\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{21} &= \tilde B + \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{31} = \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{22} &= \tilde A + \tfrac12\big(\{\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\}\big)
            + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde C\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\tilde D\}\big), \\
  W_{23} &= \{\tilde A\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde C\tilde D\}+\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde B\}\big), \qquad
  W_{32} = \tfrac12\{\tilde A\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde C\}\big), \\
  W_{33} &= \{\tilde A\tilde A\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde A\tilde D\}\big).
\end{align}
The scalar track $W_{11}$ carries the on-site evolution to third Taylor order; the $W_{12},W_{13}$ entries dress an outgoing memory leg (single and doubled), $W_{21},W_{31}$ the incoming one, and the lower-right block dresses the through-wire $\tilde A$ with all of its symmetric companions. Keeping the doubled-leg (index-3) sector is exactly what reproduces the connected clusters spanning three sites---the next-nearest-neighbour overlaps that $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ misses---and is why VD2 is genuinely second order for our model at the cost of the enlarged virtual bond.

\subsection{Comparing the two constructions in practice}
\label{app:mpo:comparison}

The claim that $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ degrades to first order for a next-nearest-neighbour Hamiltonian, while VD2 remains second order, can be checked directly on the model of this thesis. A convenient diagnostic is the loss of unitarity, since the exact $e^{-iH\delta t}$ preserves the norm of any state: applying successive layers of the approximate operator to $\ket{X+}^{\otimes N}$ and monitoring $\lVert\psi\rVert$ isolates the Trotter error, because a perfect construction would leave the norm at one.

Applying eight layers at $\delta t=0.05$ to a chain of $N=100$ sites at $p=0.1$ gives the following norms:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
 & $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ & $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ & VD2 \\
\hline
virtual bond & $4$ & $4$ & $13$ \\
$\lVert\psi\rVert$ after 1 layer & $1.3514$ & $1.1341$ & $0.99995$ \\
$\lVert\psi\rVert$ after 8 layers & $12.6146$ & $3.0181$ & $0.99923$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The separation is unambiguous. The first-order $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ inflates the norm by an order of magnitude within eight steps, and $W^{\mathrm{II}}$, which would be effectively second order for a strictly \ac{NN} chain, still triples it: the connected clusters generated by the \ac{NNN} bridge are precisely the ones it fails to capture. VD2 holds the norm to within $8\times10^{-4}$ over the same interval.

This is what justifies paying for the larger virtual bond. Since the transverse contraction needs long temporal chains, and reducing $\delta t$ to compensate a first-order error would lengthen them further and add truncation error at every extra site, a genuinely second-order operator at a moderate time step is the configuration that makes the calculation feasible at all.

\section{Implementation details}
\label{app:blockpm}

This appendix documents the implementation of the block power method of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm} for a reader who wants to know how it works internally or to run it. The core routine is \texttt{block\_transfer\_eigs}, built on top of \texttt{ITransverse.jl}~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}; all code, notebooks and cluster drivers are in the public repository of Ref.~\cite{marquezolguin2026code}. The subsections cover, in order: the numerical solve of the projected pencil, the eigenvalue-only mode, the validation against exact diagonalization, the cost profile, the options that control a production run, and the classifier that assigns the block members.

\subsection{The regularized solve and the left pairing}
\label{app:blockpm:pairing}

Near the degeneracy the columns of the right block begin to coincide and the metric $S$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_pencil} becomes nearly singular, so the pencil $Mv=\theta\,Sv$ is not handed to a generalized eigensolver, which would return infinite or wildly scattered values. It is regularized with the pseudoinverse instead,
\begin{equation}
  W = S^{+} M, \qquad W = V\,\Theta\,V^{-1},
  \tag{D1}
\end{equation}
where $S^{+}$ carries a relative tolerance of $10^{-12}$: directions that have effectively collapsed onto each other are projected out rather than inverted. The eigenvalues $\Theta$, sorted by modulus, estimate $\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{k-1}$, and the columns of $V$ give the right mixing coefficients.

The left block needs its own coefficients, and the direct route is unreliable. Diagonalizing the transposed pencil in a second call gives no guarantee that its eigenvalues come out in the same order, and inside a quasi-degenerate cluster, matching each left eigenvalue to the nearest right one is ambiguous exactly when it matters: a greedy match picks the wrong partner, the paired left and right vectors stop describing the same eigenvector, and the block oscillates instead of converging. No second diagonalization is needed. Writing $y^{\mathsf T}=u^{\mathsf T}S$, the left problem becomes $y^{\mathsf T}W=\theta\,y^{\mathsf T}$, so the $y$ are the rows of $V^{-1}$ and
\begin{equation}
  u_j = \big(S^{+}\big)^{\mathsf T}\big(V^{-1}\big)^{\mathsf T} e_j ,
  \tag{D2}
\end{equation}
paired with $\theta_j$ by construction and biorthogonal through the metric, $u_i^{\mathsf T}S\,v_j=\delta_{ij}$.

One implementation note concerns the de-mixing. The MPS linear combinations of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm} are formed by exact direct-sum addition and compressed once at the end, because the default density-matrix addition is fragile for the near-parallel vectors that a tight cluster produces.

\subsection{The eigenvalue-only mode}
\label{app:blockpm:schur}

The eigenvalue-only mode of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm} replaces the de-mixing coefficients by orthonormal ones. After the modulus sorting, the coefficient matrices are QR-decomposed,
\begin{equation}
  V \to Q_V, \qquad U \to Q_U, \qquad Q^\dagger Q = \mathbb{1},
  \tag{D3}
\end{equation}
and the blocks are rotated with $Q_V$ and $Q_U$ instead of $V$ and $U$. The leading $j$ columns of each $Q$ span the same leading subspaces as those of the sorted coefficient matrices, so this is an ordered-Schur-like choice, and the Ritz values are unchanged for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:blockpm}. What the rotation does change is the conditioning of what is truncated next: $\mathrm{cond}(V)$ grows like the inverse of the gap inside the block, while $Q_V$ and $Q_U$ are unitary by construction. The two modes can therefore drift apart only through the truncation, and in practice they barely do. Two independent ladders at $p=0.1$ and $\chi=64$, one carried in each basis, agree on the two tracked eigenvalues to between $10^{-7}$ and $10^{-4}$ in relative modulus over $T=2$ to $8$; the untracked members agree only to $10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$, which is expected since the convergence test never monitors them.

Three safeguards complete the iteration in either mode. Every block member is renormalized after each de-mixing, and a member whose norm underflows or overflows is replaced by a fresh random vector. The condition number of $S$ is monitored: a large value means two block directions have effectively merged, and the last member is then replaced by a random vector bi-orthogonalized against the rest with the bilinear form. Convergence is measured on the leading Ritz values (the leading two in production) after matching each value to its nearest predecessor from the previous iteration, since the frequent modulus-order swaps between near-degenerate eigenvalues would otherwise register as spurious jumps; the iteration stops when the matched movement falls below $10^{-6}$, or when it has stopped improving for a set number of iterations.

\subsection{Computational cost}
\label{app:blockpm:cost}

One iteration of the block method contains three kinds of tensor-network work. The application step performs $2k$ \ac{tMPO}--\ac{tMPS} products, each growing the temporal bond from $\chi$ to $D\chi$ before compression. The projection step evaluates the $k^2$ bilinear overlaps of $S$ and $M$; no state grows there, and the $k\times k$ dense algebra is negligible. The de-mixing step forms $2k$ linear combinations of $k$ states each. Every combination is built as an exact direct sum, which raises the bond dimension to $k\chi$ before the compression sweep, so both the memory peak of an iteration and most of its work sit in this step. Profiling a production rung ($\chi=64$, $k=4$, $p=0.1$) attributes roughly $85\%$ of the iteration time to the de-mixing sums and their compression sweeps.

Two adjustments reduce this cost without changing what the method converges to. The running direct sum is compressed progressively, each partial sum truncated back to the production bond dimension, with the final addition left exact. And the stagnation patience is shortened, since at late rungs the iteration spends long plateaus below the convergence threshold before the stop triggers. Together the two halve the wall-clock time of the longest rungs while moving $|\mu_0|$ by $4\times10^{-6}$ relative. The mechanisms that reduce the number of iterations, rather than the cost of each one, are described next.

\subsection{Production options}
\label{app:library:build}
\label{app:library:cluster}
\label{app:library:adaptive}

A thin, model-agnostic builder sits above the solver. It assembles the one-step propagator as an MPO (Appendix~\ref{app:mpo}), performs the $90^\circ$ rotation that maps the spatial virtual bond onto the temporal physical index, and returns the transfer-matrix MPO with a seed tMPS carrying the right index skeleton. The seed is always overwritten with random complex tensors before the iteration, because a symmetry-special seed converges to a subdominant sector. The temporal physical dimension is read from the operator rather than hard-coded, which is what lets the same code path serve the $W^{\mathrm{I}}$, $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ and VD2 kernels and every model without modification.

The options that control a production run are the following; none changes what the method converges to.

\textit{Warm starting.} Along a ladder of evolution times, the converged block at one time is re-indexed onto the longer time-site set of the next and padded with a small random tail, so the iteration only has to relax the newly added sites. The bond cap and the cutoff follow a schedule that is loose early and tight near convergence: the cap grows in steps of two up to $\chi$, and the cutoff holds at $10^{-8}$ for the first forty iterations before tightening to $10^{-10}$. This is more than a speed-up: near a degeneracy a cold random restart has nothing biasing it onto the physical branch, so warm continuity is also what keeps the tracker on the correct eigenvector family as the spectrum tightens.

\textit{Block-size escalation.} At stronger frustration the leading $k=4$ block can fill with members of a single near-degenerate cluster, pushing the subleading eigenvalue needed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq4} outside the captured window. The driver detects this, since the tower gap of the classifier below returns no value when no tower member was captured, and escalates the block in steps ($k=4\to6\to8$), warm-seeding each larger solve with the converged smaller block, so the extra cost is paid only where the spectrum demands it.

\textit{Truncation scheme.} The truncated object is selectable between \ac{RTM} and \ac{RDM} (Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}), with RTM the production default and RDM the well-conditioned fallback.

\textit{Crash safety.} Each $(p,T)$ point is written to a per-label cache as soon as it converges, keyed by its parameters, so an interrupted run loses at most the point in flight and a completed point is never recomputed. A separate checkpoint stores the last converged block, so a resubmitted run resumes warm.

\subsection{The branch classifier}
\label{app:selector}

The classifier assigns the block members relative to a candidate $\mu_0$. Writing $\mathrm{phase}(z)=\arg(-z)$ for the convention of Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}, it splits the block into a tower and a set of partners: the tower is every eigenvalue within $\pi/2$ in phase, the conformal descendants of $\mu_0$, and the partners are the momentum-$\pi$ displaced states of Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}. The tower gap is $|\mu_1|/|\mu_0|$ computed from the largest-modulus tower member only, never a partner, and it returns no value when the block holds no tower member, which is the trigger for the escalation above.

The selection rules are the following. We take the eigenvalue of largest modulus, using continuity with the preceding rung only when the two largest moduli differ by less than one per cent, and we flag a rung whenever the selected $|\mu_0|$ jumps by more than a prescribed fraction relative to the last trusted value, since such a jump signals unreliable convergence or a wrong branch assignment. Flagged rungs are not discarded outright. A rung can fail to converge and still carry the physical branch: the spurious value typically sits far above the physical band while the true $\mu_0$ survives further down the block, so a post-processing selector restricts the candidates to a window of five per cent around the last trusted modulus and applies dominance inside that window. Rungs with no candidate in the window are dropped and the reference is chained from the last accepted point. This repairs affected rungs from the stored spectra alone, with no recomputation; on the one rung where an independently converged run exists for comparison, the repaired eigenvalue agrees with it to $3\times10^{-5}$ relative.

Modulus dominance holds only while the two moduli can be told apart. Deep in the dual-unitary regime the physical branch and its displaced partner approach a common modulus, and once their splitting falls below the accuracy of the iteration the ordering between them loses meaning. The phase-predicted tracking of Section~\ref{sec:reach} then takes over: a rung whose block holds no member near the predicted phase is discarded, and the prediction resolves the branch only while the ladder samples the winding finely enough. Phases are defined modulo $2\pi$, so an advance per rung larger than $\pi$ is indistinguishable from the same advance plus a full turn, and the spacing of the ladder in $T$ therefore sets the frustration up to which the branch can be followed. We record here what a nearest-value rule does wrong, since it is the natural first attempt. The physical branch rotates between rungs by an amount that grows with the velocity and hence with $p$, while its displaced partners of similar modulus stand still in phase, so a nearest-value rule latches onto a partner and, re-anchoring on its own pick, corrupts the ladder that follows. At $p=0.5$ this manifests as the tracked $|\mu_0|$ collapsing at one rung and following partners ever after: output that looks plausible but is an artefact of the selector.

The phase-predicted tracking that takes over past the modulus crossing deserves three technical notes. First, a crossing does not lose the branch: the physical eigenvalue survives inside the block as a subleading member, and where an independently tracked ladder exists for comparison the phases agree to four decimals. Second, the prediction must be seeded, because the phase advance per rung grows with the velocity and can exceed $\pi$, making the wrapped step ambiguous; the first step is chosen by testing every candidate advance against the next few rungs and keeping the most consistent one. Third, each accepted rung records the distance between the prediction and the matched phase, and the ladder is cut at the first rung where this distance jumps, which is where the block stops resolving the branch.

\subsection{Why eigenvalues outlive eigenvectors}
\label{app:conditioning}

Section~\ref{sec:reach} states that eigenvalues remain accurate where eigenvectors do not. Both halves of that statement follow from first-order perturbation theory for a non-Hermitian matrix~\cite{wilkinson1965}. Perturb $\mathcal{E}$ by $\delta\mathcal{E}$ and expand the perturbed eigenvector as $\ket{R_i}+\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}\ket{R_j}$. Projecting the eigenvalue equation onto $\bra{L_i}$ gives the shift of the eigenvalue,
\begin{equation}
  \delta\mu_i=
  \frac{\bra{L_i}\delta\mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}}
       {\braket{L_i}{R_i}},
  \label{eq:eigenvalue_perturbation}
\end{equation}
and projecting it onto $\bra{L_j}$ with $j\neq i$ gives the mixing coefficients,
\begin{equation}
  a_{ij}=
  \frac{\bra{L_j}\delta\mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}}
       {(\mu_i-\mu_j)\,\braket{L_j}{R_j}} .
  \label{eq:eigenvector_perturbation}
\end{equation}
The two expressions differ in one respect that matters here. Equation~\eqref{eq:eigenvalue_perturbation} contains no eigenvalue difference, so an eigenvalue stays accurate as long as its own overlap $\braket{L_i}{R_i}$ stays finite. Equation~\eqref{eq:eigenvector_perturbation} is divided by $\mu_i-\mu_j$, so the eigenvector mixes with its neighbours as soon as two eigenvalues approach, and the de-mixing that separates the block members becomes correspondingly harder.

The denominator of Eq.~\eqref{eq:eigenvalue_perturbation} is the rigidity of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rigidity}, so the same expression bounds how far the protection extends. Near an exceptional point the overlap tends to zero and an eigenvalue tied to an individual eigenvector loses accuracy with it; close to a defective point individual eigenvalues are in fact fragile, splitting under a perturbation of size $\varepsilon$ as $\varepsilon^{1/n}$ rather than $\varepsilon$, where $n$ is the size of the coalescing cluster~\cite{moro1997}. What stays well conditioned is the leading subspace as a whole, whose separation from the rest of the spectrum governs the block iteration. Our eigenvalue-only mode reads the spectrum at that level: it never assigns block members to separate eigenvectors, but carries an orthonormal basis of the subspace and takes the Ritz values from the projected pencil. The eigenvalues therefore behave better than the eigenvectors not because they are immune, but because the subspace-level read avoids the ill-conditioned de-mixing that the eigenvector route cannot do without.

One further diagnostic belongs here. The spectral ratio $|\mu_1/\mu_0|$ controls the suppression of the first subleading contribution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:contraction}, but it is not a convergence criterion for the block, which is governed instead by the separation of the whole leading subspace from the rest of the spectrum. The two differ near a degeneracy inside the block, where $|\mu_1/\mu_0|$ may approach one while the subspace stays well separated and its eigenvalues keep converging.

\section{The exceptional-point wall}
\label{app:wall}

This appendix supports the diagnosis of Section~\ref{sec:results:wall}, where the temporal-entropy dome inflates at a well-defined time and stops being conformal. It has two parts: what an exceptional point is and why it spoils the eigenvectors while sparing the eigenvalues, and the controls that establish the wall as a property of the physics rather than a resolution limit.

\subsection{Exceptional points of a non-normal matrix}
\label{app:wall:ep}

Section~\ref{sec:reach} established how eigenvalues and eigenvectors respond differently as a gap inside the block closes, and Appendix~\ref{app:conditioning} gives the perturbative statement behind it. The limiting configuration is what this subsection adds~\cite{rotter2009}. As two eigenvalues of a non-normal matrix approach each other, their left and right eigenvectors need not stay apart: they can rotate towards a common direction, so the pairing $\braket{L}{R}$ of the coalescing branch tends to zero even though neither vector vanishes. Where two eigenvalues and their eigenvectors merge at once the matrix is at an exceptional point: it is defective, has no complete eigenbasis, and the sensitivity of the individual eigenvector diverges.

This is the origin of the wall. The dome breaks not because the solver fails to converge, but because the object it is asked to converge to becomes ill-defined. The order parameter is the phase rigidity of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rigidity}, which equals one for a near-Hermitian pair and collapses to zero at the exceptional point, and its measured collapse lands exactly where the dome inflates, which is what identifies the two as one event. Complex symmetry does not help, when a model has it: every matrix is similar to a complex-symmetric one, so symmetry can relabel the collapse but not forbid it, and inside an exactly degenerate subspace the individual eigenvector is undefined rather than merely ill-conditioned.

\subsection{Locating the wall from the entropy profile}
\label{app:wall:shape}

Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} predicts that the real part of the entropy is linear in the chord variable, with slope $c/8$. This gives a direct test of whether a given profile is still conformal, and therefore a way to read off the time at which the route fails.

Figure~\ref{fig:broken_dome} shows the $p=0.1$ profiles at $T=9$ and $T=10$ against the $c=\tfrac12$ line. At $T=9$ the profile follows that line, departing from it by at most $0.06$. At $T=10$ it leaves the line altogether, departing by $0.42$ and more than doubling in height, $0.72$ against $0.34$. One evolution step separates the two.

Past that step the iteration has converged to something other than the physical eigenvectors, so the profile is an artefact of the failed convergence. Its height, its slope and the second maximum it develops are not properties of the model, and we do not read anything from them. The one thing we use is the time at which the profile leaves the conformal line.

The regulator $\beta_0$ is a fourth knob, and the most promising one, because the modulus gaps grow linearly with it and it is those gaps closing that ends the route. Across $\beta_0=0.1$ to $0.8$ the rate at which the rigidity collapses is unchanged, the ratio $r(T{=}6)/r(T{=}2)$ staying between $7.35$ and $7.40\times10^{-3}$ over the whole range, so the curves are parallel and the wall sits at the same time. The rigidity itself is in fact lower at larger $\beta_0$, by about $40\%$ from one end of that range to the other, so widening the regulator makes the conditioning slightly worse rather than better.

These controls also inform which truncation to use in production. The two schemes reach the same wall, but the \ac{RDM} run is considerably more expensive, because it needs a larger bond dimension to reach the same accuracy. The reason is the one given in Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}: the \ac{RTM} keeps only the components that contribute to $\braket{L}{R}$, whereas the \ac{RDM} also represents components of one boundary that are orthogonal to the other and drop out of the contraction. We therefore use the \ac{RTM} in production.

This comparison was run at one coupling, at one bond dimension, and inside the block power method. It says nothing about how the two schemes compare elsewhere. The \ac{RDM} is Hermitian and positive, so it is the better conditioned of the two. It may well reach further at larger $\chi$, at stronger frustration, or under a plain power iteration. We have not tested any of these. The question is open, and running $p=0.3$ with a bond dimension of a few hundred would settle it.

\section{The momentum-\texorpdfstring{$\pi$}{pi} displaced boundary tower}
\label{app:pairing}

This appendix supports the boundary-exponent result of Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}. It establishes two things: that the next-nearest-neighbour coupling becomes a genuine interaction after fermionization, and that a free-fermion chain cannot produce a displaced tower.

Fermion parity, the number of fermions modulo two, is conserved and separates the transfer spectrum into the two families observed in Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}. The even-parity states form the identity tower and the odd-parity states the $\varepsilon$ tower. The displaced states are the odd ones. The measurement therefore says that a state with an odd number of fermions picks up a minus sign under a one-site shift. A minus sign is a phase of $\pi$.

\subsection{Jordan--Wigner and the four-fermion interaction}
\label{app:pairing:jw}

The main text quotes the transformation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:jwmap} and its action on the \ac{NNN} bond in Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:hop}; this subsection supplies the algebra between the two. The string is what makes the transformation possible at all. Two Pauli operators anticommute on the same site but commute on different ones, so a spin is neither a boson nor a fermion, and no purely local combination of spins can anticommute at a distance. Attaching to each fermion the parity of everything to its left converts the commuting spin algebra into the anticommuting fermion one exactly. The reward is that a Hamiltonian quadratic in fermions is exactly solvable --- diagonalizing a single-particle matrix yields independent normal modes --- which is the reason the plain Ising chain is free and its critical point a free Majorana \ac{CFT}. The working identities below are $(c+c^\dagger)(1-2n)=c^\dagger-c$ and $K_j^2=1$.

The transverse field becomes a chemical potential directly from Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:jwmap}. For the \ac{NN} Ising bond the two strings overlap on all but one site, so they cancel telescopically and leave the quadratic hopping-and-pairing term
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^z_j\sigma^z_{j+1} = (c_j^\dagger-c_j)(c_{j+1}+c_{j+1}^\dagger).
    \tag{F1}
\end{equation}
These two terms make up the pure Ising chain, which is why it is a free-fermion theory, exactly solvable, with a free Majorana \ac{CFT} at criticality.

The two terms proportional to $p$ are both quartic. The self-dual $\sigma^x\sigma^x$ term becomes the density--density factor $(1-2n_j)(1-2n_{j+1})$, diagonal in the occupation basis. For the \ac{NNN} bond the strings cancel only partially: the site skipped over contributes its factor once instead of twice, leaving the parity $(1-2n_{i+1})=(-1)^{n_{i+1}}$ in place, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:hop}---a hop from $i$ to $i+2$ whose sign is set by the occupation of the intermediate site. This single term is the origin of the displacement.

\subsection{The free-fermion normal form}
\label{app:pairing:normalform}

The argument of the next subsection rests on what being free buys, so we state it here. An operator is Gaussian, or free, if it is the exponential of an expression quadratic in the fermions, allowing both hopping terms $c^\dagger_i c_j$ and pairing terms $c^\dagger_i c^\dagger_j$. Pairing terms mean the operator cannot be diagonalized by relabelling particles alone; one must mix creation and annihilation operators through a Bogoliubov transformation, $\eta_m=\sum_j(u_{mj}c_j+v_{mj}c^\dagger_j)$, which is still a linear change of variables, so the result is still free: a set of independent normal modes.

For a Gaussian state, Wick's theorem reduces every higher correlation function to sums of products of two-point functions, one term per pairing of the operators, with a sign from the permutation that brings the partners together. It holds because the modes are independent: each is a two-level system with its own occupation, and expectation values factorize mode by mode, so nothing correlates three or four particles beyond what the pairs already encode. Its significance is that the entire theory --- correlators, entanglement, the full spectrum --- is generated by single-particle data, a matrix of size set by the number of modes rather than by the exponentially large Hilbert space. This is the precise sense in which free means exactly solvable.

Applied to a Gaussian transfer matrix, the same logic produces the normal form $\mathcal{E}=\mu_0\exp(-\sum_m\varepsilon_m\hat n_m)$ with $\hat n_m\in\{0,1\}$: every eigenvalue is $\mu_0\,e^{-\sum_{m\in S}\varepsilon_m}$ for some occupied subset $S$, so moduli and phases are both additive over occupied modes --- $\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon_m$ is a mode's cost in magnitude and $\mathrm{Im}\,\varepsilon_m$ the phase it contributes. This additivity is why the conformal tower appears as small, evenly spaced phase steps: the near-gapless modes have $\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon\approx0$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,\varepsilon=\pi x/(vT)$. A quartic term destroys this structure: the modes cease to be independent, Wick's theorem fails, and the spectrum is no longer additive over any single-particle data. The parity operator $e^{i\pi\hat N}$ is itself Gaussian, since $\hat N$ is quadratic, so the argument of the next subsection rests on the dispersion of the free chain rather than on Gaussianity alone.

\subsection{Why free fermions cannot displace}
\label{app:pairing:kw}

A free-fermion transfer matrix has the normal form of the previous subsection, so its eigenvalues are fixed by which modes are occupied. An eigenvalue close to the top of the spectrum can only occupy modes that cost almost nothing in magnitude, $\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon_m\approx0$. In an Ising-class critical chain those are the long-wavelength modes, and their phases $\pi x/(vT)$ are small.

A displaced state requires the opposite combination, a phase of $\pi$ at full modulus, and therefore a mode that neither decays nor stops oscillating. Such a mode is a gapless branch at lattice momentum $\pi$. The uniform Ising-class free chain has no such branch, so it cannot produce a displaced tower. The controls confirm this, since the critical Ising chain at two evolution times and the anisotropic XY chain at $\gamma=0.5,\,1,\,1.5$ return no displaced state at all.

The displacement therefore requires a source of momentum-$\pi$ structure, and in this model that source is an interaction. Single-term probes identify which one. Deforming the critical Ising chain by the \ac{NNN} term alone, at strength $0.1$ and $T=4$, produces a displaced family at $x\approx0.37,\,1.22,\,2.17$; deforming it by the $\sigma^x\sigma^x$ term alone produces none. It is therefore the parity-dressed hop of Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:hop}, not the density--density term, that carries the displacement. Scanning down to small couplings, the displaced family is present at every $p\neq0$ tested, down to $p=0.01$, with no threshold: the effect switches on with the interaction rather than at some finite strength.

One consequence of the single-term probe is worth recording. The \ac{NNN}-only deformation is not self-dual, and it displaces all the same. Self-duality is therefore not needed for the displacement to exist; the argument below concerns why it is pinned at exactly $\pi$ rather than at a coupling-dependent value.

The value of the displacement deserves its own remark. Across every coupling probed, from $p=0.01$ to $0.15$, the offset is identical and compatible with exactly $\pi$ rather than with any coupling-dependent phase. One structural observation fits naturally here: in Majorana variables the $XX$ and next-nearest $ZZ$ terms are both products of four consecutive Majoranas, offset by one Majorana site, and that one-site shift is the lattice Kramers--Wannier duality exchanging order and disorder --- which is why a self-dual Hamiltonian must carry both terms. \textit{The mechanism selecting the odd sector and fixing the displacement at exactly $\pi$ is ongoing work and is not settled here.} What the main text needs is established: the displacement exists for every $p\neq0$, is absent for free fermions, and is pinned at $\pi$.

\section{Extraction and equilibrium details}
\label{app:extraction}

This appendix collects the equilibrium measurements and fit conventions behind numbers quoted in the main text: the finite-size check of the universality class, the sound velocity, and the branch constant of the eigenvalue fit.

\subsection{Finite-size scaling of the mid-chain entropy}
\label{app:fss}

Section~\ref{sec:annni:equilibrium} reads the central charge from the shape of the entanglement profile at a single system size. A second, independent estimate comes from the size dependence of one point of that profile. At the mid-chain cut the chord takes its maximum, $W(L/2,L)=\ln(2L/\pi)$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft_obc} collapses to
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{vN}}(L/2) = \frac{c}{6}\,\ln L + k'.
    \tag{G0}
    \label{eq:cft_mid}
\end{equation}
Running DMRG over a ladder of sizes and plotting the mid-chain entropy against $\ln L$ therefore gives a straight line of slope $c/6$. Over $N=40$ to $200$ this returns $c\approx0.51$ at both $p=0.1$ and $p=0.5$, consistent with the profile fit of the main text and with the Ising value. The ladder uses fifteen DMRG sweeps with the bond dimension ramped to $400$ and a truncation cutoff of $10^{-10}$; the heavier $N=100$ chord fit of Section~\ref{sec:annni:equilibrium} uses fifty sweeps, a ramp to $1000$, and a cutoff of $10^{-12}$. Both ramps start with noise-assisted sweeps.

The same ground states allow a cross-check with the R\'enyi-2 entropy, the member used for every temporal measurement in this thesis. Its mid-chain slope is $c/8$ rather than $c/6$, and a plain linear fit returns $c\approx0.58$: the same universality class, but biased upward. The bias is expected, and it has the same origin on both axes of this thesis. In equilibrium, R\'enyi entropies with $n>1$ carry corrections to scaling that the von Neumann entropy does not, oscillating with the cut position and decaying as a power of the subsystem size~\cite{calabrese2010}. In the temporal problem, Ref.~\cite{boucomas2026} derives the corresponding finite-time correction for the generalized temporal R\'enyi entropies and finds $\Delta S_n \propto T^{-1/n}$ for the Ising class, which decays more slowly as $n$ grows and is why higher members are more strongly biased. A straight-line fit that ignores either correction is therefore not an unbiased estimator of $c$. We do not fit the equilibrium corrections here, because the von Neumann route already measures the equilibrium central charge; the temporal $n=2$ correction, $T^{-1/2}$, is fitted where it matters, in Section~\ref{sec:results:wall}. This is the equilibrium counterpart of the finite-time bias affecting the temporal R\'enyi-2 read, and it is why the von Neumann entropy is the estimator of choice in equilibrium, where the full Schmidt spectrum is available at no cost. Figure~\ref{fig:cft_L} compares the two finite-size estimates.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\subsection{The sound velocity from the equilibrium dispersion}
\label{app:velocity}

Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity} measures the sound velocity from the equilibrium dispersion. We use Alcaraz's relation for this chain: the ring Hamiltonian is diagonalized at small size, and $v$ follows from the gap between the lowest momentum-carrying excitation in the magnetic sector and the lowest state without momentum. This appendix records the checks behind the numbers quoted there.

The construction is validated in four ways. It recovers $v=2$ at $p=0$, to $1.987$ at $N=16$ and improving with size. The ring Hamiltonian built from scratch matches the operator used elsewhere in the code to machine precision, $7\times10^{-14}$ on the full spectrum at $N=8$. The sector-resolved and sector-blind reads agree at every $p$. Finally, feeding the measured $v(p)$ into Alcaraz's separate Casimir formula reproduces his published ground-state central charge: we obtain $c=0.504$ at $p=0$ and $c=0.525$ at $p=0.5$ from rings of $16$ sites. The values used throughout are those at $N=12$, and it is these, rather than a fixed $v=2$, that make the boundary tower of Section~\ref{sec:results:tower} read as clean half-integers at every $p$.

That the temporal transfer matrix really carries this $p$-dependent velocity was checked separately with the anisotropic XY control, whose velocity $v(\gamma)=2\gamma$ is known exactly. At $\gamma=0.5,\,1,\,1.5$ the measured first-gap slopes are $a_1 = 1.481,\,0.782,\,0.520$ against the exact $\pi/(4\gamma) = 1.571,\,0.785,\,0.524$, giving $x_1 = 0.471,\,0.498,\,0.496$; pinning $v=2$ instead would force $a_1=0.785$ at every $\gamma$ and disagree with two of the three measured slopes. The pipeline recovers $x_1=\tfrac12$ only when fed the correct $v(\gamma)$, which confirms that the method is velocity-faithful.
