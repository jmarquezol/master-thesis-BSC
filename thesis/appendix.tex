\section{Fundamentals of Tensor Networks and Optimal Truncation}
\label{app:tensor_networks}

The simulation of a quantum many-body system is fundamentally bottlenecked by the ``curse of dimensionality'': for $N$ spin-$1/2$ particles the Hilbert space has dimension $2^N$, so a completely general wavefunction requires an exponentially large number of coefficients. Tensor networks exploit the fact that nature rarely uses all of that space: physically relevant states typically carry little entanglement---the ground states of local gapped Hamiltonians obey an area law, in which the entanglement across a cut grows only with the boundary of the subsystem rather than its volume---and such states can be represented faithfully by a network of small tensors joined by internal bonds of controlled dimension. This appendix collects the foundations of that ansatz for readers who want them, but it is written with a specific destination in mind, and it is worth stating that destination up front, because it is what makes the appendix relevant to this thesis rather than a generic primer.

Everything the main text does---the transverse contraction of Section~\ref{sec:transverse}, the block power method of Section~\ref{sec:nonhermitian}---rests on repeatedly truncating a tensor network, and the decisive question is which object one truncates. In the ordinary Schr\"odinger picture that object is the reduced density matrix (RDM) of a single state; in the transverse picture, where the transfer matrix is non-Hermitian and its left and right fixed points differ, it is instead a reduced transition matrix (RTM) built from both. The property that decides which of these gives a stable truncation is gauge freedom: the RTM spectrum is gauge-invariant, the RDM spectrum is not. The subsections below build only what is needed to reach that statement---the singular value decomposition, matrix product states and operators, gauge freedom and canonical forms, the Schmidt decomposition and the RDM, optimal truncation and its variational bound, and finally the RTM itself.

\subsection{Notation and basic operations}
\label{app:sub:graphical_notation}

Two conventions are used without comment in the main text. The first is the diagrammatic notation, in which a tensor is a shape with one leg per index (a scalar has none, a vector one, a matrix two), and a contraction---a sum over a shared index---joins the two corresponding legs, the open legs becoming the free indices of the result. The picture makes the connectivity of a network, and the order in which its tensors are best contracted, immediate. The second is reshaping: since linear-algebra routines act on matrices, a higher-rank tensor is grouped into a matrix by treating several indices as one composite index---for example $A_{i,\alpha,\beta}\to M_{(i\alpha),\beta}$---which relabels the same entries without changing them. This is what lets us apply matrix tools, above all the singular value decomposition, directly inside a tensor network.

\subsection{The singular value decomposition}
\label{app:sub:svd}

One of the central tools in tensor-network algorithms is the \ac{SVD}. Any matrix $M$ of dimension $D_A \times D_B$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
    M = U S V^\dagger,
\end{equation}
where $U$ has orthonormal columns ($U^\dagger U=\mathbb{1}$), $V^\dagger$ has orthonormal rows ($V^\dagger V=\mathbb{1}$), and $S$ is a diagonal matrix whose entries are non-negative singular values, ordered as $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$.

In tensor-network language, an SVD is the operation that splits one tensor into two. The two new tensors are connected by a new internal index, and the dimension of this index is set by the number of nonzero singular values. In this sense, the SVD not only factorizes an object, but also tells us how large the new bond has to be in order to represent the original tensor exactly.

The ordering of the singular values is what makes the SVD especially useful. Large singular values correspond to the most important components across the chosen bipartition, while small singular values carry less weight. This gives a controlled way to compress a tensor: by discarding the smallest singular values, we reduce the dimension of the new bond while keeping the dominant correlations. This is the basic idea behind truncation.

We will use this mechanism repeatedly. First, it allows us to build matrix product states by splitting a many-body wavefunction site by site. Later, in Section~\ref{app:sub:truncation}, it provides the practical rule for reducing bond dimensions after operations that make them grow too large. The important point is that the SVD gives a systematic way to decide what to keep and what can be safely discarded.


\subsection{Matrix product states and operators}
\label{app:sub:mps_mpo}

A matrix product state (MPS) is obtained by applying the SVD idea repeatedly along a one-dimensional chain. A completely general state on $N$ sites can be written as
\begin{equation}
    |\Psi\rangle = \sum_{i_1,\dots,i_N} C_{i_1\dots i_N}\,|i_1,\dots,i_N\rangle,
\end{equation}
where the coefficient tensor $C$ contains $d^N$ entries. A direct representation is then impossible for large systems. But by sweeping an SVD through the chain, this large tensor can be factorized exactly into a product of smaller tensors,
\begin{equation}
    C_{i_1\dots i_N} = \sum_{\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_{N-1}}
        A^{[1]}_{i_1,\alpha_1} A^{[2]}_{i_2,\alpha_1,\alpha_2}
        \cdots A^{[N]}_{i_N,\alpha_{N-1}},
\end{equation}
where each tensor carries one physical index $i_n$ and, except at the boundaries, two virtual indices connecting it to its neighbours.

The dimensions of the virtual indices are the bond dimensions. They determine how much correlation the MPS can carry across each cut. If the bond dimension is $\chi=1$, the state is simply a product state with no entanglement between the two sides of any cut. Increasing $\chi$ allows the ansatz to represent more entangled states. The factorization itself is exact if the bond dimensions are allowed to grow as needed; the approximation enters only when we impose a maximum bond dimension and truncate the smaller singular values, as discussed in Section~\ref{app:sub:truncation}.

Matrix product operators (MPOs) use the same idea but for operators instead of states. An MPO is a chain of local tensors with two physical indices, one incoming and one outgoing, together with virtual indices that connect neighbouring tensors. Applying an MPO to an MPS generally increases the bond dimension, because the virtual spaces of the operator and the state combine. This growth is one of the main reasons truncation is needed in practical tensor-network algorithms.


\subsection{Gauge freedom and canonical forms}
\label{app:sub:canonical_forms}

The MPS representation is not unique. On any virtual bond we can insert the identity in the form $\mathbb{1}=XX^{-1}$ and absorb the two factors into the neighbouring tensors,
\begin{equation}
    A^{[n]} \to A^{[n]}X, \qquad A^{[n+1]} \to X^{-1}A^{[n+1]}.
\end{equation}
This transformation changes the individual tensors but leaves the physical state $|\Psi\rangle$ unchanged. This freedom is called gauge freedom. It is extremely useful because it allows us to choose the tensor representation that is best suited to a given calculation.

The most important choice is the canonical form. By sweeping QR or SVD decompositions from left to right, tensors can be brought into left-canonical form, meaning that they satisfy $A^\dagger A=\mathbb{1}$ when contracted over their physical and left virtual indices. Similarly, a right-to-left sweep gives right-canonical tensors satisfying $BB^\dagger=\mathbb{1}$. If the two sweeps meet at a chosen site or bond, the MPS is in mixed canonical form: tensors on the left are left-canonical, tensors on the right are right-canonical, and the center tensor carries the nontrivial weight of the state.

This form is especially useful for local expectation values. If the orthogonality center is placed at site $n$, the tensors to the left and right contract with their conjugates to identities. The full network for $\langle\Psi|\hat O_n|\Psi\rangle$ then collapses to a local contraction at the center,
\begin{equation}
    \langle\Psi|\hat O_n|\Psi\rangle =
        \mathrm{Tr}\!\left[ \Lambda^{[n]\dagger}\, \hat O_n\, \Lambda^{[n]} \right].
\end{equation}
Thus, instead of contracting the entire chain, we only need the local tensor carrying the orthogonality center. This is why canonical forms are so central in tensor-network algorithms: they turn many global-looking operations into local ones.

The deeper point, and the one this thesis rests on, is that gauge freedom draws a sharp line between quantities that are physical and quantities that are merely representational. Any object that depends on the individual tensors---rather than on the physical state they encode---can be moved around at will by a gauge transformation, and is therefore not, by itself, a meaningful thing to truncate on. A truncation scheme is only trustworthy if it targets a gauge-invariant object. In the ordinary single-state setting this subtlety is invisible, because the canonical form fixes the gauge and the Schmidt values of the next subsection are automatically the physical ones. In the transverse picture it becomes decisive: as we will see in Section~\ref{app:sub:rtm_truncation}, the reduced density matrix of a boundary state is not gauge-invariant there, whereas the reduced transition matrix built from both boundaries is---and that single fact is what selects the correct truncation for the whole method (Section~\ref{sec:transverse}).



\subsection{Schmidt decomposition and the reduced density matrix}
\label{app:sub:schmidt}

Consider a bipartition cut that divides the chain into two parts: a block $\mathcal{A}=\{1,\dots,j\}$ and its complement $\mathcal{B}=\{j+1,\dots,N\}$. If we group all physical indices in $\mathcal A$ into one composite index and all physical indices in $\mathcal B$ into another, the wavefunction becomes a matrix $\Psi_{(\mathcal A),(\mathcal B)}$. Performing an SVD of this matrix gives the Schmidt decomposition,
\begin{equation}
  \ket\psi = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{\chi} s_\alpha\,
    \ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal A}\otimes\ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal B},
  \qquad s_\alpha\ge0,\quad \sum_\alpha s_\alpha^2=1,
\end{equation}
where the states $\ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal A}$ and $\ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal B}$ form orthonormal bases on the two sides of the cut. The numbers $s_\alpha$ are the Schmidt coefficients, and they are exactly the singular values of the reshaped wavefunction.

In an MPS, this structure is already built into the bonds. If the state is brought into mixed canonical form with the center placed on bond $j$, the center object is precisely the diagonal matrix of Schmidt coefficients across that cut. The MPS bond dimension $\chi$ is therefore the number of Schmidt values kept on that bond. In this sense, the canonical form makes the entanglement structure of every cut explicit.

The reduced density matrix of the block $\mathcal A$ is diagonal in the Schmidt basis,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mathcal A} = \mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal B}\,\ket\psi\bra\psi
    = \sum_\alpha s_\alpha^2\, \ket\alpha_{\mathcal A}\bra\alpha_{\mathcal A},
\end{equation}
so its eigenvalues are simply $p_\alpha=s_\alpha^2$. These probabilities determine the entanglement across the cut, for example through the von Neumann entropy or the R\'enyi entropies.


\subsection{Optimal truncation}
\label{app:sub:truncation}

Truncating a tensor network means reducing some or all of their bond dimensions from $\chi$ to a smaller value $\chi'<\chi$. In the Schmidt basis, the natural way to do this is to keep the $\chi'$ largest Schmidt coefficients and discard the rest. This choice is optimal in a precise sense, given by the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem: among all states $\ket{\tilde\psi}$ with Schmidt rank at most $\chi'$, the truncated state is the closest one to $\ket\psi$ in norm. The corresponding error is simply the discarded weight,
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = \big\lVert \ket\psi - \ket{\tilde\psi}\big\rVert_2^2
    = \sum_{\alpha>\chi'} s_\alpha^2.
\end{equation}
This gives a direct and practical criterion for compression: if the discarded Schmidt values are small, the truncation error is small.

For the states of local gapped Hamiltonians, for instance, the Schmidt values typically decay rapidly, reflecting the limited entanglement expected from the area-law structure discussed above. As a result, keeping only the largest singular values often gives an accurate representation with a much smaller bond dimension. In practice, every truncation step follows this logic. Whenever an operation increases the bond dimension, for example when applying an MPO to an MPS, we perform an SVD and keep only the leading singular values. This trades a controlled and usually very small loss of accuracy for a representation that remains computationally manageable.

Two features of this scheme are worth isolating, because the transverse picture keeps one and loses the other. The first is that the object being truncated, the reduced density matrix, is Hermitian and positive, so its eigenvalues are the (squared) Schmidt values and dropping the smallest of them is unambiguous. The second is the variational guarantee stated above: the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem certifies that this truncation is the best rank-$\chi'$ approximation and bounds its error by the discarded weight. Both features are special to the Hermitian, single-state setting. When we move to the non-Hermitian transition matrix of the next subsection, the eigenvalue--singular-value coincidence and the variational bound are both lost, and the truncation becomes a physically-motivated heuristic rather than a theorem---a trade we make deliberately, and monitor, because of the large gain in efficiency it brings.

\subsection{The reduced transition matrix and its truncation}
\label{app:sub:rtm_truncation}

The discussion so far concerned the truncation of a single state $\ket\psi$, where the reduced density matrix (RDM) provides the natural object to diagonalize. In the transverse picture, the situation is different. The transfer matrix is non-Hermitian, so its dominant left and right fixed points are distinct: a left tMPS $\bra L$ and a right tMPS $\ket R$. The object that must be truncated is therefore not an ordinary reduced density matrix, but a reduced transition matrix (RTM) built from both fixed points.

For a temporal cut that separates the ``past'' steps $\le j$ from the ``future'' steps $>j$, the RTM is defined as
\begin{equation}
  T_j = \frac{\mathrm{Tr}_{\text{future}}\,\ket R\bra L}{\braket{L|R}}.
\end{equation}
Because $\bra L$ and $\ket R$ are generally not related by Hermitian conjugation, $T_j$ is itself non-Hermitian. Its eigenvalues can be complex. They still sum to one, but they are not probabilities, so the usual Schmidt-value interpretation of Section~\ref{app:sub:schmidt} no longer applies directly.

This is exactly the setting that the gauge discussion of Section~\ref{app:sub:canonical_forms} was pointing towards, and it is worth making the pivotal property explicit. Under a gauge transformation $X$ inserted on the cut bond, the two candidate objects behave completely differently. The isolated boundary RDM transforms by a congruence, $\rho_L\to X^\dagger\rho_L X$, which for a non-unitary $X$ ($X^\dagger X\neq\mathbb{1}$, the generic case here) distorts its spectrum---so the Schmidt values of a single boundary state are not physical in the transverse picture, and truncating on them can discard precisely the weight that the global overlap needs. The transition matrix, by contrast, transforms by a similarity, $T_j\to X^{-1}T_j X$, which leaves its eigenvalues untouched. The RTM spectrum, and therefore the generalized temporal entropies built from it, are thus gauge-invariant: they are properties of the physical contraction, not of the representation. This is the precise sense in which the RTM is the ``correct'' object to truncate on, and it is what the production scheme of Section~\ref{sec:transverse} exploits; the concrete cost comparison between the two schemes is carried out there.

This changes the logic of truncation. In the ordinary case, we keep the directions that best preserve the norm of a single state. Here, the physically relevant quantities are instead bilinear overlaps of the form $\braket{L|\mathcal O|R}$. A component of $\ket R$ that has little overlap with $\bra L$ does not contribute much to such observables, even if it looks important from the point of view of $\ket R$ alone. Conversely, a component with small weight in $\ket R$ can still matter if it has a large projection onto $\bra L$.

The appropriate truncation must therefore use the transition matrix itself. The method introduced by Carignano, Marim\'on and Tagliacozzo~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024} performs a biorthogonal decomposition of $\ket R\bra L$ and keeps the eigenvalues of largest modulus. These are the directions that dominate the bilinear form and therefore the physical contractions. Since relevance is measured through the overlap between left and right fixed points, this procedure can retain fewer states than a standard reduced-density-matrix truncation at the same accuracy.

The corresponding cost is naturally measured by the generalized R\'enyi-2 temporal entropy,
\begin{equation}
  S_2^{\text{gen}}(j) = -\log \mathrm{Tr}\, T_j^{\,2}.
\end{equation}
This is the entropy we use throughout the thesis to quantify the temporal entanglement that controls the difficulty of the transverse contraction.

\section{Conformal-field-theory derivations}
\label{app:cft}

This appendix supplies the derivations behind the results quoted in Section~\ref{sec:cft}. It is not needed to follow the main line of the thesis---every formula it produces is stated, and used, in the main text---but it makes the conformal machinery self-contained for the interested reader.

\subsection{Conformal maps and holomorphic factorization}
\label{app:cft:maps}

In two dimensions one packages the Euclidean coordinates $(x,\tau)$ into $z=x+i\tau$ and $\bar z=x-i\tau$, and physical quantities factorize into a holomorphic piece (a function of $z$) and an antiholomorphic piece (a function of $\bar z$); this is the origin of the separate weights $h$ and $\bar h$. Because every analytic map $z\mapsto f(z)$ is a conformal symmetry, a correlation function known on the plane is known on any geometry an analytic map reaches, by substitution. The map we use repeatedly is the plane-to-cylinder (or strip) logarithm $\omega=\tfrac{\beta}{2\pi}\log z$, which wraps the plane onto a cylinder of circumference $\beta$; its time-like continuation ($\beta\to iT$, $\sinh\to\sin$) produces the strip geometry of the quench (Section~\ref{sec:cft:boundary}).

\subsection{Primary operators and their two-point functions}
\label{app:cft:ops}

A primary operator $\phi$ of weights $(h,\bar h)$ has, on the infinite plane, the pure power-law two-point function
\begin{equation}
    \langle \phi(z_1)\phi(z_2)\rangle
      = \frac{1}{(z_1-z_2)^{2h}\,(\bar z_1-\bar z_2)^{2\bar h}},
    \label{eq:cft:plane2pt}
\end{equation}
so the exponent is the scaling dimension $x=h+\bar h$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:dim}. Under a conformal map $\omega=f(z)$ a primary transforms by Jacobian factors, $\langle\phi(\omega_1)\phi(\omega_2)\rangle=\prod_i f'(z_i)^{-h}\bar f'(\bar z_i)^{-\bar h}\,\langle\phi(z_1)\phi(z_2)\rangle$. Feeding in the plane-to-cylinder map turns Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:plane2pt} into the hyperbolic-sine form characteristic of a cylinder of circumference $\beta$,
\begin{equation}
    \langle\phi(\omega_1)\phi(\omega_2)\rangle \;\propto\;
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\omega_1-\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2h}
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\bar\omega_1-\bar\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2\bar h}.
    \label{eq:cft:cyl2pt}
\end{equation}
The time-like version of this expression---a $\sin$ instead of a $\sinh$---is the source of the entropy chord of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:sgen}.

\subsection{The transfer-matrix spectrum from the stress tensor}
\label{app:cft:spectrum}

The carrier of the central charge is the stress-energy tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$, the operator measuring the theory's response to a change of coordinates. Because coordinate changes are the symmetry in 2D, the stress tensor generates conformal transformations, and its modes generate the Virasoro algebra. One mode is special: $L_0$, the generator of scaling, whose eigenvalues on the primaries are their weights $h$---so $L_0$ is the ``dictionary operator'' whose spectrum is the operator content. Quantizing on a strip of transverse size $\beta$, the Hamiltonian generating translations along it is, up to the sound velocity $v$, an integral of the stress tensor, and the conformal map from the plane leaves behind one extra, purely geometric term---the Casimir anomaly proportional to $c$---giving Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir}, $\hat H = \tfrac{\pi}{\beta}(L_0-\tfrac{c}{24})$. Since the transfer matrix is essentially $\mathcal{E}=e^{-\delta t\,\hat H}$, this hands over its entire spectrum in the form of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer}: a dominant eigenvalue whose modulus encodes the Casimir ($c$) term, and a tower of subleading eigenvalues whose ratios and phases encode the operator dimensions.

\subsection{Twist fields and the entropy coefficients}
\label{app:cft:twist}

To obtain the entanglement entropy of an interval $\mathcal A$ one needs the R\'enyi moments $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}$, then continues $n\to1$. The replica trick~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004} computes $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}$ as a path integral on $n$ copies of the plane sewn cyclically along $\mathcal A$; that geometry is equivalent, on a single plane, to inserting a pair of twist operators $\sigma_n$ at the endpoints of $\mathcal A$. The twist operator is a primary of dimension $\Delta_n=\tfrac{c}{12}(n-1/n)$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:twist}), and its two-point function, a power law of the form~\eqref{eq:cft:plane2pt}, gives $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}\sim\ell^{-4\Delta_n}$ for an interval of length $\ell$, hence the logarithm
\begin{equation}
    S_n = \frac{c}{6}\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)\log\ell + \text{const},
    \qquad
    S_{n\to1} = \frac{c}{3}\log\ell + \text{const}.
    \label{eq:cft:cc}
\end{equation}
The R\'enyi order enters through the known factor $(1+1/n)$. For a boundary (half-line) cut, which is the temporal situation of this thesis, the coefficient is halved, giving the family $\tfrac{c}{12}\tfrac{n+1}{n}$---i.e.\ $c/6$ for $n\to1$ and $c/8$ for $n=2$, not the bulk value $c/3$.

\section{Construction of the time-evolution MPO}
\label{app:mpo}

This appendix records the machinery behind the exponential-MPO family of Section~\ref{sec:mporep}: how the connected/disconnected organization is turned into an explicit operator, the auxiliary-boson trick that generates the correct symmetric cross terms, the time-step split that keeps them stable, and the closed-form second-order (VD2) blocks we use in production.

\subsection{Size-extensivity and the connected/disconnected organization}
\label{app:mpo:extensive}

A naive truncated Taylor series $e^{\tau H}=\mathbb{I}+\tau H+\tfrac{\tau^2}{2!}H^2+\cdots$ cannot be used directly in the thermodynamic limit: each power $H^n$ is a sum of $\mathcal{O}(N^n)$ local terms, so successive orders scale with different powers of the chain length $N$ and the result cannot be normalized as $N\to\infty$~\cite{vandamme2024}. Any acceptable construction must be size-extensive---applied to a normalizable state it must return a normalizable state, regardless of $N$. The organizing idea is to expand not by powers of $H$ but by how its local terms overlap along the chain. Terms acting on disjoint regions commute and their product factorizes trivially; these disconnected contributions are harmless and can be kept to all orders at no cost. The difficulty is confined to the connected contributions, where two or more local terms act on overlapping sites and need not commute. For instance an on-site field $D$ may act on the same site where an interaction is initiated by $C$; both orderings $CD$ and $DC$ are then required, since $H^2=(\sum_i h_i)^2$ sums over every ordered pair regardless of commutation, and dropping either would break the Hermiticity that $H^2$ inherits from $H$ (individually $(CD)^\dagger=DC\neq CD$). A suitable time-evolution MPO keeps all disconnected terms exactly, captures connected clusters up to a controlled size, and pushes the truncation error to a chosen order in $\delta t$.

\subsection{The auxiliary-boson trick and the time-step split}
\label{app:mpo:boson}

The simplest construction on this logic, $W^{\mathrm{I}}$, keeps every disconnected term and discards all overlaps; it is built from the blocks $A,B,C,D$ of $W_H$ by distributing the time step across the initiators $C$, the terminators $B$, and the on-site block $D$, keeping the virtual bond at $1+\chi$, and is only first order. The improvement to $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ additionally keeps single-site overlaps. The algebraic obstacle---writing out the correct symmetric combinations of overlapping blocks by hand---is bypassed by a clever device of Zaletel et al.~\cite{zaletel2015}: encode the four blocks in the dynamics of two auxiliary hard-core bosons, exponentiate a small effective Hamiltonian built from $A,B,C,D$ acting on these bosons, and read off the new MPO blocks by projecting the result onto the boson occupations. Because a hard-core boson can be occupied at most once, the exponential truncates on its own and produces exactly the symmetric cross terms required, keeping the on-site term exactly ($e^{\tau D}$) at the same bond dimension $1+\chi$. The Van Damme construction~\cite{vandamme2024} generalizes this auxiliary-space idea to arbitrary order.

Common to all three schemes is the time-step split, which is what keeps them numerically stable. The complex time step $\tau=-i\delta t$ is not divided evenly: we assign a real factor $t_C=\sqrt{\delta t}$ to the initiator block $C$ and put the phase in $t_B=\tau/t_C$ on the terminator block $B$, so that the product $C\otimes B$ still carries the correct factor $-i\delta t$ while the intermediate memory channels remain well behaved.

\subsection{The second-order VD2 blocks}
\label{app:mpo:vd2}

We record the explicit local tensor of the second-order MPO (VD2) used throughout this work. These are the closed-form expressions of Van Damme et al.~\cite{vandamme2024}, specialized to our finite-state-machine blocks; they are what the exponentiation routine assembles for a given memory dimension $\chi$.

We start from the four blocks $A,B,C,D$ of the Hamiltonian MPO $W_H$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:Wannni}): $D$ is the on-site term, $C$ initiates an interaction, $B$ terminates one, and $A$ propagates it. Following the time-step split above, we scale them as
\begin{equation}
  \tilde D = \tau D, \qquad \tilde C = t_C\, C, \qquad \tilde B = t_B\, B, \qquad \tilde A = A,
  \qquad t_C = \sqrt{\delta t},\ \ t_B = \tau/t_C,\ \ \tau=-i\delta t .
\end{equation}
The construction organizes the exponential $e^{\tau W_H}$ not by powers of $W_H$, but by the symmetric products of overlapping blocks that its Taylor series generates. We write $\{X_1\dots X_m\}$ for the symmetrized product, i.e.\ the sum over all distinct orderings of the operator-valued blocks (for example $\{XY\}=X\otimes Y + Y\otimes X$, and $\{XXY\}=XXY+XYX+YXX$), where $\otimes$ is the Kronecker product on the virtual memory index and the ordinary product on the physical index. The multinomial weights $1/2!,\,1/3!,\dots$ of the exponential are carried by the explicit prefactors below.

The resulting local tensor is a $3\times3$ block object (indices $1,2,3$ label the scalar, single- and doubled-memory sectors, which is the origin of the enlarged $1+\chi+\chi^2$ virtual bond),
\begin{equation}
  W^{\mathrm{VD2}} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    W_{11} & W_{12} & W_{13} \\
    W_{21} & W_{22} & W_{23} \\
    W_{31} & W_{32} & W_{33}
  \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
  W_{11} &= \mathbb{I} + \tilde D + \tfrac12\{\tilde D\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde D\tilde D\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{12} &= \tilde C + \tfrac12\{\tilde C\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde C\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{13} = \{\tilde C\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{21} &= \tilde B + \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{31} = \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{22} &= \tilde A + \tfrac12\big(\{\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\}\big)
            + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde C\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\tilde D\}\big), \\
  W_{23} &= \{\tilde A\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde C\tilde D\}+\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde B\}\big), \qquad
  W_{32} = \tfrac12\{\tilde A\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde C\}\big), \\
  W_{33} &= \{\tilde A\tilde A\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde A\tilde D\}\big).
\end{align}
The scalar track $W_{11}$ carries the on-site evolution to third Taylor order; the $W_{12},W_{13}$ entries dress an outgoing memory leg (single and doubled), $W_{21},W_{31}$ the incoming one, and the lower-right block dresses the through-wire $\tilde A$ with all of its symmetric companions. Keeping the doubled-leg (index-3) sector is exactly what reproduces the connected clusters spanning three sites---the next-nearest-neighbour overlaps that $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ misses---and is why VD2 is genuinely second order for our model at the cost of the enlarged virtual bond.

\section{The block power method and its implementation}
\label{app:blockpm}

This appendix develops the algorithm summarized in Section~\ref{sec:blockpm}, together with the driver and bookkeeping that turn it into the production tool used for the sweeps of Section~\ref{sec:results}. The core routine is \texttt{block\_transfer\_eigs}, which we added on top of \texttt{ITransverse.jl}; the surrounding machinery lives in the small library \texttt{transverse\_tools.jl} layered on \texttt{ITensors.jl} and \texttt{ITransverse.jl}. The first half derives the method; the second collects the practical pieces---the unified driver, the adaptive block size, the physical-branch selector, and the warm-start machinery---because several of them encode physics lessons that only became visible while running the calculation. Throughout we reuse the notation of Section~\ref{sec:nonhermitian}: the spatial transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$, its biorthogonal eigenpairs $\langle L_i|$, $|R_i\rangle$ with eigenvalues $\mu_i$ ordered by modulus. Equation numbers are local, (B1)--(B7).

\subsection{The two-sided block and the oblique projection}

We keep $k$ right vectors $|R_1\rangle,\dots,|R_k\rangle$ and, independently, $k$ left vectors $\langle L_1|,\dots,\langle L_k|$, all temporal MPS on the same time sites, seeded with random complex tensors (Section~\ref{sec:powermethod} explains why a random seed is required). One iteration applies the transfer matrix to every member of the right block and its transpose to every member of the left block,
\begin{equation}
  |\tilde R_j\rangle = \mathcal{E}\,|R_j\rangle, \qquad
  \langle \tilde L_j| = \langle L_j|\,\mathcal{E}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  |\tilde L_j\rangle = \mathcal{E}^{\mathsf T}|L_j\rangle .
  \tag{B1}
\end{equation}
The transpose---implemented as a prime-level swap of the MPO's physical legs, with no complex conjugation---is the adjoint with respect to the bilinear form of Section~\ref{sec:nonhermitian}: it is defined by $(\mathcal{E}^{\mathsf T}L)^{\mathsf T}R = L^{\mathsf T}(\mathcal{E}R)$, so left overlaps computed without conjugation remain consistent. Using the Hermitian adjoint $\mathcal{E}^\dagger$ here would be a conceptual error, not a convention choice: it would converge the left block to the dominant eigenvector of $\mathcal{E}^\dagger$, which for a non-normal $\mathcal{E}$ is not the dominant left eigenvector of $\mathcal{E}$. As in the single-vector method, each application multiplies the bond dimension, so every product in (B1) is truncated back to a cap $\chi_{\max}$ and each vector is renormalized.

After the applications, we compress all the spectral information the blocks carry into two small $k\times k$ matrices,
\begin{equation}
  S_{ij} = \langle L_i | R_j\rangle, \qquad
  M_{ij} = \langle L_i | \mathcal{E} | R_j \rangle ,
  \tag{B2}
\end{equation}
both evaluated with the bilinear overlap (no conjugation anywhere; $S$ has no Hermiticity properties at all, and that is fine). $S$ is the metric of the oblique (Petrov--Galerkin) projection---how the left test space and the right trial space are angled against each other---and $M$ is the transfer matrix seen through that projection. Approximations $\theta$ to the eigenvalues of $\mathcal{E}$ (Ritz values) solve the small generalized eigenproblem $M\,v=\theta\,S\,v$.

\subsection{Regularization and exact left pairing}

Numerically we do not hand that generalized problem to a solver. Near the degeneracy the columns of the right block begin to coincide, $S$ becomes nearly singular, and a direct generalized eigensolver responds with infinite or wildly scattered eigenvalues. Instead we regularize with the pseudoinverse,
\begin{equation}
  W = S^{+} M, \qquad W = V\,\Theta\,V^{-1},
  \tag{B3}
\end{equation}
where $S^{+}$ is the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse with a relative tolerance ($10^{-12}$): directions of the block that have effectively collapsed onto each other are projected out rather than inverted. The eigenvalues $\Theta=\mathrm{diag}(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k)$, sorted by modulus, are our estimates of $\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{k-1}$, and the columns of $V$ say which combination of the current right block approximates each eigenvector.

The left block needs its own mixing coefficients, and here hides a trap. One could diagonalize the transposed pencil in a second, independent call---but then nothing guarantees its eigenvalues come out in the same order, and inside a quasi-degenerate cluster ``match each left eigenvalue to the nearest right one'' is ambiguous precisely when it matters: several $\theta$'s sit within a fraction of a percent, the greedy match picks a wrong partner, and the de-mixed left and right vectors no longer describe the same eigenvector. The block then oscillates instead of converging---a failure mode that, before it was fixed, contaminated the near-degenerate gap data. The fix is that no second diagonalization is needed: writing $y^{\mathsf T}=u^{\mathsf T}S$, the left eigenproblem becomes $y^{\mathsf T}W=\theta\,y^{\mathsf T}$, so the $y$'s are the left eigenvectors of the same $W$, i.e.\ the rows of $V^{-1}$. Hence
\begin{equation}
  u_j = \big(S^{+}\big)^{\mathsf T}\big(V^{-1}\big)^{\mathsf T} e_j ,
  \tag{B4}
\end{equation}
paired with $\theta_j$ by construction and automatically biorthogonal through the metric, $u_i^{\mathsf T}S\,v_j=\delta_{ij}$ (up to the regularization). One decomposition, exact pairing, no heuristic.

\subsection{De-mixing, truncation modes, and the Schur basis}

The iteration closes by rotating the applied blocks onto the Ritz combinations,
\begin{equation}
  |R_j\rangle \leftarrow \sum_i V_{ij}\, |\tilde R_i\rangle, \qquad
  |L_j\rangle \leftarrow \sum_i U_{ij}\, |\tilde L_i\rangle ,
  \tag{B5}
\end{equation}
where the MPS linear combinations are formed with an exact direct-sum addition and compressed once at the end (the naive density-matrix addition is numerically fragile for near-parallel vectors). Each new pair is then truncated, in one of two modes mirroring the RTM/RDM discussion of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}: \textbf{RTM mode} truncates the matched pair $(L_j,R_j)$ jointly on its bilinear transition matrix $|R_j\rangle\langle L_j|$---optimal for the physical overlap, far cheaper, but built on the SVD of a non-Hermitian object that becomes ill-conditioned exactly at the degeneracy; \textbf{RDM mode} truncates every vector independently on its Hermitian density matrix---blind to the $L$--$R$ pairing, more expensive, but positive and well-conditioned through the cluster, and the safe fallback when RTM output turns noisy.

One structural weakness remains that no truncation mode removes. Equation (B5) de-mixes onto the eigenvector basis, and for a non-normal matrix that basis itself degenerates as eigenvalues cluster: the condition number of $V$ grows like the inverse gap, and rotating the block onto nearly-parallel directions amplifies every source of noise by that condition number. The remedy, standard in non-Hermitian subspace iteration, is to give up on individual eigenvectors inside the cluster and maintain only a well-conditioned basis of the invariant subspace: we QR-orthonormalize the modulus-sorted coefficient matrices,
\begin{equation}
  V \to Q_V, \qquad U \to Q_U, \qquad (Q^\dagger Q = \mathbb{1}),
  \tag{B6}
\end{equation}
an ordered-Schur-like choice whose leading $j$ columns span the same leading-$j$ eigenspaces while the rotations stay unitary. The Ritz values are still read from the pencil at every step; only the basis carried between iterations changes. In this mode the individual pairs $(L_j,R_j)$ inside a cluster are no longer eigenvector approximations---a price to remember when feeding them into entropy formulas---but the spectrum, and the leading pair when it is isolated, remain clean.

\subsection{Convergence and validation}

Convergence is declared on the movement of the leading Ritz values between iterations, $\max_j|\theta_j^{(n)}-\theta_j^{(n-1)}|<\varepsilon$, with the physical branch $\mu_0$ identified by the modulus-dominance selector described below. The whole construction was validated against an exact dense diagonalization of $\mathcal{E}$ at small bond dimension: on all the models tested, the block-method eigenvalues reproduce the exact leading spectrum to between $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-13}$, confirming that the eigenvalue machinery is correct independently of any power-method comparison.

\subsection{The tMPO builder and the unified entropy driver}
\label{app:library:build}

Above the solver sits a thin, model-agnostic driver, which is what guaranteed that every model in the study was measured by exactly the same pipeline. A single generic constructor, \texttt{build\_tmpo(mp, scheme, T)}, turns any of the model definitions into the rotated temporal network: it assembles the one-step propagator $U(\delta t)$ as an MPO (Appendix~\ref{app:mpo}), performs the $90^\circ$ rotation that maps the spatial virtual bond onto the temporal physical index, and returns the transfer-matrix MPO together with a structured seed tMPS carrying the correct index skeleton. Because the VD2 exponentiation enlarges the spatial bond to $1+\chi+\chi^2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:mpo:vd2}), the temporal physical dimension differs between schemes; the builder reads it dynamically rather than hard-coding it, which lets the same code path serve the WI, WII and VD2 kernels and the Ising, ANNNI-type, and control models without modification. On top of it, \texttt{compute\_entropies(mp, T; \dots)} wires the builder to the block solver and the generalized-entropy routines: it overwrites the structured seed with random complex tensors (a symmetry-special seed converges to a subdominant sector, Section~\ref{sec:powermethod}), runs the power method, trims the $N_\beta/2$ cooling bonds from each end, and returns the per-cut R\'enyi-2 profile together with the leading spectrum.

\subsection{Adaptive block size}
\label{app:library:adaptive}

A fixed block size $k$ is not always enough. The physical first-subleading eigenvalue $\mu_1$ that Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:eq4} needs lives in the same parity tower as $\mu_0$; but at stronger frustration the leading $k=4$ block can consist of $\mu_0$ plus only displaced $\pi$-partners (Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}), with the genuine $\mu_1$ pushed out of the captured window entirely. The wrapper \texttt{block\_transfer\_eigs\_adaptive} detects this---the physical tower gap (below) returns ``not a number'' because no tower member was captured---and escalates the block in steps ($k\to6\to8$), warm-seeding each larger solve with the just-converged smaller block so that the added vectors are the only thing that has to relax. The escalation is decided afresh at every temporal extent, so a better-conditioned rung falls back to the cheap $k=4$ on its own, and the extra cost is paid only where the spectrum actually demands it. Without it, the boundary tower at $p\gtrsim0.8$ is simply unmeasurable---not because the physics is absent, but because it sits outside a too-small block.

\subsection{Phase classification and the physical-branch selector}
\label{app:selector}

The most consequential piece of bookkeeping is which eigenvalue to call the physical $\mu_0$, and it is what most directly controls whether the extracted numbers make sense. Three small phase-based routines do the work. Writing $\mathrm{phase}(z)=\arg(-z)$ for the convention of Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}, \texttt{classify\_tower} splits a block relative to a chosen $\mu_0$ into a tower (every eigenvalue within $\pi/2$ in phase---$\mu_0$'s own conformal descendants) and partners (the rest---the momentum-$\pi$ displaced states of Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}); \texttt{tower\_gap} then returns the physical gap $|\mu_1|/|\mu_0|$ using the largest-modulus tower member only, never a partner, and returns ``not a number'' when the block holds no tower member (the trigger for the escalation above).

The load-bearing routine is \texttt{pick\_phys\_continuity}, and its final form corrects an earlier mistake worth recording because the mistake is instructive. The physical branch must be tracked from one temporal extent to the next, and the obvious rule---take the eigenvalue closest in the complex plane to the previous rung's $\mu_0$---is wrong, in a way that only shows up under frustration. The imaginary part of the leading eigenvalue winds, $\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0\approx a\,v\,T$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3}), so between two rungs the true $\mu_0$ rotates by an amount that grows with the velocity, that is, with $p$. A nearest-complex-value rule then latches onto a stationary near-modulus partner instead of the rotated physical branch; and because the next rung re-anchors on that wrong pick, a single slip cascades down the entire ladder. In the $p=0.5$ arm this appeared as the tracked $|\mu_0|$ collapsing to a spurious value at one extent and then following partners for every later time---plausible-looking output that is entirely an artifact of the selector.

The fix is to anchor on the quantity that is stable rather than the one that winds: the physical $\mu_0$ is the eigenvalue of largest modulus (its $\pi$-partner sits at slightly smaller modulus for every frustration we can resolve, $p\lesssim1$), and continuity is used only to break a genuine sub-percent modulus tie---the exact $\pm\pi$ near-degeneracy---where the two candidates differ by $\approx\pi$ and the physical one is the closer. Re-running the stored $p=0.5$ spectra through this rule---pure post-processing, no recomputation---recovers a flat $|\mu_0|$ and a clean, monotone tower gap. A companion guard, \texttt{phys\_lambda0\_suspect}, flags any rung whose $|\mu_0|$ jumps by more than a set fraction from the previous trusted one; such a jump is the signature of a non-convergence blow-up (a cold-started rung, or a genuinely unresolved degeneracy at $p\ge1$), telling the driver to distrust that point rather than silently anchor the rest of the ladder on it.

\subsection{Warm-starting and crash-safe sweeps}
\label{app:library:cluster}

Two accelerations complete the algorithm and were decisive for reaching long times. Along a ladder of temporal extents $T_1<T_2<\dots$ the converged block at one rung warm-starts the next: the converged tensors are re-indexed onto the longer time-site set and the few added sites are padded with a small random tail, so the iteration only relaxes the new sites, and the bond cap and cutoff follow a schedule---loose and cheap early, tight near convergence. This is not merely a speed-up: because the block near the wall is near-degenerate, a cold random restart at each extent has nothing biasing it onto the physical branch, so warm continuity is what keeps the tracker on the correct tower as the gap tightens. Finally, all sweeps are crash-safe: each $(p,T)$ point is written to a per-label cache as soon as it converges, keyed by parameters, so an interrupted run loses at most the point in flight, parallel jobs never clobber a shared file, and a completed point is never recomputed---which is why the campaign could be run as many independent, resumable jobs across a cluster rather than one monolithic calculation.

\section{The entanglement-barrier wall: exceptional points and its numerical origin}
\label{app:wall}

This appendix collects the material behind the diagnosis of Section~\ref{sec:results:wall}, where the temporal-entropy dome inflates at a well-defined time and the profile stops being conformal. It has two parts: a short account of what an exceptional point is and why it degrades the eigenvectors while sparing the eigenvalues, and the set of controls that establish the wall as a gap-limited feature of the physics rather than a resolution limit of the simulation.

\subsection{Exceptional points of a non-normal matrix}
\label{app:wall:ep}

For a Hermitian operator the eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis and stay well-separated as long as the eigenvalues do; a small perturbation rotates them only gently, by an amount controlled by the inverse spectral gap. A non-normal matrix $\mathcal{E}$ behaves qualitatively differently. Its left and right eigenvectors are distinct objects, paired by the bilinear form $\braket{L_i|R_j}\propto\delta_{ij}$ of Section~\ref{sec:nonhermitian}, and as two eigenvalues approach each other the associated left and right eigenvectors need not stay apart: they can rotate towards a common direction, so that the biorthogonal pairing of the coalescing branch, $\braket{L|R}$, tends to zero even though neither vector vanishes. The limiting configuration, where two eigenvalues and their eigenvectors merge simultaneously, is an exceptional point. There the matrix is defective---it no longer has a complete eigenbasis and cannot be diagonalized at all---and the sensitivity of the individual eigenvector diverges.

The consequence for our measurements is a clean split between what survives and what does not. The eigenvalues themselves are well-conditioned: their first-order sensitivity to a perturbation $\delta\mathcal{E}$ is $\braket{L_i|\delta\mathcal{E}|R_i}/\braket{L_i|R_i}$, which the block method keeps bounded, so moduli, phases, and spectral gaps remain accurate arbitrarily deep into the near-degenerate regime. The eigenvectors, by contrast, carry a condition number that scales as the inverse gap and diverges at the exceptional point, so any quantity built from an individual eigenvector---the temporal entropy in particular, which is read off the dominant biorthogonal pair---loses meaning there. This is the origin of the wall: the entropy dome breaks not because the solver fails to converge but because the object it is asked to converge to becomes ill-defined. The order parameter for the approach is the phase rigidity $r_j$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:rigidity}, which equals one for a near-Hermitian pair and collapses to zero at the exceptional point; its measured collapse across the sweep (Section~\ref{sec:results:wall}) lands precisely where the dome inflates, which is what identifies the two as the same event. We stress that complex symmetry, when a model has it, does not help: every matrix is similar to a complex-symmetric one, so symmetry can relabel the near-exceptional collapse but cannot forbid it, and inside an exactly degenerate subspace the individual eigenvector is not merely ill-conditioned but undefined.

\subsection{The wall is gap-limited, not resolution-limited}
\label{app:wall:eps}

An exceptional point is a property of the transfer matrix, so its location must not move when we change how finely the transfer matrix is represented. We verified this along three independent knobs, holding the physics fixed at $p=0.1$ and varying only the numerical resolution.

First, the bond dimension: halving the cap $\chi$ from $64$ to $32$ leaves the onset of the dome inflation at the same time, so the wall is not a matter of keeping too few states. Second, the truncation cutoff: tightening it by orders of magnitude likewise leaves the onset unchanged. Third, the truncation object itself: switching from the cheap gauge-invariant RTM scheme to the Hermitian, well-conditioned RDM scheme of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation} moves the wall nowhere either---the RDM run, at several times the cost, breaks at the same time as the RTM run. That three qualitatively different refinements all leave the onset fixed is what pins the wall to the spectral gap of $\mathcal{E}$ rather than to any representational shortfall. Consistent with this, phase rigidity itself is a Rayleigh-quotient-like quantity that remains accurate even from a cold start, so the collapse it reports is a robust reading of the physics and not an artifact of the sweep protocol.

The same controls settle the practical question of which truncation to run in production. The RTM and RDM schemes return identical eigenvalues and identical rigidity, so they agree on all the physics; RDM is simply more expensive, running several times slower for no change in the answer. RTM is therefore the production choice, with RDM kept as the well-conditioned fallback for the rare points where the RTM output turns noisy before the wall.

\section{The momentum-\texorpdfstring{$\pi$}{pi} displaced boundary tower}
\label{app:pairing}

This appendix derives the effect that resolves the boundary-exponent puzzle of Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}: why, for any $p\neq0$, the odd-parity boundary tower is displaced in phase by momentum $\approx\pi$, and why free-fermion models never show it. The one-line summary is that the next-nearest-neighbour coupling is a four-fermion interaction of a specific kind, and only such a term can attach a momentum-$\pi$ phase to the odd-parity sector.

\subsection{Jordan--Wigner and the four-fermion interaction}
\label{app:pairing:jw}

Spins are algebraically awkward: two Pauli operators anticommute on the same site but commute on different sites, so a spin is neither a boson nor a fermion. The Jordan--Wigner (JW) transformation rewrites the chain exactly as fermions, at the cost of a non-local string. In the convention matched to our model (field on $\sigma^x$, order parameter on $\sigma^z$),
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^x_j = 1 - 2n_j, \qquad
    \sigma^z_j = (c_j + c_j^\dagger)\,K_j, \qquad
    K_j = \prod_{k<j}(1-2n_k),
    \tag{F1}
\end{equation}
with $n_j=c_j^\dagger c_j$ and the string $K_j=(-1)^{\#\text{fermions left of }j}$, which supplies exactly the signs that make the $c$'s anticommute. Using $(c+c^\dagger)(1-2n)=c^\dagger-c$ and $K_j^2=1$, the four terms of the ANNNI-type Hamiltonian (Section~\ref{sec:annni}) fermionize as follows. The transverse field is a chemical potential, $\lambda\,\sigma^x_j=\lambda(1-2n_j)$, and the nearest-neighbour Ising bond telescopes its strings away to leave a quadratic hopping-plus-pairing term,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^z_j\sigma^z_{j+1} = (c_j^\dagger-c_j)(c_{j+1}+c_{j+1}^\dagger)\quad\text{(free)}.
    \tag{F2}
\end{equation}
These two terms are the pure Ising chain, which is therefore a free-fermion theory---the reason it is exactly solvable and flows to a free ($c=\tfrac12$) Majorana CFT. The two extra terms of the ANNNI-type model are different: both are quartic. The self-dual $XX$ term is a plain density--density interaction, $p\lambda\,\sigma^x_j\sigma^x_{j+1}=p\lambda(1-2n_j)(1-2n_{j+1})$, which is diagonal and moves nothing; but the next-nearest-neighbour bond, whose strings no longer fully cancel, becomes a parity-dressed hop,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^z_i\sigma^z_{i+2} = (c_i^\dagger-c_i)\,\underbrace{(1-2n_{i+1})}_{(-1)^{n_{i+1}}}\,(c_{i+2}+c_{i+2}^\dagger)\quad\text{(quartic)},
    \tag{F3}
\end{equation}
a hop from $i$ to $i+2$ carrying a sign set by the occupation of the site skipped over. This single term is the origin of everything in Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}.

\subsection{Why free fermions cannot displace, and the quantization at \texorpdfstring{$\pi$}{pi}}
\label{app:pairing:kw}

A free-fermion transfer matrix has the normal form $\mathcal{E}=\mu_0\exp(-\sum_m \varepsilon_m \hat n_m)$: independent modes whose moduli and phases are both additive over occupations. An eigenvalue near the top may only occupy modes with $\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon_m\approx0$, and in an Ising-class critical chain those are the long-wavelength modes, whose phases $\pi x/(vT)$ are small. Assembling a lump of exactly $\pi$ at full modulus would require a mode that neither decays nor stops oscillating---a gapless branch at lattice momentum $\pi$---which the uniform Ising-class free chain does not possess. Hence a free chain cannot produce a displaced tower, which is precisely the zero-partner result of the Ising and XY controls (Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}). Only an interaction can supply the momentum-$\pi$ structure, and single-term probes confirm it is specifically the parity-dressed hop~(F3), not the density--density $XX$ term, that does so.

The quantization at exactly $\pi$ (rather than a coupling-dependent value) has a natural home in the model's self-duality. In Majorana variables the $XX$ and next-nearest $ZZ$ terms are both products of four consecutive Majoranas, offset by one Majorana site; the shift by one Majorana site is the lattice Kramers--Wannier duality that exchanges order and disorder, and it maps the two terms onto each other---which is why the self-dual ANNNI-type Hamiltonian must carry both. At the self-dual point the network admits a half-step transfer operation $\mathcal{E}_{1/2}$ with $\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{1/2}^{2}$; a sector whose half-step eigenvalue carries a factor $\pm i$ lands, after squaring, at phase exactly $\pi$---a discrete index rather than a tunable phase, consistent with the displacement being identical across the probed couplings. A first-principles selection of which sector carries the index remains open and is noted in the outlook; what the main text needs is established here---that the displacement exists for every $p\neq0$, is absent for free fermions, and is pinned at $\pi$.

\section{Extraction and equilibrium details}
\label{app:extraction}

\subsection{The sound velocity from the equilibrium dispersion}
\label{app:velocity}

Every temporal formula that converts a transfer-matrix phase into a scaling dimension carries the model's sound velocity $v(p)$ (Equations~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3}--\eqref{eq:cft:eq4}), so it must be known independently. We take it from equilibrium, using Alcaraz's own dispersion relation for this exact self-dual chain: diagonalizing the periodic ring Hamiltonian exactly at small size and reading the velocity off the lowest magnetic-sector excitation. The construction is validated four ways---exact recovery of $v=2$ at $p=0$; a machine-precision match of the from-scratch ring Hamiltonian to the \texttt{OpSum} used elsewhere; agreement between the sector-resolved and sector-blind reads; and, decisively, reproducing Alcaraz's published ground-state central charge $c(p)\approx\tfrac12$ through his separate Casimir formula fed with our $v(p)$. The measured values grow sharply with frustration,
\begin{equation}
    v(0)=1.98,\quad v(0.1)=2.60,\quad v(0.3)=3.80,\quad v(0.5)=4.93,
    \tag{G1}
\end{equation}
and it is these---not a fixed $v=2$---that make the boundary tower of Section~\ref{sec:results:tower} read as clean half-integers at every $p$. That the temporal transfer matrix carries this true, $p$-dependent velocity (and not the bare Ising value) was itself checked with the anisotropic XY control, whose velocity $v(\gamma)=2\gamma$ is exactly known: the pipeline recovers $x_1=\tfrac12$ there only when fed the correct $v(\gamma)$, confirming that the method is velocity-faithful.

\subsection{The branch constant in the eigenvalue central-charge fit}
\label{app:eq3}

The eigenvalue route to $c$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3}) has a subtlety that wrecks the fit if ignored. Because the paper works with $\lambda_0=\log(-\mu_0)$, and $\log(-\mu_0)=\log(\mu_0)+i\pi$, the imaginary part carries a constant $-\pi$ branch offset that the bare fit form omits. Omitting it dumps a spurious $-\pi/T$ contribution into the $1/T^2$ Casimir slot and returns a nonsensical central charge (of order $10^3$). The corrected procedure pins $B=-\pi$ (verified to four digits at the integrable points), fits the three-term form $\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0 = A\,T + B + C/T + D/T^3$ on the de-biased phase, and reads $c=24\,v\,|C|/\pi$ with no stray $\delta t$. This returns $c=0.4997$ on Ising and reproduces the reference Ising/Potts values; the free four-parameter fit with a floating $B$ is unstable on the short pre-wall window, which is why the pinning matters and why the absolute read at $p=0.1$ waits for the longer-$T$ cluster data.
