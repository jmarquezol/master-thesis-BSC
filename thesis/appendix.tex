\section{Conformal-field-theory derivations}
\label{app:cft}

This appendix collects the standard conformal-field-theory results used in Section~\ref{sec:cft}---the chord form of the two-point function, the strip quantization behind the transfer-matrix spectrum, and the entropy coefficients---and sketches where each comes from. Full derivations are in Refs.~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004,difrancesco1997}; what is specific to this thesis is only how the results are used.

\subsection{Conformal maps, the two-point function and the entropy coefficients}
\label{app:cft:maps}

In two dimensions the Euclidean coordinates are packaged into $z=x+i\tau$ and $\bar z=x-i\tau$. A primary operator $\phi$ carries a pair of weights $(h,\bar h)$, its exponents under the two chiral halves of the conformal group, and the combination
\begin{equation}
    x = h + \bar h
    \label{eq:cft:dim}
\end{equation}
is its scaling dimension, the exponent quoted in the main text. On the plane the two-point function is the power law
\begin{equation}
    \langle \phi(z_1)\phi(z_2)\rangle
      = \frac{1}{(z_1-z_2)^{2h}\,(\bar z_1-\bar z_2)^{2\bar h}}.
    \label{eq:cft:plane2pt}
\end{equation}
Because every analytic map is a conformal symmetry, this fixes the correlator on any geometry such a map reaches. Mapping the plane onto a cylinder of circumference $\beta$ with the logarithm map turns the power law into~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004,difrancesco1997}
\begin{equation}
    \langle\phi(\omega_1)\phi(\omega_2)\rangle \;\propto\;
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\omega_1-\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2h}
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\bar\omega_1-\bar\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2\bar h}.
    \label{eq:cft:cyl2pt}
\end{equation}
Continuing to real time, with $\beta\to iT$ and $\sinh\to\sin$, produces the strip geometry of the quench, and the time-like version of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:cyl2pt} is the origin of the chord variable of Eq.~\eqref{eq:chord}.

The same two-point function fixes the entropy coefficients, which is why the two derivations belong together. The entanglement entropy of an interval $\mathcal A$ follows from the R\'enyi moments $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}$, continued to $n\to1$. The replica trick~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004} computes those moments by inserting one twist operator at each endpoint of $\mathcal A$ that lies in the interior of the system; the twist operator is a primary of dimension~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004}
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_n = \frac{c}{12}\Big(n - \frac{1}{n}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:twist}
\end{equation}
For a bulk interval both endpoints are interior, the moments follow the two-point power law $\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}\sim\ell^{-4\Delta_n}$, and the entropy is
\begin{equation}
    S_n = \frac{c}{6}\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)\log\ell + \text{const},
    \qquad
    S_{n\to1} = \frac{c}{3}\log\ell + \text{const}.
    \label{eq:cft:cc}
\end{equation}
The cuts of this thesis are the other case. Each temporal region is anchored at an edge of the strip, so it shares only one border with its complement: a single interior endpoint, a single twist operator, and half the logarithmic coefficient. That gives $\tfrac{c}{12}(1+\tfrac1n)$---$c/6$ at $n\to1$ and $c/8$ at $n=2$---rather than the bulk $c/3$, and using the bulk coefficient by mistake would double every fitted central charge.




\subsection{The transfer-matrix spectrum from the stress tensor}
\label{app:cft:spectrum}

The central charge is carried by the stress-energy tensor, the operator that measures the response of the theory to a change of coordinates. In two dimensions those changes are the symmetry itself, so the modes $L_n$ of the stress tensor generate the conformal transformations; their commutators close into the Virasoro algebra, whose central term is proportional to $c$, which is where the central charge gets its name~\cite{difrancesco1997}. One mode is special: $L_0$ generates scalings, and its eigenvalues on the primary operators are their weights, so its spectrum is the operator content of the theory.

Quantizing on a strip of transverse size $\beta$ makes the anomaly of the main text explicit. The generator of translations along the strip is an integral of the stress tensor, and the conformal map from the plane leaves behind one extra, purely geometric term proportional to $c$~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025},
\begin{equation}
    \hat H = \frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(L_0 - \frac{c}{24}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:casimir}
\end{equation}
This single expression contains both kinds of universal data: the constant $-c/24$ is the Casimir anomaly and carries the central charge, while the spectrum of $L_0$ lists the scaling dimensions. The transfer matrix advances one column of the network, so it is $\mathcal{E}=e^{-a\,\hat H}$ with $a$ the spatial lattice spacing, and substituting Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir} fixes its whole spectrum in the form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer}, with $g=a/\beta$ and $a=1$ in lattice units: a dominant eigenvalue carrying the Casimir term, and a tower of subleading ones whose ratios and phases carry the operator dimensions.

The width entering on the lattice is not purely Euclidean, since the regulator supplies imaginary time at the two ends and the quench real time in between, and the velocity converts both durations into lengths,
\begin{equation}
    \beta = v\,(2\beta_0 + i\,T),
    \qquad
    \frac{1}{\beta} \;\simeq\; \frac{1}{v}\Big(\frac{2\beta_0}{T^2} - \frac{i}{T}\Big),
    \label{eq:cft:continuation}
\end{equation}
the expansion holding to first order in $\beta_0/T$. Substituting it into Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer} gives the eigenvalues of the transfer matrix,
\begin{equation}
    \log\mu_i \;\simeq\; \frac{i\pi}{v\,T}\Big(x_i-\frac{c}{24}\Big)
      \;-\; \frac{2\pi\beta_0}{v\,T^2}\Big(x_i-\frac{c}{24}\Big).
    \label{eq:cft:contexpand}
\end{equation}
The universal data therefore appear in the phases at order $1/T$, and in the moduli only through the regulator, at order $\beta_0/T^2$. Taking $x_i=0$ for the identity gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3}, and the difference between two eigenvalues gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq4}. The moduli are flat to the extent that $\beta_0$ is small, which is the emergent dual unitarity measured in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Universal versus non-universal constants}
\label{app:universal}
\label{sec:numerics:universal}

The predictions of Section~\ref{sec:cft} are exact only in the continuum limit. A simulation instead runs on a microscopic lattice, at finite evolution time $T$, bond dimension $\chi$ and Trotter step $\delta t$, and with a nonzero regulator $\beta_0$. Its measurements therefore mix two kinds of contributions. The universal ones are fixed by the universality class: the central charge $c$ and the scaling dimensions $x_i$, which set every coefficient in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cft:chord}--\eqref{eq:gapclose}. The non-universal ones are introduced by the lattice and the algorithm: the regulator $\beta_0$ and the entropy offset $s_0$ it feeds, the Trotter step $\delta t$, the sound velocity $v$, and the overall normalization of the transfer matrix, which is model dependent and makes the modulus of the dominant eigenvalue differ from one. How well these contributions can be separated determines which predictions can be compared with a simulation and how that comparison should be made.

The sound velocity is one of these non-universal constants, and we have already determined it: the extrapolated $v_\infty(p)$ of Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity} is the velocity entering every conformal formula below. Since the eigenvalue phases depend on the combination $\pi x/(vT)$, it is what converts a measured phase gap into the corresponding universal scaling dimension $x_i$.

This separation is possible because the universal and non-universal contributions affect the spectrum differently. The transfer matrix carries an overall normalization that the lattice construction fixes and the continuum theory does not. Each column of the network is one Trotter layer together with the cooling rows, so its scale depends on $\delta t$, on $\beta_0$, and on the normalization convention chosen for the local \ac{MPO} tensor. Its closed form is not needed: the constant multiplies every eigenvalue alike, so it rescales all the moduli $|\mu_i|$ and leaves the phases untouched. The universal level structure therefore appears in the phases, and we read scaling dimensions from phase differences, using the moduli only through ratios or their limiting behaviour.

Ratios remove more than the normalization. A single phase gap contains a universal dimension, but also depends on the regulator $\beta_0$ and the velocity. The ratio of two gaps,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{Im}(\lambda_2-\lambda_0)}{\mathrm{Im}(\lambda_1-\lambda_0)}
      = \frac{x_2-x_0}{x_1-x_0},
    \label{eq:cft:gapratio}
\end{equation}
is independent of all three at once, since $\beta_0$, the velocity and the normalization cancel between numerator and denominator. It therefore probes the operator content without any external input and provides the cleanest measurement available to us.

Not every quantity can be turned into a ratio of this kind. The entropy slope of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} measures $c$ directly, but at finite $(T,\chi,\delta t)$ the measured slope is not the continuum coefficient,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{slope}(p) = \frac{c(p)}{8}\,K(T,\chi,\delta t,\beta_0) + R(p;T),
  \label{eq:cft:calib}
\end{equation}
where $K$ collects the finite-time, truncation and Trotter corrections that two models in the same universality class share when run through the same pipeline at matched parameters, and $R$ collects the subleading corrections they do not share: their amplitudes involve the operator content and boundary amplitudes of the specific model~\cite{boucomas2026}, so they depend on the coupling. The integrable point of the chain makes the shared factor measurable: there $c=\tfrac12$ exactly, so the measured $\mathrm{slope}(0)$ determines $K$ itself. Dividing the two slopes then cancels $K$, though not $R$,
\begin{equation}
  c(p) \approx \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{slope}(p)}{\mathrm{slope}(0)}.
  \label{eq:cft:calibratio}
\end{equation} The cancellation therefore assumes that the two models share a universality class, which is part of what the measurement is meant to establish, and the residual $R$ sets the uncertainty of the calibrated number, which is why it is a consistency check rather than an independent determination. The second route uses the fact that the functional form of the corrections is itself fixed by the \ac{CFT}~\cite{boucomas2026}, so they can be included explicitly in the fit and $c$ extracted absolutely, without a reference. The costs differ: the absolute route needs a window in $T$ long enough to separate the correction from the leading term, while calibration works at shorter times but needs the reference simulation.

\section{Truncation schemes}
\label{app:tensor_networks}

For the general framework of tensor networks---diagrammatic notation, matrix product states and operators, canonical forms and the standard algorithms---we refer to the reviews of Refs.~\cite{schollwock2011,banuls2023}. This appendix records only the linear algebra behind the truncation choice of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}: why truncating on Schmidt values is optimal for a single state, why gauge freedom decides which spectra are physical, and how the construction generalizes to the \ac{RTM}.

\subsection{Gauge freedom and canonical forms}
\label{app:sub:canonical_forms}

The \ac{MPS} representation of a state is not unique. On any virtual bond one may insert $\mathbb{1}=XX^{-1}$ and absorb the two factors into the neighbouring tensors, $A^{[n]}\to A^{[n]}X$ and $A^{[n+1]}\to X^{-1}A^{[n+1]}$, changing the tensors while leaving the state untouched. The canonical form fixes this freedom: sweeping QR or SVD decompositions inward from both ends makes every tensor an isometry, $A^\dagger A=\mathbb{1}$ from the left and $BB^\dagger=\mathbb{1}$ from the right, with one centre tensor carrying the non-trivial weight. Refs.~\cite{schollwock2011,banuls2023} give the construction in full.

Gauge freedom draws the line between physical and representational quantities. Anything that depends on the individual tensors rather than on the state they encode can be changed at will by a gauge transformation, and is therefore not meaningful to truncate on. In the single-state setting this causes no trouble, because the canonical form fixes the gauge and the Schmidt values below are automatically the physical ones. In the transverse picture it becomes the deciding issue, as Section~\ref{app:sub:rtm_truncation} shows.

\subsection{Schmidt values and optimal truncation}
\label{app:sub:truncation}

Consider a cut dividing the chain into a block $\mathcal{A}$ and its complement $\mathcal{B}$. Grouping the physical indices on each side into one composite index turns the wavefunction into a matrix, whose \ac{SVD} gives the Schmidt decomposition
\begin{equation}
  \ket\psi = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{\chi} s_\alpha\,
    \ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal A}\otimes\ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal B},
  \qquad s_\alpha\ge0,\quad \sum_\alpha s_\alpha^2=1,
\end{equation}
with orthonormal bases on the two sides. In mixed canonical form this structure sits on the cut bond: the centre object is the diagonal matrix of Schmidt coefficients, and the \ac{RDM} of the block is diagonal in the same basis with eigenvalues $p_\alpha=s_\alpha^2$, the probabilities that fix the entanglement across the cut.

Truncating a bond from $\chi$ to $\chi'<\chi$ means keeping the $\chi'$ largest Schmidt coefficients. This is optimal in a precise sense: by the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem, among all states of Schmidt rank at most $\chi'$ the truncated state is the closest to $\ket\psi$ in norm, with an error equal to the discarded weight,
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = \big\lVert \ket\psi - \ket{\tilde\psi}\big\rVert_2^2
    = \sum_{\alpha>\chi'} s_\alpha^2.
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:truncation} shows why the truncation can be performed locally in canonical form.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{imgs/truncation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Why the \ac{RDM} truncation is local.} (a) The canonical condition: a tensor
  contracted with its own conjugate over the physical index and one virtual index reduces to the
  identity on the remaining one. A tensor and its conjugate are drawn as the same shape in a lighter
  and a darker shade. (b) The reduced density matrix at a cut, with the left block open and the
  right block traced. In mixed canonical form the isometries either side of the orthogonality centre
  collapse by (a), so the eigenvalues of the whole object are the squared singular values of the
  single tensor $M$, and the optimal truncation never touches the rest of the chain.}
  \label{fig:truncation}
\end{figure}

Two features of this scheme matter later, because the transverse picture keeps one and loses the other. First, the truncated object is Hermitian and positive, so its eigenvalues are the squared Schmidt values and dropping the smallest is unambiguous. Second, the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem provides a variational guarantee and bounds the error. Both are properties of the Hermitian, single-state setting, and neither survives the move to a non-Hermitian transition matrix.

\subsection{The reduced transition matrix and its truncation}
\label{app:sub:rtm_truncation}

In the transverse picture the transfer matrix is non-Hermitian, so its dominant left and right fixed points are distinct states, and the object to truncate is the \ac{RTM} of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rtm}, built from both. Its eigenvalues can be complex, so the Schmidt interpretation above does not carry over. The gauge freedom also controls how well the two fixed points can be represented at a given bond dimension, since it moves weight between the components that contribute to $\braket{L}{R}$ and those that do not~\cite{tang2025}. Figure~\ref{fig:rdm_rtm} contrasts the reduced density matrix with the transition matrix at the same temporal cut.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{imgs/rdm_rtm.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The two candidate objects at a temporal cut.} Sites after the cut are traced over, so their
  physical legs are contracted between the two rows; the legs before the cut stay open and carry the
  indices of the resulting matrix. (a) The reduced density matrix of a single temporal boundary pairs
  $\ket{L}$ with its own conjugate, and is Hermitian and positive. (b) The \ac{RTM} instead pairs the
  two distinct fixed points $\ket{R}$ and $\bra{L}$, so it is not Hermitian and its eigenvalues need
  not be real or positive.}
  \label{fig:rdm_rtm}
\end{figure}

The choice between the two objects is settled by their behaviour under a gauge transformation. Insert $X$ on the cut bond: the reduced density matrix and the transition matrix respond differently. The boundary RDM transforms by a congruence, $\rho_L\to X^\dagger\rho_L X$, which for the non-unitary $X$ generic here distorts its spectrum. The transition matrix transforms by a similarity, $\mathcal{T}_t\to X^{-1}\mathcal{T}_t X$, which leaves its eigenvalues untouched. Figure~\ref{fig:gauge} shows the difference at the level of the network. The Schmidt values of a single temporal boundary are therefore not physical, while the RTM spectrum is, and truncating on the former can discard exactly the weight the overlap needs.

The gauge argument does not make the \ac{RDM} scheme unusable. As long as one works in a fixed canonical form the gauge is pinned, and the \ac{RDM} truncation is well defined and behaves exactly as it does in any ordinary tensor-network calculation. What the argument establishes is which of the two spectra is a property of the physics rather than of the representation, and therefore which one the \ac{CFT} constrains.

A second question is why the \ac{RTM} scheme truncates on singular values at all, when the \ac{RDM} scheme truncates on eigenvalues. Neither scheme ever diagonalises the exponentially large global object. Canonical forms reduce both to a local $\chi\times\chi$ environment matrix $M_A$ sitting on the cut bond, and the two reductions differ. For the Hermitian case a bipartite cut gives
\begin{equation}
  \rho_B = U_B\,M_A\,U_B^{\dagger},
  \label{eq:app:rdm_env}
\end{equation}
with $U_B$ unitary because it is built from left-orthogonal tensors. Equation~\eqref{eq:app:rdm_env} is a similarity transformation, so $\mathrm{eig}(\rho_B)=\mathrm{eig}(M_A)$: the eigenvalues of the small environment are the eigenvalues of the full reduced density matrix, and one may truncate on them rigorously. For the \ac{RTM}, built from two different states, the same cut instead gives
\begin{equation}
  \tau_B = U_B\,M_A\,V_B,
  \qquad U_B \neq V_B^{\dagger},
  \label{eq:app:rtm_env}
\end{equation}
which is no longer a similarity transformation, so the eigenvalues are not preserved and $\mathrm{eig}(\tau_B)\neq\mathrm{eig}(M_A)$. Fortunately $U_B$ and $V_B$ are still isometries, and isometries preserve singular values,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{sv}(\tau_B) = \mathrm{sv}(M_A).
  \label{eq:app:rtm_sv}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:app:rtm_sv} is what makes the scheme possible: the singular values of the small environment are the singular values of the full object, so they are the quantity we are entitled to truncate on. Using them is not a modelling choice but the only invariant the reduction leaves us.

This last step requires care, though, because singular values and eigenvalues answer different questions. They are related by $\mathrm{sv}(M)=\sqrt{\mathrm{eig}(MM^{\dagger})}$, and for a Hermitian positive \ac{RDM} the two coincide, so discarding the smallest singular values is the same as discarding the smallest eigenvalues. For the non-Hermitian \ac{RTM} they do not, and the quantity we actually need is the trace $\operatorname{Tr}\mathcal{T}_t=\sum_i\lambda_i$, which gives the physical overlap. A trace is a signed sum, so it is not controlled by singular-value magnitudes. The simplest illustration is an \ac{RTM} with the two eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$: both have modulus one, so an \ac{SVD} would rank them among the most important components and keep them, yet they cancel exactly in the trace and contribute nothing to the physical answer.

Truncating the \ac{RTM} therefore lacks the rigorous bound that the \ac{RDM} enjoys, and it would very likely fail on a generic random non-Hermitian matrix. The reason it nevertheless works here is physical rather than mathematical: the transfer matrices generated by a time evolution are highly structured, not generic, and in practice the cancellations that would defeat the criterion do not occur. This is an empirical statement about the operators we meet, not a theorem, which is why we keep the \ac{RDM} scheme available as an independent check.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{imgs/gauge.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Why the \ac{RTM} spectrum is the gauge-invariant one.} (a) A pair $X^{-1}X$ may be inserted
  on every bond at no cost: the two factors cancel, so the contraction equals the ungauged one and
  the Loschmidt echo is untouched, while the individual tensors change. In (b) and (c) only the
  lower time site is contracted; the upper one carries the open indices, so each open index keeps
  one factor and the contracted bond receives both. (b) The \ac{RDM} pairs $\ket{R}$ with its own
  conjugate, the factors meet as $XX^{\dagger}\neq\mathbb{1}$, and the spectrum moves under the
  congruence $X\rho X^{\dagger}$. (c) The \ac{RTM} pairs $\ket{R}$ with $\bra{L}$, the factors meet
  as $XX^{-1}=\mathbb{1}$, and the similarity $X\mathcal{T}_t X^{-1}$ leaves the spectrum unchanged.}
  \label{fig:gauge}
\end{figure}

The canonical form still helps, but in a generalized version, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rtm_truncation}: the condition of Figure~\ref{fig:truncation}(a) is imposed on the pair $\bra{L},\ket{R}$ rather than on a state and its own conjugate. The tensors away from the cut then contract away exactly as before, and the singular values of the \ac{RTM} again follow from a local decomposition, so the two routes cost the same to evaluate and differ only in which object they compress.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{imgs/rtm_truncation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The \ac{RTM} counterpart of Figure~\ref{fig:truncation}.} (a) The generalized canonical
  condition: the contraction pairs a tensor of $\bra{L}$ with the corresponding tensor of $\ket{R}$,
  not with its own conjugate. (b) Imposed along the chain, this condition makes everything before
  the cut contract away, so the singular values of the \ac{RTM} are those of the two tensors at the
  cut alone. The construction is as local as the \ac{RDM} one; what it loses is the variational
  guarantee, since the object is no longer Hermitian.}
  \label{fig:rtm_truncation}
\end{figure}

The logic of truncation changes accordingly. In the ordinary case one keeps the directions that best preserve the norm of a state. Here the relevant quantities are bilinear matrix elements $\bra{L}\mathcal O\ket{R}$. A component of $\ket R$ with little overlap with $\bra L$ contributes little, however large it looks from $\ket R$ alone, and a component with small weight in $\ket R$ can still matter if its projection onto $\bra L$ is large. The scheme used here therefore performs a biorthogonal decomposition of $\ket R\bra L$ and keeps the eigenvalues of largest modulus~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024}, which are the directions that dominate the physical contraction. Because relevance is measured through the overlap, this can retain fewer states than an RDM truncation at the same accuracy.

The stability caveat of Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation} can now be made precise. The singular values of $\mathcal{T}_t$ and its eigenvalues are different quantities, and it is the eigenvalues that enter the contraction, since the trace is their sum. A component with a large singular value can therefore still fail to contribute: an operator with eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$ has both at unit modulus, so an SVD-based truncation keeps both, yet they cancel exactly in the trace. Truncating the RTM on singular values is thus a motivated heuristic rather than a bound.

A second approximation enters through the environment. The truncation at each step uses the local environment available at that point of the sweep, not the full remaining network, because the contraction is built column by column. For a translation-invariant problem this is benign, since every bulk column is the same operator and weight discarded too early is regenerated by the next application. It remains an approximation, and it is one reason the RDM route behaves more predictably when the RTM output turns noisy.

\section{The fermionic form of the NNN interactions}
\label{app:pairing}
\label{sec:annni:jw}

This appendix derives the fermionic form of the model: the \ac{NNN} coupling becomes a genuine interaction after fermionization, which is what breaks integrability for every $p\neq0$.


The effect of the additional interactions becomes explicit after mapping the spin chain to fermions. In the convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni_H}, with the transverse field along $\sigma^x$ and the order parameter along $\sigma^z$, the \ac{JW} transformation~\cite{jordan_wigner1928,fradkin2013} is
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^x_j = 1 - 2n_j, \qquad
    \sigma^z_j = \big(c_j + c_j^\dagger\big)\,K_j, \qquad
    K_j = \prod_{k<j}\big(1-2n_k\big),
    \label{eq:annni:jwmap}
\end{equation}
where $c_j$ annihilates a spinless fermion, $n_j=c^\dagger_j c_j$, and the string $K_j$ enforces the fermionic anticommutation relations.

The two Ising terms remain quadratic under this transformation. The transverse field becomes a chemical-potential term, while the strings associated with the \ac{NN} bond cancel and leave ordinary hopping and pairing. The $p=0$ chain is therefore a free-fermion model and can be diagonalised exactly~\cite{pfeuty1970}. At criticality, its continuum limit is the free Majorana \ac{CFT} with $c=\tfrac12$~\cite{difrancesco1997}.

The terms proportional to $p$ introduce quartic fermionic contributions. The self-dual $\sigma^x_j\sigma^x_{j+1}$ term becomes the density--density factor $(1-2n_j)(1-2n_{j+1})$, which is diagonal in the occupation basis. For the \ac{NNN} bond, the Jordan--Wigner strings do not cancel completely: a parity factor remains on the intermediate site,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^z_i \sigma^z_{i+2}
    = \big(c_i^\dagger - c_i\big)\,\big(1-2n_{i+1}\big)\,\big(c_{i+2}+c_{i+2}^\dagger\big).
    \label{eq:annni:hop}
\end{equation}
This term describes hopping and pairing between sites $i$ and $i+2$, with an amplitude whose sign depends on the occupation of site $i+1$. We refer to this process as parity-dressed hopping. The quartic contributions allow the fermions to scatter, so Wick's theorem no longer reduces correlation functions to two-point functions~\cite{wick1950}. Consequently, the model is genuinely interacting and no longer admits a free-fermion solution for $p\neq0$. The algebra behind these statements is given below.

\subsection{Jordan--Wigner and the four-fermion interaction}
\label{app:pairing:jw}

The main text quotes the transformation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:jwmap} and its action on the \ac{NNN} bond in Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:hop}; this subsection supplies the algebra between the two. The string is what makes the transformation possible at all. Two Pauli operators anticommute on the same site but commute on different ones, so a spin is neither a boson nor a fermion, and no purely local combination of spins can anticommute at a distance. Attaching to each fermion the parity of everything to its left converts the commuting spin algebra into the anticommuting fermion one exactly. The reward is that a Hamiltonian quadratic in fermions is exactly solvable --- diagonalizing a single-particle matrix yields independent normal modes --- which is the reason the plain Ising chain is free and its critical point a free Majorana \ac{CFT}. The working identities below are $(c+c^\dagger)(1-2n)=c^\dagger-c$ and $K_j^2=1$.

The transverse field becomes a chemical potential directly from Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:jwmap}. For the \ac{NN} Ising bond the two strings overlap on all but one site, so they cancel telescopically and leave the quadratic hopping-and-pairing term
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^z_j\sigma^z_{j+1} = (c_j^\dagger-c_j)(c_{j+1}+c_{j+1}^\dagger).
    \tag{F1}
\end{equation}
These two terms make up the pure Ising chain, which is why it is a free-fermion theory, exactly solvable, with a free Majorana \ac{CFT} at criticality.

The two terms proportional to $p$ are both quartic. The self-dual $\sigma^x\sigma^x$ term becomes the density--density factor $(1-2n_j)(1-2n_{j+1})$, diagonal in the occupation basis. For the \ac{NNN} bond the strings cancel only partially: the site skipped over contributes its factor once instead of twice, leaving the parity $(1-2n_{i+1})=(-1)^{n_{i+1}}$ in place, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni:hop}---a hop from $i$ to $i+2$ whose sign is set by the occupation of the intermediate site.

\subsection{Finite-size scaling of the mid-chain entropy}
\label{app:fss}

Section~\ref{sec:annni:equilibrium} reads the central charge from the shape of the entanglement profile at a single system size. A second, independent estimate comes from the size dependence of one point of that profile. At the mid-chain cut the chord takes its maximum, $W(L/2,L)=\ln(2L/\pi)$, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft_obc} collapses to
\begin{equation}
    S_{\mathrm{vN}}(L/2) = \frac{c}{6}\,\ln L + k'.
    \tag{G0}
    \label{eq:cft_mid}
\end{equation}
Running DMRG over a ladder of sizes and plotting the mid-chain entropy against $\ln L$ therefore gives a straight line of slope $c/6$. Over $N=40$ to $200$ this returns $c\approx0.51$ at both $p=0.1$ and $p=0.5$, consistent with the profile fit of the main text and with the Ising value. The ladder uses fifteen DMRG sweeps with the bond dimension ramped to $400$ and a truncation cutoff of $10^{-10}$; the heavier $N=400$ chord fit of Section~\ref{sec:annni:equilibrium} uses fifty sweeps, a ramp to $1000$, and a cutoff of $10^{-12}$. Both ramps start with noise-assisted sweeps.

The same ground states allow a cross-check with the R\'enyi-2 entropy, the member used for every temporal measurement in this thesis. Its mid-chain slope is $c/8$ rather than $c/6$, and a plain linear fit returns $c\approx0.58$: the same universality class, but biased upward. The bias is expected. R\'enyi entropies with $n>1$ carry corrections to scaling that the von Neumann entropy does not, oscillating with the cut position and decaying as a power of the subsystem size~\cite{calabrese2010}, so a straight-line fit that ignores them overestimates the slope. The temporal analogue of this bias is the finite-time correction $\Delta S_n \propto T^{-1/n}$~\cite{boucomas2026}, which decays more slowly as $n$ grows; it is fitted where it matters, in Section~\ref{sec:results:wall}. We do not fit the equilibrium corrections here, because the von Neumann route already measures the equilibrium central charge cleanly, which is why it is the estimator of choice where the full Schmidt spectrum is available. Figure~\ref{fig:cft_L} compares the two finite-size estimates.

\subsection{The sound velocity from the equilibrium dispersion}
\label{app:velocity}

Section~\ref{sec:annni:velocity} measures the sound velocity from the equilibrium dispersion. This appendix gives the construction in full and records the checks behind the numbers quoted there.

The measurement uses a periodic chain, so that momentum is a good quantum number. We build the ring Hamiltonian of Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni_H} at $\lambda=1$ for a small number of sites and diagonalize it exactly. Because the ring is translation invariant, every eigenstate can be labelled by its momentum, which we read off from the expectation value of the one-site translation operator, and by its parity under the spin flip. Degenerate levels come out of the diagonalization as arbitrary mixtures, so we resolve momentum and parity together before assigning labels. Within the magnetic (odd-parity) sector we then take the lowest state carrying no momentum and the lowest state carrying momentum $k$, and the velocity follows from the slope of the dispersion between them,
\begin{equation}
  v = \frac{N}{2\pi}\,\frac{E_1(k)-E_1(0)}{|k|},
  \label{eq:app:velocity}
\end{equation}
with $k$ the smallest non-zero momentum available on the ring. Equation~\eqref{eq:app:velocity} is the lattice form of the linear dispersion $\omega=v|k|$: on a ring of $N$ sites the allowed momenta are spaced by $2\pi/N$, so the factor $N/2\pi$ converts an energy difference between neighbouring momentum levels into the slope of that line. Everything is computed at $\lambda=1$, where the chain is critical and the dispersion is linear at long wavelengths.

The construction is validated in four ways. It recovers $v=2$ at $p=0$, to $1.990$ at $N=18$ and improving with size. The ring Hamiltonian built from scratch matches the operator used elsewhere in the code to machine precision, $7\times10^{-14}$ on the full spectrum at $N=8$. The sector-resolved and sector-blind reads agree at every $p$. Finally, feeding the measured $v(p)$ into Alcaraz's separate Casimir formula reproduces his published ground-state central charge: we obtain $c=0.504$ at $p=0$ and $c=0.525$ at $p=0.5$ from rings of $16$ sites.

The ring sizes that exact diagonalization reaches are not in the thermodynamic limit. At $p=0$ the measurement still returns $1.9899$ at $N=18$, against the exact $2$. That error is not random: multiplying it by $N^2$ gives $3.27$, $3.28$, $3.28$, $3.28$ and $3.28$ at $N=10$ to $18$, so it decays as $1/N^2$. Reaching three-digit accuracy by enlarging the ring would need $N\approx57$, far beyond what can be diagonalized, but the regularity of the error means it can be fitted away instead.

We therefore fit $v(N)=v_\infty + C/N^2$ at each coupling and take $v_\infty$ as the production velocity. Table~\ref{tab:velocity} collects the measurements and the fits, and Figure~\ref{fig:velocity_extrap} shows them. The residuals stay below $2.8\times10^{-2}$ at every coupling, on velocities of order two to eleven, so the assumed form describes the data rather than being imposed on it. The $p=0$ row is the control: the extrapolation returns $1.99994$ against the exact $2$, an error of $6\times10^{-5}$ where the largest ring is still $1.0\times10^{-2}$ away. The correction grows steeply with frustration, from $C=-3.27$ at $p=0$ to $-140.7$ at $p=1.5$, so the extrapolation matters most at the couplings where the raw ring values sit furthest from the limit. It is these extrapolated values, rather than a fixed $v=2$, that make the measured boundary dimensions read as clean half-integers at every $p$.

\subsection{Fit windows and the controls behind the quoted numbers}
\label{app:errors}

The central charges of Table~\ref{tab:cp} come from least-squares fits, and two conventions fix them: which times enter the fit, and what is held fixed. This appendix records both, together with the checks that show how far each number can move.

The window has no unique start, and the choice matters. Moving the first fitted time later shortens the ladder and pushes the extrapolation upwards, because the $1/T$ and $1/T^{2}$ terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3} become degenerate over a short range. Scanning the window start moves the result by $0.014$ at $p=0$, $0.024$ at $p=0.1$ and $0.21$ at $p=0.3$. Every value quoted is taken from the widest window that passes the consistency test below, because that window has the most points and the least degeneracy between the two correction terms. The window ends where the phase of $\mu_0$ stops advancing at a constant rate: over $T=2.2$ to $4.5$ at $p=0.5$ that rate is $1.8326$ with a spread of $0.0008$, and beyond it the rate scatters. That criterion is sharper than any test on $|\mu_0|$ or on the rigidity, and it is what sets the windows in Table~\ref{tab:cp}.

The branch constant of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3} is pinned rather than fitted. Its value is known from the definition $\lambda_0=\log(-\mu_0)$, and where the window is long enough to determine it independently the fit returns $B/\pi=-1.000$, so pinning costs nothing and removes a parameter that a short window cannot constrain. The phase itself is unwrapped over the whole ladder before any window is applied: unwrapping inside a window discards the absolute branch, and the pinned fit is then meaningless.

Three controls bound how far the numbers can move. Repeating a ladder at $\chi=128$ changes the imaginary plateau by about $2\%$ and $|\mu_0|$ by less than $10^{-4}$, while the chord slope of the real part moves by up to $0.12$; this is why the entropy measurements are read from the plateau and not from the slope. Halving the Trotter step, with $N_\beta$ doubled so that $\beta_0$ is unchanged, moves the plateau by under $1\%$ well inside the window and by $18\%$ at its edge. Repeating a single rung with five independent random seeds spreads the plateau by $3\%$ and the chord slope by $12\%$, while $|\mu_0|$ agrees to $1.3\times10^{-4}$, so the spectral route is far less exposed to the seed than the entropy route. The sound velocity enters linearly, and the extrapolation that produces it returns $1.99994$ against the exact $2$ at the integrable point.

For the absolute entropy result of Eq.~\eqref{eq:res:c_entropy_p0} the correction law is a further choice, since it is not known in advance. The plateau is fitted with a constant, with $a+b\,T^{-1/2}$, with $a+b/T$ and with $a+b/T+c/T^{2}$, and the four are compared by corrected Akaike information. Over $T=2$ to $24$ the constant is rejected by a difference of $21$, while the $T^{-1/2}$ and two-term forms differ by less than $1$ and give $0.506$ and $0.511$. Over the short window $T\le11$ the same comparison cannot separate any of the four, which is why the long ladder is necessary.

\section{Construction of the time-evolution MPO}
\label{app:mpo}

This appendix records the construction details behind Section~\ref{sec:mporep}: how the exponentiation is carried out in practice, the two schemes it can apply, the closed-form second-order tensor used in production, and the measurement that justifies it.

\subsection{How the exponentiation is carried out}
\label{app:mpo:code}

The exponentiation is performed with the \texttt{ITensorExpMPO.jl} package~\cite{pokart2024expmpo}, which implements the constructions of Zaletel et al.~\cite{zaletel2015} on top of \texttt{ITensors.jl}~\cite{itensor2022}. The package takes the Hamiltonian in the symbolic form used to build any \ac{MPO} in that library, a sum of operator strings with their coefficients, so the \ac{FSM} of Section~\ref{sec:mporep:fsm} never has to be written out by hand. It walks along the chain, collects at every bond the terms that cross it, and compresses the resulting coefficient matrix with an \ac{SVD}. What survives is the smallest set of memory channels able to carry the interactions across that bond, which for Eq.~\eqref{eq:annni_H} is the three channels of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Wannni}. The blocks $A$, $B$, $C$ and $D$ are then read off site by site and passed to the routine that builds the local tensor of the evolution operator.

That routine is where our own contribution sits. The package provided the first- and second-order operators of Zaletel et al.; we added a mode that applies the same machinery with the second-order recipe of Van Damme et al.~\cite{vandamme2024}, together with the enlargement of the virtual bond from $1+\chi$ to $1+\chi+\chi^2$ that this recipe requires. The addition is available in a fork of the package~\cite{marquezolguin2026expmpo}, and the two repositories document the software details.

\subsection{The two constructions in outline}
\label{app:mpo:outline}

Both constructions solve the same problem. Reaching second order by squaring $W_H$ directly produces cross terms between the blocks, since $H^2$ sums over every ordered pair: both orderings $CD$ and $DC$ of an initiator and a field on the same site must be kept, or the operator loses the Hermiticity it inherits from $H$. Writing out the correct symmetric combinations by hand is impractical. The first construction avoids this by encoding the blocks in an auxiliary space of two hard-core bosons, where the exponential terminates by itself once both modes are filled; exponentiating that small operator and projecting onto the four boson states returns the blocks of $W$ directly~\cite{zaletel2015}. Keeping only the terms with disjoint support gives $W^{\mathrm{I}}$, which is first order, and adding the single-site overlaps gives $W^{\mathrm{II}}$, which is second order provided the Hamiltonian has only \ac{NN} interactions. Both keep the virtual bond at $1+\chi$.

The second construction organizes the same expansion by clusters of overlapping terms rather than by powers of $W_H$~\cite{vandamme2024}. Terms with disjoint support commute and factorize, so they are kept to all orders, and only the connected clusters have to be reproduced explicitly; retaining clusters up to size $n$ leaves a one-step error of order $\delta t^{n+1}$. The second-order member is the operator used here. It carries a doubled memory leg, which is the origin of the virtual bond $1+\chi+\chi^2$, and that leg is what reproduces the three-site clusters generated by the \ac{NNN} bond. The original papers give the derivations; the resulting tensor is recorded below.

All of these schemes share one device, which is what keeps them stable. The complex step $\tau=-i\delta t$ is split unevenly between the blocks: the initiator $C$ carries a real factor $t_C=\sqrt{\delta t}$ and the terminator $B$ the phase $t_B=\tau/t_C$, so the product $C\otimes B$ still carries the correct factor while the intermediate memory channels stay well behaved.

\subsection{The second-order \textit{VD2} blocks}
\label{app:mpo:vd2}

We record the explicit local tensor of the second-order MPO (\textit{VD2}) used throughout this work. These are the closed-form expressions of Van Damme et al.~\cite{vandamme2024}, specialized to our \ac{FSM} blocks; they are what the exponentiation routine assembles for a given memory dimension $\chi$.

We start from the four blocks $A,B,C,D$ of the Hamiltonian MPO $W_H$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:Wannni}): $D$ is the on-site term, $C$ initiates an interaction, $B$ terminates one, and $A$ propagates it. Following the time-step split above, we scale them as
\begin{equation}
  \tilde D = \tau D, \qquad \tilde C = t_C\, C, \qquad \tilde B = t_B\, B, \qquad \tilde A = A,
  \qquad t_C = \sqrt{\delta t},\ \ t_B = \tau/t_C,\ \ \tau=-i\delta t .
\end{equation}
The construction organizes the exponential $e^{\tau W_H}$ not by powers of $W_H$, but by the symmetric products of overlapping blocks that its Taylor series generates. We write $\{X_1\dots X_m\}$ for the symmetrized product, i.e.\ the sum over all distinct orderings of the operator-valued blocks (for example $\{XY\}=X\otimes Y + Y\otimes X$, and $\{XXY\}=XXY+XYX+YXX$), where $\otimes$ is the Kronecker product on the virtual memory index and the ordinary product on the physical index. The multinomial weights $1/2!,\,1/3!,\dots$ of the exponential are carried by the explicit prefactors below.

The resulting local tensor is a $3\times3$ block object (indices $1,2,3$ label the scalar, single- and doubled-memory sectors, which is the origin of the enlarged $1+\chi+\chi^2$ virtual bond),
\begin{equation}
  W^{\textit{VD2}} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    W_{11} & W_{12} & W_{13} \\
    W_{21} & W_{22} & W_{23} \\
    W_{31} & W_{32} & W_{33}
  \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
  W_{11} &= \mathbbm{1} + \tilde D + \tfrac12\{\tilde D\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde D\tilde D\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{12} &= \tilde C + \tfrac12\{\tilde C\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde C\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{13} = \{\tilde C\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{21} &= \tilde B + \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{31} = \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{22} &= \tilde A + \tfrac12\big(\{\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\}\big)
            + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde C\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\tilde D\}\big), \\
  W_{23} &= \{\tilde A\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde C\tilde D\}+\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde B\}\big), \qquad
  W_{32} = \tfrac12\{\tilde A\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde C\}\big), \\
  W_{33} &= \{\tilde A\tilde A\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde A\tilde D\}\big).
\end{align}
The scalar track $W_{11}$ carries the on-site evolution to third Taylor order; the $W_{12},W_{13}$ entries dress an outgoing memory leg (single and doubled), $W_{21},W_{31}$ the incoming one, and the lower-right block dresses the through-wire $\tilde A$ with all of its symmetric companions. Keeping the doubled-leg (index-3) sector is exactly what reproduces the connected clusters spanning three sites---the \ac{NNN} overlaps that $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ misses---and is why \textit{VD2} is genuinely second order for our model at the cost of the enlarged virtual bond.

\subsection{Comparing the two constructions in practice}
\label{app:mpo:comparison}

The claim that $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ degrades to first order for a Hamiltonian with \ac{NNN} interactions, while \textit{VD2} remains second order, can be checked directly on the model of this thesis. A convenient diagnostic is the loss of unitarity, since the exact $e^{-iH\delta t}$ preserves the norm of any state: applying successive layers of the approximate operator to $\ket{X+}^{\otimes N}$ and monitoring $\lVert\psi\rVert$ isolates the Trotter error, because a perfect construction would leave the norm at one.

Applying eight layers at $\delta t=0.05$ to a chain of $N=100$ sites at $p=0.1$ gives the following norms:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
 & $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ & $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ & \textit{VD2} \\
\hline
virtual bond & $4$ & $4$ & $13$ \\
$\lVert\psi\rVert$ after 1 layer & $1.3514$ & $1.1341$ & $0.99995$ \\
$\lVert\psi\rVert$ after 8 layers & $12.6146$ & $3.0181$ & $0.99923$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The separation is unambiguous. The first-order $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ inflates the norm by an order of magnitude within eight steps, and $W^{\mathrm{II}}$, which would be effectively second order for a strictly \ac{NN} chain, still triples it: the connected clusters generated by the \ac{NNN} bridge are precisely the ones it fails to capture. \textit{VD2} holds the norm to within $8\times10^{-4}$ over the same interval.

This is what justifies paying for the larger virtual bond. Since the transverse contraction needs long temporal chains, and reducing $\delta t$ to compensate a first-order error would lengthen them further and add truncation error at every extra site, a genuinely second-order operator at a moderate time step is the configuration that makes the calculation feasible at all.

\section{Implementation of the block power method}
\label{app:blockpm}

This appendix documents the implementation of the block power method of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm}. The core routine is \texttt{block\_transfer\_eigs}, built on top of \texttt{ITransverse.jl}~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}; all code, notebooks and cluster drivers are in the public repository for this thesis~\cite{marquezolguin2026code}.

\subsection{The regularized solve and the left pairing}
\label{app:blockpm:pairing}

Near a degeneracy the columns of the right block begin to coincide and the metric $S$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_pencil} becomes nearly singular. A generalized eigensolver applied to the pencil $Mv=\theta\,Sv$ would then return infinite or scattered values, so we regularize it with the pseudoinverse instead,
\begin{equation}
  W = S^{+} M, \qquad W = V\,\Theta\,V^{-1},
  \tag{D1}
\end{equation}
where $S^{+}$ carries a relative tolerance of $10^{-12}$: directions that have effectively collapsed onto each other are projected out rather than inverted. The eigenvalues $\Theta$, sorted by modulus, estimate $\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{k-1}$, and the columns of $V$ give the right mixing coefficients.

The left block needs its own coefficients, and obtaining them from a second diagonalization is unreliable: the transposed pencil returns its eigenvalues in no guaranteed order, and inside a quasi-degenerate cluster the matching of left to right eigenvalues becomes ambiguous exactly when it matters, so the paired vectors can stop describing the same eigenvector and the block oscillates instead of converging. Fortunately, no second diagonalization is needed. Writing $y^{\mathsf T}=u^{\mathsf T}S$, the left problem becomes $y^{\mathsf T}W=\theta\,y^{\mathsf T}$, so the $y$ are the rows of $V^{-1}$ and
\begin{equation}
  u_j = \big(S^{+}\big)^{\mathsf T}\big(V^{-1}\big)^{\mathsf T} e_j ,
  \tag{D2}
\end{equation}
paired with $\theta_j$ by construction and biorthogonal through the metric, $u_i^{\mathsf T}S\,v_j=\delta_{ij}$.

One implementation note concerns the de-mixing. The MPS linear combinations of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm} are formed by exact direct-sum addition and compressed once at the end, because the default density-matrix addition is fragile for the near-parallel vectors that a tight cluster produces.

\subsection{The eigenvalue-only mode}
\label{app:blockpm:schur}

The eigenvalue-only mode of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm} replaces the de-mixing coefficients by orthonormal ones. After the modulus sorting, the coefficient matrices are QR-decomposed,
\begin{equation}
  V \to Q_V, \qquad U \to Q_U, \qquad Q^\dagger Q = \mathbb{1},
  \tag{D3}
\end{equation}
and the blocks are rotated with $Q_V$ and $Q_U$ instead of $V$ and $U$. The leading $j$ columns of each $Q$ span the same leading subspaces as those of the sorted coefficient matrices, so this is an ordered-Schur-like choice. The Ritz values are unchanged because recombining the blocks by any invertible $B$ and $C$ sends $S\to B^{\mathsf T}SC$ and $M\to B^{\mathsf T}MC$, which leaves the solutions of the projected pencil untouched; the two bases differ only in how well conditioned they are when truncated. What the rotation does change is the conditioning of what is truncated next: $\mathrm{cond}(V)$ grows like the inverse of the gap inside the block, while $Q_V$ and $Q_U$ are unitary by construction. The two modes can therefore drift apart only through the truncation, and in practice they barely do. Two independent ladders at $p=0.1$ and $\chi=64$, one carried in each basis, agree on the two tracked eigenvalues to between $10^{-7}$ and $10^{-4}$ in relative modulus over $T=2$ to $8$; the untracked members agree only to $10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$, which is expected since the convergence test never monitors them.

Three safeguards complete the iteration in either mode. Every block member is renormalized after each de-mixing, and a member whose norm underflows or overflows is replaced by a fresh random vector. The condition number of $S$ is monitored: a large value means two block directions have effectively merged, and the last member is then replaced by a random vector bi-orthogonalized against the rest with the bilinear form. Convergence is measured on the leading Ritz values (the leading two in production) after matching each value to its nearest predecessor from the previous iteration, since the frequent modulus-order swaps between near-degenerate eigenvalues would otherwise register as spurious jumps; the iteration stops when the matched movement falls below $10^{-6}$, or when it has stopped improving for a set number of iterations.

\subsection{Validation against exact diagonalization}
\label{app:blockpm:validation}

\subsection{Computational cost}
\label{app:blockpm:cost}

One iteration of the block method contains three kinds of tensor-network work. The application step performs $2k$ \ac{tMPO}--\ac{tMPS} products, each growing the temporal bond from $\chi$ to $D\chi$ before compression. The projection step evaluates the $k^2$ bilinear overlaps of $S$ and $M$; no state grows there, and the $k\times k$ dense algebra is negligible. The de-mixing step forms $2k$ linear combinations of $k$ states each. Every combination is built as an exact direct sum, which raises the bond dimension to $k\chi$ before the compression sweep, so both the memory peak of an iteration and most of its work sit in this step. Profiling a production rung ($\chi=64$, $k=4$, $p=0.1$) attributes roughly $85\%$ of the iteration time to the de-mixing sums and their compression sweeps.

Two adjustments reduce this cost without changing what the method converges to. The running direct sum is compressed progressively, each partial sum truncated back to the production bond dimension, with the final addition left exact. And the stagnation patience is shortened, since at late rungs the iteration spends long plateaus below the convergence threshold before the stop triggers. Together the two halve the wall-clock time of the longest rungs while moving $|\mu_0|$ by $4\times10^{-6}$ relative. The mechanisms that reduce the number of iterations, rather than the cost of each one, are described next.

One conceptual point about the truncation belongs here. Each truncation uses only the current states, not the columns of the network still to be absorbed, so a component discarded now could in principle matter later. Translation invariance keeps this harmless: every subsequent column applies the same operator $\mathcal{E}$, which regenerates any part of the leading invariant subspace that has been trimmed away.

\subsection{Production options}
\label{app:library:build}
\label{app:library:cluster}
\label{app:library:adaptive}

A thin, model-agnostic builder sits above the solver. It assembles the one-step propagator as an MPO (Appendix~\ref{app:mpo}), performs the $90^\circ$ rotation that maps the spatial virtual bond onto the temporal physical index, and returns the transfer-matrix MPO with a seed tMPS carrying the right index skeleton. The seed is always overwritten with random complex tensors before the iteration, because a symmetry-special seed converges to a subdominant sector. The temporal physical dimension is read from the operator rather than hard-coded, which is what lets the same code path serve the $W^{\mathrm{I}}$, $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ and \textit{VD2} kernels and every model without modification.

The options that control a production run are the following; none changes what the method converges to.

\textit{Warm starting.} Along a ladder of evolution times, the converged block at one time is re-indexed onto the longer time-site set of the next and padded with a small random tail, so the iteration only has to relax the newly added sites. The bond cap and the cutoff follow a schedule that is loose early and tight near convergence: the cap grows in steps of two up to $\chi$, and the cutoff holds at $10^{-8}$ for the first forty iterations before tightening to $10^{-10}$. Warm continuity also stabilises the selection along the ladder, since consecutive rungs then start from the same leading subspace.

\textit{Block-size escalation.} At stronger frustration the leading $k=4$ block can fill with members of a single near-degenerate cluster, pushing the subleading eigenvalue needed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq4} outside the captured window. The driver detects this and escalates the block in steps ($k=4\to6\to8$), warm-seeding each larger solve with the converged smaller block, so the extra cost is paid only where the spectrum demands it.

\textit{Truncation scheme.} The truncated object is selectable between \ac{RTM} and \ac{RDM} (Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}), with RTM the production default and RDM the well-conditioned fallback.

\textit{Crash safety.} Each $(p,T)$ point is written to a per-label cache as soon as it converges, keyed by its parameters, so an interrupted run loses at most the point in flight and a completed point is never recomputed. A separate checkpoint stores the last converged block, so a resubmitted run resumes warm.

\subsection{Selecting the dominant eigenvalue}
\label{app:selector}

The production rule is the one stated in Section~\ref{sec:reach}: the eigenvalue of largest modulus, with continuity against the preceding rung breaking ties below one per cent. Two safeguards protect a ladder built on this rule. A rung whose selected $|\mu_0|$ jumps by more than a prescribed fraction relative to the last trusted value is flagged, since such a jump signals unreliable convergence rather than physics. A flagged rung is not discarded: the physical eigenvalue usually survives further down the block, so a post-processing pass restricts the candidates to a window of five per cent around the last trusted modulus and applies dominance inside it, chaining the reference from the last accepted point. This repairs affected rungs from the stored spectra alone; on the one rung where an independently converged run exists for comparison, the repaired eigenvalue agrees with it to $3\times10^{-5}$ relative. The subleading members are then ordered by their phase distance from $\mu_0$ and matched to the conformal tower rung by rung.

Finally, whenever a limitation is observed, we test whether it is physical or resolution dependent by repeating representative calculations at half the bond dimension, with a tighter truncation cutoff, and with \ac{RDM} rather than \ac{RTM} truncation. A feature is attributed to the transfer matrix only if it persists under all three changes.

\subsection{Why eigenvalues outlive eigenvectors}
\label{app:conditioning}

Section~\ref{sec:reach} states that eigenvalues remain accurate where eigenvectors do not. Both halves of that statement follow from first-order perturbation theory for a non-Hermitian matrix~\cite{wilkinson1965}. Perturb $\mathcal{E}$ by $\delta\mathcal{E}$ and expand the perturbed eigenvector as $\ket{R_i}+\sum_{j\neq i}a_{ij}\ket{R_j}$. Projecting the eigenvalue equation onto $\bra{L_i}$ gives the shift of the eigenvalue,
\begin{equation}
  \delta\mu_i=
  \frac{\bra{L_i}\delta\mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}}
       {\braket{L_i}{R_i}},
  \label{eq:eigenvalue_perturbation}
\end{equation}
and projecting it onto $\bra{L_j}$ with $j\neq i$ gives the mixing coefficients,
\begin{equation}
  a_{ij}=
  \frac{\bra{L_j}\delta\mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}}
       {(\mu_i-\mu_j)\,\braket{L_j}{R_j}} .
  \label{eq:eigenvector_perturbation}
\end{equation}
The two expressions differ in one respect that matters here. Equation~\eqref{eq:eigenvalue_perturbation} contains no eigenvalue difference, so an eigenvalue stays accurate as long as its own overlap $\braket{L_i}{R_i}$ stays finite. Equation~\eqref{eq:eigenvector_perturbation} is divided by $\mu_i-\mu_j$, so the eigenvector mixes with its neighbours as soon as two eigenvalues approach, and the de-mixing that separates the block members becomes correspondingly harder.

The size of the denominator in Eq.~\eqref{eq:eigenvalue_perturbation} is measured by the phase rigidity of a biorthogonal pair,
\begin{equation}
    r_j = \frac{|\braket{L_j}{R_j}|}{\lVert L_j\rVert\,\lVert R_j\rVert},
    \label{eq:rigidity}
\end{equation}
the normalized overlap between the left and right eigenvector of the same eigenvalue: it equals one for a Hermitian matrix and falls towards zero as the two rotate towards a common direction. The same expression therefore bounds how far the protection extends, since an eigenvalue tied to an individual eigenvector loses accuracy as its overlap falls. What stays well conditioned is the leading subspace as a whole, whose separation from the rest of the spectrum governs the block iteration. Our eigenvalue-only mode reads the spectrum at that level: it never assigns block members to separate eigenvectors, but carries an orthonormal basis of the subspace and takes the Ritz values from the projected pencil. The eigenvalues therefore behave better than the eigenvectors not because they are immune, but because the subspace-level read avoids the ill-conditioned de-mixing that the eigenvector route cannot do without.

One further diagnostic belongs here. The spectral ratio $|\mu_1/\mu_0|$ controls the suppression of the first subleading contribution in Eq.~\eqref{eq:contraction}, but it is not a convergence criterion for the block, which is governed instead by the separation of the whole leading subspace from the rest of the spectrum. The two differ near a degeneracy inside the block, where $|\mu_1/\mu_0|$ may approach one while the subspace stays well separated and its eigenvalues keep converging.
