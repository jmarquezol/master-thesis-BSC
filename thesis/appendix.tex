\section{Fundamentals of Tensor Networks and Optimal Truncation}
\label{app:tensor_networks}

For $N$ spin-$1/2$ particles the Hilbert space has dimension $2^N$, so a general wavefunction needs
exponentially many coefficients. Tensor networks work because physically relevant states rarely use
all of that space. The ground states of local gapped Hamiltonians obey an area law, in which the
entanglement across a cut grows with the boundary of the subsystem rather than its volume, and such
states are well represented by a network of small tensors joined by bonds of controlled dimension.

This appendix builds only what is needed for one specific question. Every method in this thesis
repeatedly truncates a tensor network, and the decisive choice is which object to truncate. In the
Schr\"odinger picture it is the reduced density matrix of a single state. In the transverse picture,
where the left and right fixed points of the transfer matrix differ, it is a reduced transition
matrix built from both. What separates them is gauge freedom, and the last subsection shows why.

\subsection{Notation and basic operations}
\label{app:sub:graphical_notation}

Two conventions are used without comment in the main text. The first is the diagrammatic notation. A
tensor is drawn as a shape with one leg per index, so a scalar has none, a vector one and a matrix
two. A contraction, meaning a sum over a shared index, joins the two corresponding legs, and the
legs left open are the free indices of the result. The picture makes the connectivity of a network
immediate, and with it the order in which the tensors are best contracted.

The second is reshaping. Linear-algebra routines act on matrices, so a higher-rank tensor is grouped
into one by treating several indices as a single composite index, for instance
$A_{i,\alpha,\beta}\to M_{(i\alpha),\beta}$. This relabels the entries without changing them, and it
is what allows matrix tools to be used inside a tensor network.

\subsection{The singular value decomposition}
\label{app:sub:svd}

The \ac{SVD} is the central tool of tensor-network algorithms. Any matrix $M$ of dimension
$D_A \times D_B$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
    M = U S V^\dagger,
\end{equation}
where $U$ has orthonormal columns, $V^\dagger$ has orthonormal rows, and $S$ is diagonal with
non-negative entries ordered as $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$.

In tensor-network language the SVD splits one tensor into two, joined by a new internal index whose
dimension is the number of non-zero singular values. It therefore does two things at once: it
factorizes the tensor, and it says how large the new bond must be to represent it exactly.

The ordering of the singular values is what makes this useful. Large values carry the most important
components across the chosen bipartition and small ones carry little weight, so discarding the
smallest reduces the bond dimension while keeping the dominant correlations. This is the basis of
every truncation in this work.

\subsection{Matrix product states and operators}
\label{app:sub:mps_mpo}

An \ac{MPS} follows from applying the SVD repeatedly along a chain. A general state on $N$ sites is
\begin{equation}
    |\Psi\rangle = \sum_{i_1,\dots,i_N} C_{i_1\dots i_N}\,|i_1,\dots,i_N\rangle,
\end{equation}
whose coefficient tensor has $d^N$ entries and cannot be stored directly for large $N$. Sweeping an
SVD through the chain factorizes it exactly into
\begin{equation}
    C_{i_1\dots i_N} = \sum_{\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_{N-1}}
        A^{[1]}_{i_1,\alpha_1} A^{[2]}_{i_2,\alpha_1,\alpha_2}
        \cdots A^{[N]}_{i_N,\alpha_{N-1}},
\end{equation}
where each tensor carries one physical index and, away from the boundaries, two virtual indices.

The dimensions of the virtual indices are the bond dimensions, and they fix how much correlation the
state can carry across each cut. At $\chi=1$ the state is a product state with no entanglement;
larger $\chi$ allows more entangled states. The factorization is exact if the bonds may grow freely,
and the approximation enters only when a maximum is imposed and the smaller singular values are
discarded.

An \ac{MPO} applies the same idea to operators: a chain of tensors with two physical indices, one
incoming and one outgoing, joined by virtual indices. Applying an MPO to an MPS multiplies the bond
dimensions of the two, which is the main reason truncation is needed at all.

\subsection{Gauge freedom and canonical forms}
\label{app:sub:canonical_forms}

The MPS representation is not unique. On any virtual bond we may insert $\mathbb{1}=XX^{-1}$ and
absorb the factors into the neighbouring tensors,
\begin{equation}
    A^{[n]} \to A^{[n]}X, \qquad A^{[n+1]} \to X^{-1}A^{[n+1]}.
\end{equation}
This changes the tensors but leaves the physical state untouched. The freedom is called gauge
freedom, and it lets us pick whichever representation suits the calculation.

The standard choice is the canonical form. Sweeping QR or SVD decompositions from the left produces
left-canonical tensors, satisfying $A^\dagger A=\mathbb{1}$ when contracted over the physical and
left virtual indices; sweeping from the right gives right-canonical tensors with
$BB^\dagger=\mathbb{1}$. If the two sweeps meet at one site, the state is in mixed canonical form,
with the centre tensor carrying all the non-trivial weight.

This form makes local expectation values cheap. With the orthogonality centre at site $n$, every
tensor to its left and right contracts with its conjugate to the identity, and the whole network
collapses to
\begin{equation}
    \langle\Psi|\hat O_n|\Psi\rangle =
        \mathrm{Tr}\!\left[ \Lambda^{[n]\dagger}\, \hat O_n\, \Lambda^{[n]} \right].
\end{equation}
Canonical forms are central to tensor-network algorithms for exactly this reason: they turn
global-looking operations into local ones.

Gauge freedom also draws the line between physical and representational quantities. Anything that
depends on the individual tensors, rather than on the state they encode, can be changed at will by a
gauge transformation, and is therefore not meaningful to truncate on. In the ordinary single-state
setting this never causes trouble, because the canonical form fixes the gauge and the Schmidt values
below are automatically the physical ones. In the transverse picture it becomes the deciding issue,
as Section~\ref{app:sub:rtm_truncation} shows.

\subsection{Schmidt decomposition and the reduced density matrix}
\label{app:sub:schmidt}

Consider a cut dividing the chain into a block $\mathcal{A}=\{1,\dots,j\}$ and its complement
$\mathcal{B}$. Grouping the physical indices on each side into one composite index turns the
wavefunction into a matrix, whose SVD gives the Schmidt decomposition
\begin{equation}
  \ket\psi = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{\chi} s_\alpha\,
    \ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal A}\otimes\ket{\alpha}_{\mathcal B},
  \qquad s_\alpha\ge0,\quad \sum_\alpha s_\alpha^2=1,
\end{equation}
with orthonormal bases on the two sides. The $s_\alpha$ are the Schmidt coefficients, and they are
the singular values of the reshaped wavefunction.

In an MPS this structure sits on the bonds. In mixed canonical form with the centre on bond $j$, the
centre object is the diagonal matrix of Schmidt coefficients across that cut, so the bond dimension
is the number of Schmidt values kept there. The reduced density matrix of the block is diagonal in
the same basis,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mathcal A} = \mathrm{Tr}_{\mathcal B}\,\ket\psi\bra\psi
    = \sum_\alpha s_\alpha^2\, \ket\alpha_{\mathcal A}\bra\alpha_{\mathcal A},
\end{equation}
with eigenvalues $p_\alpha=s_\alpha^2$. These are the probabilities that fix the entanglement across
the cut.

\subsection{Optimal truncation}
\label{app:sub:truncation}

Truncating means reducing a bond dimension from $\chi$ to some $\chi'<\chi$. In the Schmidt basis one
keeps the $\chi'$ largest coefficients and discards the rest. This is optimal in a precise sense: by
the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem, among all states of Schmidt rank at most $\chi'$ the truncated
state is the closest to $\ket\psi$ in norm, with an error equal to the discarded weight,
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon = \big\lVert \ket\psi - \ket{\tilde\psi}\big\rVert_2^2
    = \sum_{\alpha>\chi'} s_\alpha^2.
\end{equation}
The criterion is therefore direct: if the discarded Schmidt values are small, so is the error. For
ground states of local gapped Hamiltonians the Schmidt values decay quickly, which is why a modest
bond dimension usually suffices. Figure~\ref{fig:truncation} shows why this truncation can be
performed locally in canonical form.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{imgs/truncation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Why the \ac{RDM} truncation is local.} (a) The canonical condition: a tensor
  contracted with its own conjugate over the physical index and one virtual index reduces to the
  identity on the remaining one. A tensor and its conjugate are drawn as the same shape in a lighter
  and a darker shade. (b) The reduced density matrix at a cut, with the left block open and the
  right block traced. In mixed canonical form the isometries either side of the orthogonality centre
  collapse by (a), so the eigenvalues of the whole object are the squared singular values of the
  single tensor $M$, and the optimal truncation never touches the rest of the chain.}
  \label{fig:truncation}
\end{figure}

Two features of this scheme matter later, because the transverse picture keeps one and loses the
other. First, the truncated object is Hermitian and positive, so its eigenvalues are the squared
Schmidt values and dropping the smallest is unambiguous. Second, the Eckart--Young--Mirsky theorem
provides a variational guarantee and bounds the error. Both are properties of the Hermitian,
single-state setting, and neither survives the move to a non-Hermitian transition matrix.

\subsection{The reduced transition matrix and its truncation}
\label{app:sub:rtm_truncation}

Everything so far concerned a single state $\ket\psi$, for which the reduced density matrix is the
natural object. The transverse picture is different. The transfer matrix is non-Hermitian, so its
dominant left and right fixed points are distinct states, a left tMPS $\bra L$ and a right tMPS
$\ket R$. What has to be truncated is built from both.

For a temporal cut at $t$, separating the steps before it from those after, the \ac{RTM} is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{T}_t = \frac{\mathrm{Tr}_{T-t}\,\ket R\bra L}{\braket{L}{R}}.
\end{equation}
Since $\bra L$ is not the conjugate of $\ket R$, this object is non-Hermitian and its eigenvalues can
be complex. They still sum to one, but they are not probabilities, so the Schmidt interpretation of
the previous subsections does not carry over. Figure~\ref{fig:rdm_rtm} contrasts the reduced density
matrix with the transition matrix at the same temporal cut.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{imgs/rdm_rtm.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The two candidate objects at a temporal cut.} Sites after the cut are traced over, so their
  physical legs are contracted between the two rows; the legs before the cut stay open and carry the
  indices of the resulting matrix. (a) The reduced density matrix of a single temporal boundary pairs
  $\ket{L}$ with its own conjugate, and is Hermitian and positive. (b) The \ac{RTM} instead pairs the
  two distinct fixed points $\ket{R}$ and $\bra{L}$, so it is not Hermitian and its eigenvalues need
  not be real or positive.}
  \label{fig:rdm_rtm}
\end{figure}

This is where the gauge argument becomes decisive. Insert a gauge transformation $X$ on the cut bond
and the two candidate objects respond in opposite ways. The boundary RDM transforms by a congruence,
$\rho_L\to X^\dagger\rho_L X$, which for the non-unitary $X$ generic here distorts its spectrum. The
transition matrix transforms by a similarity, $\mathcal{T}_t\to X^{-1}\mathcal{T}_t X$, which leaves its eigenvalues
untouched. Figure~\ref{fig:gauge} shows the difference at the level of the network. The Schmidt
values of a single temporal boundary are therefore not physical, while the RTM spectrum is, and
truncating on the former can discard exactly the weight the overlap needs.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{imgs/gauge.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Why the \ac{RTM} spectrum is the gauge-invariant one.} (a) A pair $X^{-1}X$ may be inserted
  on every bond at no cost: the two factors cancel, so the contraction equals the ungauged one and
  the Loschmidt echo is untouched, while the individual tensors change. In (b) and (c) only the
  lower time site is contracted; the upper one carries the open indices, so each open index keeps
  one factor and the contracted bond receives both. (b) The \ac{RDM} pairs $\ket{R}$ with its own
  conjugate, the factors meet as $XX^{\dagger}\neq\mathbb{1}$, and the spectrum moves under the
  congruence $X\rho X^{\dagger}$. (c) The \ac{RTM} pairs $\ket{R}$ with $\bra{L}$, the factors meet
  as $XX^{-1}=\mathbb{1}$, and the similarity $X\mathcal{T}_t X^{-1}$ leaves the spectrum unchanged.}
  \label{fig:gauge}
\end{figure}

The canonical form still helps, but in a generalized version, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:rtm_truncation}: the condition of Figure~\ref{fig:truncation}(a) is imposed on the
pair $\bra{L},\ket{R}$ rather than on a state and its own conjugate. The tensors away from the cut
then contract away exactly as before, and the singular values of the \ac{RTM} again follow from a
local decomposition, so the two routes cost the same to evaluate and differ only in which object
they compress.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{imgs/rtm_truncation.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The \ac{RTM} counterpart of Figure~\ref{fig:truncation}.} (a) The generalized canonical
  condition: the contraction pairs a tensor of $\bra{L}$ with the corresponding tensor of $\ket{R}$,
  not with its own conjugate. (b) Imposed along the chain, this condition makes everything before
  the cut contract away, so the singular values of the \ac{RTM} are those of the two tensors at the
  cut alone. The construction is as local as the \ac{RDM} one; what it loses is the variational
  guarantee, since the object is no longer Hermitian.}
  \label{fig:rtm_truncation}
\end{figure}

The logic of truncation changes accordingly. In the ordinary case one keeps the directions that best
preserve the norm of a state. Here the relevant quantities are bilinear matrix elements
$\bra{L}\mathcal O\ket{R}$. A component of $\ket R$ with little overlap with $\bra L$ contributes
little, however large it looks from $\ket R$ alone, and a component with small weight in $\ket R$ can
still matter if its projection onto $\bra L$ is large. The scheme of
Ref.~\cite{carignano_marimon_tagliacozzo2024} therefore performs a biorthogonal decomposition of
$\ket R\bra L$ and keeps the eigenvalues of largest modulus, which are the directions that dominate
the physical contraction. Because relevance is measured through the overlap, this can retain fewer
states than an RDM truncation at the same accuracy.

There is a price, and it is why the ordinary scheme is kept as a fallback. Standard truncation rests
on a variational bound: the reduced density matrix is Hermitian and positive, its singular values
coincide with its eigenvalues, and discarding the smallest removes the least weight from the norm.
None of this survives for a non-Hermitian object. The singular values of $\mathcal{T}_t$ and its eigenvalues
are different quantities, and it is the eigenvalues that enter the contraction, since the trace is
their sum. A component with a large singular value can therefore still fail to contribute. The
clearest illustration is an operator with eigenvalues $+1$ and $-1$: both have unit modulus, so an
SVD-based truncation keeps both, yet they cancel exactly in the trace. Truncating the RTM on
singular values is thus a motivated heuristic rather than a bound, and its convergence can be less
smooth.

A second approximation enters through the environment. The truncation at each step uses the local
environment available at that point of the sweep, not the full remaining network, because the
contraction is built column by column. For a translation-invariant problem this is benign, since
every bulk column is the same operator and weight discarded too early is regenerated by the next
application. It remains an approximation, and it is one reason the RDM route behaves more
predictably when the RTM output turns noisy.

The cost of the whole scheme is measured by the generalized R\'enyi-2 temporal entropy,
\begin{equation}
  S_2^{\text{gen}}(t) = -\log \mathrm{Tr}\, \mathcal{T}_t^{\,2},
\end{equation}
which is the quantity used throughout this thesis to quantify the temporal entanglement.

\section{Conformal-field-theory derivations}
\label{app:cft}

This appendix derives the results quoted in Section~\ref{sec:cft}. Every formula it produces is
stated and used in the main text, so it is not needed to follow the argument there.

\subsection{Conformal maps and holomorphic factorization}
\label{app:cft:maps}

In two dimensions the Euclidean coordinates are packaged into $z=x+i\tau$ and $\bar z=x-i\tau$.
Physical quantities then factorize into a holomorphic piece, depending on $z$, and an
antiholomorphic one depending on $\bar z$, which is where the separate weights $h$ and $\bar h$ come
from. Since every analytic map $z\mapsto f(z)$ is a conformal symmetry, a correlation function known
on the plane is known on any geometry such a map reaches, simply by substitution.

The map used throughout is the plane-to-cylinder logarithm $\omega=\tfrac{\beta}{2\pi}\log z$, which
wraps the plane onto a cylinder of circumference $\beta$. Continuing it to real time, with
$\beta\to iT$ and $\sinh\to\sin$, produces the strip geometry of the quench.

\subsection{Primary operators and their two-point functions}
\label{app:cft:ops}

A primary operator $\phi$ of weights $(h,\bar h)$ has on the plane the two-point function
\begin{equation}
    \langle \phi(z_1)\phi(z_2)\rangle
      = \frac{1}{(z_1-z_2)^{2h}\,(\bar z_1-\bar z_2)^{2\bar h}},
    \label{eq:cft:plane2pt}
\end{equation}
whose exponent is the scaling dimension $x=h+\bar h$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:dim}. Under a map
$\omega=f(z)$ a primary picks up Jacobian factors,
$\langle\phi(\omega_1)\phi(\omega_2)\rangle=\prod_i f'(z_i)^{-h}\bar f'(\bar z_i)^{-\bar h}
\langle\phi(z_1)\phi(z_2)\rangle$. Applying the plane-to-cylinder map turns
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:plane2pt} into the hyperbolic form characteristic of a cylinder,
\begin{equation}
    \langle\phi(\omega_1)\phi(\omega_2)\rangle \;\propto\;
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\omega_1-\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2h}
      \Big[\sinh\tfrac{\pi(\bar\omega_1-\bar\omega_2)}{\beta}\Big]^{-2\bar h}.
    \label{eq:cft:cyl2pt}
\end{equation}
Its time-like version, with a $\sin$ in place of the $\sinh$, is the origin of the chord variable of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:chord}.

\subsection{The transfer-matrix spectrum from the stress tensor}
\label{app:cft:spectrum}

The central charge is carried by the stress-energy tensor, the operator measuring the response of
the theory to a change of coordinates. In two dimensions those changes are the symmetry, so the
stress tensor generates the conformal transformations and its modes generate the Virasoro algebra.
One mode is special: $L_0$ generates scaling, and its eigenvalues on the primaries are their weights.
Its spectrum is therefore the operator content.

Quantizing on a strip of transverse size $\beta$, the generator of translations along the strip is an
integral of the stress tensor, up to the velocity $v$. The conformal map from the plane leaves behind
one extra, purely geometric term, the Casimir anomaly proportional to $c$, which gives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:casimir}. Since the transfer matrix is essentially
$\mathcal{E}=e^{-\delta t\,\hat H}$, this fixes its whole spectrum in the form of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:transfer}: a dominant eigenvalue carrying the Casimir term, and a tower of
subleading ones whose ratios and phases carry the operator dimensions.

\subsection{Twist fields and the entropy coefficients}
\label{app:cft:twist}

The entanglement entropy of an interval $\mathcal A$ follows from the R\'enyi moments
$\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}$, continued to $n\to1$. The replica
trick~\cite{calabrese_cardy2004} computes those moments as a path integral on $n$ copies of the plane
sewn cyclically along $\mathcal A$. On a single plane, that geometry is equivalent to inserting a
pair of twist operators at the endpoints of $\mathcal A$.

The twist operator is a primary of dimension $\Delta_n=\tfrac{c}{12}(n-1/n)$, so its two-point
function is a power law of the form~\eqref{eq:cft:plane2pt}. This gives
$\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{\mathcal A}^{\,n}\sim\ell^{-4\Delta_n}$ for an interval of length $\ell$, and
hence the logarithm
\begin{equation}
    S_n = \frac{c}{6}\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)\log\ell + \text{const},
    \qquad
    S_{n\to1} = \frac{c}{3}\log\ell + \text{const}.
    \label{eq:cft:cc}
\end{equation}
The R\'enyi order enters through the factor $(1+1/n)$. For a boundary cut, which is the temporal
situation of this thesis, the coefficient is halved, giving $\tfrac{c}{12}\tfrac{n+1}{n}$: that is
$c/6$ at $n\to1$ and $c/8$ at $n=2$, rather than the bulk value $c/3$.

\section{Construction of the time-evolution MPO}
\label{app:mpo}

This appendix records the machinery behind the exponential-MPO family of
Section~\ref{sec:mporep}, together with the closed-form second-order blocks used in production and
a direct comparison of the two constructions.

\subsection{Standard time-evolution algorithms}
\label{app:mpo:standard}

The conventional way of building a time-evolution operator as a tensor network is the
Trotter--Suzuki decomposition~\cite{vidal2004, schollwock2011}, which is the reference point for
Section~\ref{sec:mporep}. The evolution is split into short steps, and each step $e^{-iH\delta t}$ is
factorized into two-site gates by separating the Hamiltonian into terms acting on even and on odd
bonds. Within each group the terms commute, so each group exponentiates exactly. The error comes only
from the non-commutation between groups: $\mathcal{O}(\delta t^2)$ per step for the first-order
splitting, and $\mathcal{O}(\delta t^3)$ for the symmetric second-order one.

For an \ac{NN} chain this gives the brick-wall circuit of alternating two-site gate layers.
Each gate is split by an SVD into two tensors joined by a virtual bond, and the tensors on the same
site are contracted together, so a layer becomes a sequence of identical local tensors, that is, an
MPO. The sequence is uniform because every gate has the same structure and acts on adjacent sites,
which is exactly the translation invariance the transverse contraction needs. Ref.~\cite{paeckel2019}
reviews these and other time-evolution schemes for matrix product states.

The construction fails as soon as an interaction connects sites that are not adjacent, since such a
term does not act on a single bond of the pattern and cannot be written as one of its gates. That is
the situation here, and the reason for the \ac{FSM} route.

\subsection{Size-extensivity and the connected/disconnected organization}
\label{app:mpo:extensive}

A truncated Taylor series $e^{\tau H}=\mathbbm{1}+\tau H+\tfrac{\tau^2}{2!}H^2+\cdots$ cannot be used
in the thermodynamic limit. Each power $H^n$ is a sum of $\mathcal{O}(N^n)$ local terms, so
successive orders scale with different powers of the chain length and the result cannot be
normalized as $N\to\infty$~\cite{vandamme2024}. An acceptable construction must be size-extensive:
applied to a normalizable state it must return one, whatever the value of $N$.

The organizing idea is to expand by how the local terms overlap along the chain, rather than by
powers of $H$. Terms acting on disjoint regions commute and their product factorizes, so these
disconnected contributions are harmless and can be kept to all orders at no cost. The difficulty is
confined to the connected ones, where two or more local terms act on overlapping sites and need not
commute. An on-site field $D$ may act on the site where an interaction is initiated by $C$, for
example, and both orderings $CD$ and $DC$ are then needed, since $H^2$ sums over every ordered pair.
Dropping either would break the Hermiticity that $H^2$ inherits from $H$. A suitable operator
therefore keeps all disconnected terms exactly, captures connected clusters up to a controlled size,
and pushes the error to a chosen order in $\delta t$.

\subsection{The auxiliary-boson trick and the time-step split}
\label{app:mpo:boson}

The simplest construction on this logic is $W^{\mathrm{I}}$, which keeps every disconnected term and
discards all overlaps. It distributes the time step across the initiators $C$, the terminators $B$
and the on-site block $D$, keeps the virtual bond at $1+\chi$, and is first order. Improving it to
$W^{\mathrm{II}}$ means additionally keeping the single-site overlaps.

Writing out the correct symmetric combinations of overlapping blocks by hand is awkward, and
Zaletel et al.~\cite{zaletel2015} avoid it with an auxiliary-space device. The four blocks are
encoded in the dynamics of two hard-core bosons; a small effective Hamiltonian built from
$A,B,C,D$ acting on those bosons is exponentiated, and the new MPO blocks are read off by projecting
onto the boson occupations. Since a hard-core boson can be occupied at most once, the exponential
truncates by itself and produces exactly the required symmetric cross terms, keeping the on-site
term exact at the same bond dimension. The Van Damme construction~\cite{vandamme2024} generalizes
this idea to arbitrary order.

All three schemes share the time-step split, which is what keeps them stable. The complex step
$\tau=-i\delta t$ is divided unevenly: a real factor $t_C=\sqrt{\delta t}$ goes to the initiator
block and the phase $t_B=\tau/t_C$ to the terminator block, so the product $C\otimes B$ still carries
the correct factor while the intermediate memory channels stay well behaved.

\subsection{The second-order VD2 blocks}
\label{app:mpo:vd2}

We record the explicit local tensor of the second-order MPO (VD2) used throughout this work. These are the closed-form expressions of Van Damme et al.~\cite{vandamme2024}, specialized to our \ac{FSM} blocks; they are what the exponentiation routine assembles for a given memory dimension $\chi$.

We start from the four blocks $A,B,C,D$ of the Hamiltonian MPO $W_H$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:Wannni}): $D$ is the on-site term, $C$ initiates an interaction, $B$ terminates one, and $A$ propagates it. Following the time-step split above, we scale them as
\begin{equation}
  \tilde D = \tau D, \qquad \tilde C = t_C\, C, \qquad \tilde B = t_B\, B, \qquad \tilde A = A,
  \qquad t_C = \sqrt{\delta t},\ \ t_B = \tau/t_C,\ \ \tau=-i\delta t .
\end{equation}
The construction organizes the exponential $e^{\tau W_H}$ not by powers of $W_H$, but by the symmetric products of overlapping blocks that its Taylor series generates. We write $\{X_1\dots X_m\}$ for the symmetrized product, i.e.\ the sum over all distinct orderings of the operator-valued blocks (for example $\{XY\}=X\otimes Y + Y\otimes X$, and $\{XXY\}=XXY+XYX+YXX$), where $\otimes$ is the Kronecker product on the virtual memory index and the ordinary product on the physical index. The multinomial weights $1/2!,\,1/3!,\dots$ of the exponential are carried by the explicit prefactors below.

The resulting local tensor is a $3\times3$ block object (indices $1,2,3$ label the scalar, single- and doubled-memory sectors, which is the origin of the enlarged $1+\chi+\chi^2$ virtual bond),
\begin{equation}
  W^{\mathrm{VD2}} =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    W_{11} & W_{12} & W_{13} \\
    W_{21} & W_{22} & W_{23} \\
    W_{31} & W_{32} & W_{33}
  \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
  W_{11} &= \mathbbm{1} + \tilde D + \tfrac12\{\tilde D\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde D\tilde D\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{12} &= \tilde C + \tfrac12\{\tilde C\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde C\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{13} = \{\tilde C\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{21} &= \tilde B + \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde D\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde D\tilde D\}, \qquad
  W_{31} = \tfrac12\{\tilde B\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde D\}, \\
  W_{22} &= \tilde A + \tfrac12\big(\{\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\}\big)
            + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde C\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde A\tilde D\tilde D\}\big), \\
  W_{23} &= \{\tilde A\tilde C\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde C\tilde D\}+\{\tilde C\tilde C\tilde B\}\big), \qquad
  W_{32} = \tfrac12\{\tilde A\tilde B\} + \tfrac16\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde D\}+\{\tilde B\tilde B\tilde C\}\big), \\
  W_{33} &= \{\tilde A\tilde A\} + \tfrac13\big(\{\tilde A\tilde B\tilde C\}+\{\tilde A\tilde A\tilde D\}\big).
\end{align}
The scalar track $W_{11}$ carries the on-site evolution to third Taylor order; the $W_{12},W_{13}$ entries dress an outgoing memory leg (single and doubled), $W_{21},W_{31}$ the incoming one, and the lower-right block dresses the through-wire $\tilde A$ with all of its symmetric companions. Keeping the doubled-leg (index-3) sector is exactly what reproduces the connected clusters spanning three sites---the next-nearest-neighbour overlaps that $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ misses---and is why VD2 is genuinely second order for our model at the cost of the enlarged virtual bond.

\subsection{Comparing the two constructions in practice}
\label{app:mpo:comparison}

The claim that $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ degrades to first order for a next-nearest-neighbour Hamiltonian, while VD2 remains second order, can be checked directly on the model of this thesis. A convenient diagnostic is the loss of unitarity, since the exact $e^{-iH\delta t}$ preserves the norm of any state: applying successive layers of the approximate operator to $\ket{X+}^{\otimes N}$ and monitoring $\lVert\psi\rVert$ isolates the Trotter error, because a perfect construction would leave the norm at one.

Applying eight layers at $\delta t=0.05$ to a chain of $N=100$ sites at $p=0.1$ gives the following norms:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\hline
 & $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ & $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ & VD2 \\
\hline
virtual bond & $4$ & $4$ & $13$ \\
$\lVert\psi\rVert$ after 1 layer & $1.3514$ & $1.1341$ & $0.99995$ \\
$\lVert\psi\rVert$ after 8 layers & $12.6146$ & $3.0181$ & $0.99923$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The separation is unambiguous. The first-order $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ inflates the norm by an order of magnitude within eight steps, and $W^{\mathrm{II}}$, which would be effectively second order for a strictly \ac{NN} chain, still triples it: the connected clusters generated by the \ac{NNN} bridge are precisely the ones it fails to capture. VD2 holds the norm to within $8\times10^{-4}$ over the same interval. The same ordering appears in the Schr\"odinger-picture benchmark of the notebooks, where VD2 reproduces the TDVP evolution at every time while $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ agrees only at short times before drifting.

This is what justifies paying for the larger virtual bond. Since the transverse contraction needs long temporal chains, and reducing $\delta t$ to compensate a first-order error would lengthen them further and add truncation error at every extra site, a genuinely second-order operator at a moderate time step is the configuration that makes the calculation feasible at all.

\section{Implementation of the block power method}
\label{app:blockpm}

This appendix collects the implementation of the algorithm derived in Section~\ref{sec:blockpm}: the regularized solve with its exact left pairing, the validation against exact diagonalization, the model-agnostic drivers, the machinery that accelerates convergence along the production sweeps, and the classifier that selects the physical branch. The core routine is \texttt{block\_transfer\_eigs}, built on top of \texttt{ITransverse.jl}~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}. All code, notebooks and cluster drivers of this work are available in the public repository of Ref.~\cite{marquezolguin2026code}.

\subsection{Regularized solve and exact left pairing}
\label{app:blockpm:pairing}

Near the degeneracy the columns of the right block begin to coincide and the metric $S$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:blockpm_pencil} becomes nearly singular, so the pencil $Mv=\theta\,Sv$ is not handed to a generalized eigensolver, which would return infinite or wildly scattered values. It is regularized with the pseudoinverse instead,
\begin{equation}
  W = S^{+} M, \qquad W = V\,\Theta\,V^{-1},
  \tag{D1}
\end{equation}
where $S^{+}$ carries a relative tolerance of $10^{-12}$: directions that have effectively collapsed onto each other are projected out rather than inverted. The eigenvalues $\Theta$, sorted by modulus, estimate $\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{k-1}$, and the columns of $V$ give the right mixing coefficients.

The left block needs its own coefficients, and the obvious route hides a trap. Diagonalizing the transposed pencil in a second call gives no guarantee that its eigenvalues come out in the same order, and inside a quasi-degenerate cluster, matching each left eigenvalue to the nearest right one is ambiguous exactly when it matters: a greedy match picks the wrong partner, the paired left and right vectors stop describing the same eigenvector, and the block oscillates instead of converging. No second diagonalization is needed. Writing $y^{\mathsf T}=u^{\mathsf T}S$, the left problem becomes $y^{\mathsf T}W=\theta\,y^{\mathsf T}$, so the $y$ are the rows of $V^{-1}$ and
\begin{equation}
  u_j = \big(S^{+}\big)^{\mathsf T}\big(V^{-1}\big)^{\mathsf T} e_j ,
  \tag{D2}
\end{equation}
paired with $\theta_j$ by construction and biorthogonal through the metric, $u_i^{\mathsf T}S\,v_j=\delta_{ij}$.

One implementation note concerns the de-mixing. The MPS linear combinations of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm} are formed by exact direct-sum addition and compressed once at the end, because the default density-matrix addition is fragile for the near-parallel vectors that a tight cluster produces.

\subsection{The Schur basis and iteration safeguards}
\label{app:blockpm:schur}

The eigenvalue-only mode of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm} replaces the de-mixing coefficients by orthonormal ones. After the modulus sorting, the coefficient matrices are QR-decomposed,
\begin{equation}
  V \to Q_V, \qquad U \to Q_U, \qquad Q^\dagger Q = \mathbb{1},
  \tag{D3}
\end{equation}
and the blocks are rotated with $Q_V$ and $Q_U$ instead of $V$ and $U$. The rotations are unitary, and the leading $j$ columns of each $Q$ span the same leading subspaces as those of the sorted coefficient matrices, so this is an ordered-Schur-like choice. The Ritz values are still read from the pencil at every step; only the basis carried between iterations changes, from approximations of individual eigenvectors to a well-conditioned basis of the same invariant subspace. The pencil is insensitive to that change: under $R\to RC$ and $L\to LB$ the two matrices become $B^{\mathsf T}SC$ and $B^{\mathsf T}MC$, so $\det(B^{\mathsf T}MC-\theta B^{\mathsf T}SC)=\det B\,\det C\,\det(M-\theta S)$ and the roots $\theta$ are unchanged for any invertible $B$ and $C$. The rotation does change the conditioning of what is truncated next: $\mathrm{cond}(V)$ grows like the inverse of the gap inside the block, while $Q_V$ and $Q_U$ are unitary by construction. The two modes can therefore drift apart only through the truncation, and in practice they barely do. Two independent ladders at $p=0.1$ and $\chi=64$, one carried in each basis, agree on the two tracked eigenvalues to between $10^{-7}$ and $10^{-4}$ in relative modulus over $T=2$ to $8$. The untracked members of the block agree only to $10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$, which is expected since the convergence test never monitors them.

Three safeguards complete the iteration. Every block member is renormalized after each de-mixing, and a member whose norm underflows or overflows is replaced by a fresh random vector. The condition number of $S$ is monitored: a large value means two block directions have effectively merged, and the last member is then replaced by a random vector bi-orthogonalized against the rest with the bilinear form. Convergence is measured on the leading Ritz values (the leading two in production) after matching each value to its nearest predecessor from the previous iteration, since the frequent modulus-order swaps between near-degenerate eigenvalues would otherwise register as spurious jumps; the iteration stops when the matched movement falls below $10^{-6}$, or when it has stopped improving for a set number of iterations. The block members outside the tracked pair are carried along but never driven to this tolerance, which is why they are the less accurate ones in the comparison above.

\subsection{The tMPO builder and the unified entropy driver}
\label{app:library:build}

A thin, model-agnostic driver sits above the solver, and it is what guaranteed that every model in
the study was measured by the same pipeline. A single constructor turns any model definition into
the rotated temporal network: it assembles the one-step propagator as an MPO
(Appendix~\ref{app:mpo}), performs the $90^\circ$ rotation that maps the spatial virtual bond onto
the temporal physical index, and returns the transfer-matrix MPO with a structured seed tMPS
carrying the right index skeleton. Since the VD2 exponentiation enlarges the spatial bond, the
temporal physical dimension differs between schemes, so the builder reads it dynamically instead of
hard-coding it. That is what lets the same code path serve the $W^{\mathrm{I}}$, $W^{\mathrm{II}}$
and VD2 kernels and every model without modification.

On top of it, a single entropy driver wires the builder to the block solver. It overwrites the
structured seed with random complex tensors, since a symmetry-special seed converges to a
subdominant sector, runs the power method, trims the $N_\beta/2$ cooling bonds from each end, and
returns the per-cut R\'enyi-2 profile together with the leading spectrum.

\subsection{Computational cost}
\label{app:blockpm:cost}

One iteration of the block method contains three kinds of tensor-network work. The application step performs $2k$ \ac{tMPO}--\ac{tMPS} products, each growing the temporal bond from $\chi$ to $D\chi$ before compression. The projection step evaluates the $k^2$ bilinear overlaps of $S$ and $M$; no state grows there, and the $k\times k$ dense algebra is negligible. The de-mixing step forms $2k$ linear combinations of $k$ states each. Every combination is built as an exact direct sum, which raises the bond dimension to $k\chi$ before the compression sweep, so both the memory peak of an iteration and most of its work sit in this step. Profiling a production rung ($\chi=64$, $k=4$, $p=0.1$) attributes roughly $85\%$ of the iteration time to the de-mixing sums and their compression sweeps.

Two adjustments reduce this cost without changing what the method converges to. The running direct sum is compressed progressively, each partial sum truncated back to the production bond dimension, with the final addition left exact. And the stagnation patience is shortened, since at late rungs the iteration spends long plateaus below the convergence threshold before the stop triggers. Together the two halved the wall-clock time of a $T=18$ rung, from $9500$ to $4900$ seconds, while moving $|\mu_0|$ by $4\times10^{-6}$ relative. The mechanisms that reduce the number of iterations, rather than the cost of each one, are described next.

\subsection{Convergence acceleration}
\label{app:library:cluster}
\label{app:library:adaptive}

Three mechanisms keep the production sweeps affordable; none changes what the method converges to.

The first is warm starting along a ladder of evolution times. The converged block at one time is
re-indexed onto the longer time-site set of the next and padded with a small random tail, so the
iteration only has to relax the newly added sites; the bond cap and the cutoff follow a schedule
that is loose early and tight near convergence (the bond cap grows in steps of two up to $\chi$, and the cutoff holds at $10^{-8}$ for the first forty iterations before tightening to $10^{-10}$). This is more than a speed-up: near a degeneracy a
cold random restart has nothing biasing it onto the physical branch, so warm continuity is also
what keeps the tracker on the correct eigenvector family as the spectrum tightens.

The second is adaptivity in the block size. At stronger frustration the leading $k=4$ block can
fill with members of a single near-degenerate cluster, pushing the subleading eigenvalue needed by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:eq4} outside the captured window. The driver detects this, since the tower
gap of the classifier below returns no value when no tower member was captured, and escalates the
block in steps ($k=4\to6\to8$), warm-seeding each larger solve with the converged smaller block, so
the extra cost is paid only where the spectrum demands it. Without the escalation the boundary
tower at $p\gtrsim0.8$ is unmeasurable, not because the physics is absent but because it sits
outside a block that is too small.

The third is crash safety. Each $(p,T)$ point is written to a per-label cache as soon as it
converges, keyed by its parameters, so an interrupted run loses at most the point in flight and a
completed point is never recomputed. This is what allows the campaign to run as independent,
resumable jobs rather than one monolithic calculation.

\subsection{Phase classification and the physical-branch selector}
\label{app:selector}

Deciding which eigenvalue is the physical $\mu_0$ is the most consequential piece of bookkeeping in
the pipeline. Writing $\mathrm{phase}(z)=\arg(-z)$ for the convention of
Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}, the classifier splits a block relative to a candidate $\mu_0$ into
a tower and a set of partners: the tower is every eigenvalue within $\pi/2$ in phase, the conformal
descendants of $\mu_0$, and the partners are the momentum-$\pi$ displaced states of
Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}. The tower gap is $|\mu_1|/|\mu_0|$ computed from the
largest-modulus tower member only, never a partner, and it returns no value when the block holds no
tower member, which is the trigger for the escalation above.

The selector applies the rule of Section~\ref{sec:numerics:practical}, and anchoring on the modulus
rather than on complex-plane proximity is essential for a quantitative reason. The imaginary part of
the leading eigenvalue winds as $\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0\approx a\,v\,T$, so between two rungs the
physical branch rotates by an amount that grows with the velocity and hence with $p$, while its
displaced partners of similar modulus stand still in phase. A nearest-value rule latches onto a
partner and, re-anchoring on its own pick, corrupts the ladder that follows; at $p=0.5$ this
manifests as the tracked $|\mu_0|$ collapsing at one rung and following partners ever after, output
that looks plausible but is an artifact of the selector. Modulus dominance is stable while the
physical branch sits above its $\pi$-partner in modulus, and continuity is consulted only for
genuine sub-percent ties; past the point where the splitting closes, the tracking hands over to the
phase prediction described below.

Two safeguards complete the implementation. A guard flags any rung whose selected $|\mu_0|$ jumps
by more than a set fraction from the previous trusted one, the signature of a non-converged block,
so the driver distrusts that point instead of anchoring the ladder on it. A companion
post-processing selector then attempts a repair: it restricts the candidates to a window of five
percent around the last accepted modulus before applying dominance, which recovers rungs whose
spurious leading Ritz value sits far outside the physical band while the true $\mu_0$ survives
further down the block. On the one rung where an independently converged full calculation exists
for comparison, the repaired eigenvalue agrees with it to within $3\times10^{-5}$ relative.

The phase-predicted tracking that takes over past the modulus crossing (Section~\ref{sec:numerics:practical}) deserves three technical notes. First, a crossing does not lose the branch: the physical eigenvalue survives inside the block as a subleading member, and where an independently tracked ladder exists for comparison the phases agree to four decimals. Second, the prediction must be seeded, because the phase advance per rung grows with the velocity and can exceed $\pi$, making the wrapped step ambiguous; the first step is chosen by testing every candidate advance against the next few rungs and keeping the most consistent one. Third, each accepted rung records the distance between the prediction and the matched phase, and the ladder is cut at the first rung where this distance jumps, which is where the block stops resolving the branch.

\section{The entanglement-barrier wall}
\label{app:wall}

This appendix supports the diagnosis of Section~\ref{sec:results:wall}, where the temporal-entropy
dome inflates at a well-defined time and stops being conformal. It has two parts: what an
exceptional point is and why it spoils the eigenvectors while sparing the eigenvalues, and the
controls that establish the wall as a property of the physics rather than a resolution limit.

\subsection{Exceptional points of a non-normal matrix}
\label{app:wall:ep}

For a Hermitian operator the eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis and stay separated as long as
the eigenvalues do. A small perturbation rotates them only gently, by an amount set by the inverse
gap. A non-normal matrix behaves differently. Its left and right eigenvectors are distinct objects
paired by the bilinear form, and as two eigenvalues approach each other those vectors need not stay
apart: they can rotate towards a common direction, so the pairing $\braket{L}{R}$ of the coalescing
branch tends to zero even though neither vector vanishes. The limiting configuration, where two
eigenvalues and their eigenvectors merge at once, is an exceptional point. There the matrix is
defective, has no complete eigenbasis, and the sensitivity of the individual eigenvector diverges.

This splits our measurements cleanly in two. The eigenvalues are well conditioned: their first-order
sensitivity to a perturbation is $\bra{L_i}\delta\mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}/\braket{L_i}{R_i}$, which the
block method keeps bounded, so moduli, phases and gaps stay accurate arbitrarily deep into the
near-degenerate regime. The eigenvectors carry a condition number growing like the inverse gap,
which diverges at the exceptional point, so anything built from an individual eigenvector loses
meaning there. The temporal entropy is exactly such a quantity, since it is read off the dominant
pair.

This is the origin of the wall. The dome breaks not because the solver fails to converge, but
because the object it is asked to converge to becomes ill-defined. The order parameter is the phase
rigidity of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rigidity}, which equals one for a near-Hermitian pair and collapses to
zero at the exceptional point. Its measured collapse lands exactly where the dome inflates, which is
what identifies the two as one event. Complex symmetry does not help, when a model has it: every
matrix is similar to a complex-symmetric one, so symmetry can relabel the collapse but not forbid
it, and inside an exactly degenerate subspace the individual eigenvector is undefined rather than
merely ill-conditioned.

\subsection{What the broken dome looks like}
\label{app:wall:shape}

Section~\ref{sec:results:wall} reads the wall from the chord plot, where the profiles past it appear
as a detached arc rather than as points scattered about the line. The shape has a simple origin, and
it makes a sharper diagnostic than the height of the dome.

The chord variable of Eq.~\eqref{eq:chord} is not monotonic in the cut position. It grows as the cut
moves in from either edge and is largest at mid-strip, so a profile plotted against $W$ is traversed
twice: once for cuts in the first half, once for cuts in the second. A conformal profile depends on
the cut only through $W$, which is what Eq.~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} states, so the two traversals give
the same value at the same $W$ and land on top of one another. The dome then collapses onto a single
straight line, and the fold is invisible. This is what makes the chord plot a test rather than a
change of variables: the collapse only happens if the profile really is a function of $W$.

Past the wall that ceases to hold. The two halves of the profile no longer agree at equal $W$, the
fold opens, and the two branches trace out the arc seen in Figure~\ref{fig:wall}. At $p=0.1$ the
transition is abrupt: between $T=9$ and $T=10$ the peak of the dome jumps from $0.34$ to $0.72$ and
a second maximum appears, so a single evolution step separates a profile that collapses cleanly from
one that does not. The hump count and the failure to collapse are two views of the same failure, and
either can be used to locate the wall without fitting anything.

We do not plot the broken profiles in the main text. Once the dome carries two maxima its height and
its slope have no conformal meaning, so a figure of them would document the failure of the estimator
rather than any property of the model. The rigidity of Figure~\ref{fig:wall} says the same thing in
a quantity that remains well defined on both sides of the wall, which is why it is the diagnostic we
report.

\subsection{The wall is gap-limited, not resolution-limited}
\label{app:wall:eps}

An exceptional point is a property of the transfer matrix, so its location must not move when we
change how finely that matrix is represented. We checked this along three independent knobs, holding
the physics fixed at $p=0.1$ and varying only the numerical resolution.

Halving the bond dimension from $\chi=64$ to $32$ leaves the onset of the inflation at the same
time, so the wall is not a matter of keeping too few states. Tightening the truncation cutoff by
orders of magnitude leaves it unchanged as well. Switching the truncated object itself, from the
cheap RTM scheme to the Hermitian RDM one, also moves nothing: the RDM run breaks at the same time,
at several times the cost. Three qualitatively different refinements leaving the onset fixed is what
pins the wall to the spectral gap rather than to any shortfall of the representation. Phase rigidity
supports the same reading, since it is a Rayleigh-quotient-like quantity that stays accurate even
from a cold start.

These controls also settle which truncation to use in production. Where both converge, RTM and RDM
agree on every eigenvalue and on the rigidity to four to five digits, so they agree on the physics,
and RDM is simply slower for the same answer, by factors that grow with $T$ up to an order of
magnitude. RTM is therefore the production choice, with RDM kept as the
well-conditioned fallback for the rare points where RTM output turns noisy before the wall.

\section{The momentum-\texorpdfstring{$\pi$}{pi} displaced boundary tower}
\label{app:pairing}

This appendix derives the effect behind the boundary-exponent result of
Section~\ref{sec:results:tower}: why the odd-parity boundary tower is displaced in phase by
momentum $\approx\pi$ for any $p\neq0$, and why free-fermion models never show it. In short, the
next-nearest-neighbour coupling is a four-fermion interaction of a particular kind, and only such a
term can attach a momentum-$\pi$ phase to the odd-parity sector.

\subsection{Jordan--Wigner and the four-fermion interaction}
\label{app:pairing:jw}

Spins are algebraically awkward, since two Pauli operators anticommute on the same site but commute
on different ones, so a spin is neither a boson nor a fermion. The Jordan--Wigner transformation
rewrites the chain exactly as fermions at the cost of a non-local string. In the convention matched
to our model,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^x_j = 1 - 2n_j, \qquad
    \sigma^z_j = (c_j + c_j^\dagger)\,K_j, \qquad
    K_j = \prod_{k<j}(1-2n_k),
    \tag{F1}
\end{equation}
with $n_j=c_j^\dagger c_j$, and the string $K_j$ supplying the signs that make the $c$ anticommute.

Using $(c+c^\dagger)(1-2n)=c^\dagger-c$ and $K_j^2=1$, the terms fermionize as follows. The
transverse field becomes a chemical potential. The \ac{NN} Ising bond telescopes its
strings away and leaves a quadratic hopping-and-pairing term,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^z_j\sigma^z_{j+1} = (c_j^\dagger-c_j)(c_{j+1}+c_{j+1}^\dagger).
    \tag{F2}
\end{equation}
Those two terms make up the pure Ising chain, which is why it is a free-fermion theory, exactly
solvable, flowing to a free Majorana CFT.

The two extra terms of the ANNNI-type model are both quartic. The self-dual $XX$ term is a plain
density--density interaction, diagonal in the occupation basis, which moves nothing. The
next-nearest-neighbour bond is the interesting one: its strings no longer cancel, and it becomes a
parity-dressed hop,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma^z_i\sigma^z_{i+2} = (c_i^\dagger-c_i)\,\underbrace{(1-2n_{i+1})}_{(-1)^{n_{i+1}}}\,(c_{i+2}+c_{i+2}^\dagger),
    \tag{F3}
\end{equation}
a hop from $i$ to $i+2$ whose sign is set by the occupation of the site skipped over. This single
term is the origin of the effect.

\subsection{Why free fermions cannot displace, and the quantization at \texorpdfstring{$\pi$}{pi}}
\label{app:pairing:kw}

A free-fermion transfer matrix has the normal form
$\mathcal{E}=\mu_0\exp(-\sum_m \varepsilon_m \hat n_m)$: independent modes whose moduli and phases
are both additive over occupations. An eigenvalue near the top can only occupy modes with
$\mathrm{Re}\,\varepsilon_m\approx0$, and in an Ising-class critical chain those are the
long-wavelength modes, whose phases $\pi x/(vT)$ are small. Building a phase of exactly $\pi$ at full
modulus would need a mode that neither decays nor stops oscillating, that is a gapless branch at
lattice momentum $\pi$, which the uniform Ising-class free chain does not have. A free chain
therefore cannot produce a displaced tower. This is what the free-fermion controls show: the critical
Ising chain at two evolution times, and the anisotropic XY chain at $\gamma=0.5,\,1,\,1.5$, return no
displaced state at all.

What the argument requires is a source of momentum-$\pi$ structure, and in this model that source is
an interaction. Single-term probes identify which one. Deforming the critical Ising chain by the
\ac{NNN} term alone, at strength $0.1$ and $T=4$, produces a displaced family at
$x\approx0.37,\,1.22,\,2.17$; deforming it by the $\sigma^x\sigma^x$ term alone produces none. It is
therefore the parity-dressed hop of Eq.~(F3), not the density--density term, that carries the
displacement. Scanning down to small couplings, the displaced family is present at every
$p\neq0$ tested, down to $p=0.01$, with no threshold: the effect switches on with the interaction
rather than at some finite strength.

One consequence of the single-term probe is worth recording. The \ac{NNN}-only deformation is not
self-dual, and it displaces all the same. Self-duality is therefore not needed for the displacement
to exist; the argument below concerns why it is pinned at exactly $\pi$ rather than at a
coupling-dependent value.

The quantization at exactly $\pi$, rather than at a coupling-dependent value, has a natural home in
the self-duality of the model. In Majorana variables the $XX$ and next-nearest $ZZ$ terms are both
products of four consecutive Majoranas, offset by one Majorana site. That one-site shift is the
lattice Kramers--Wannier duality exchanging order and disorder, and it maps the two terms onto each
other, which is why a self-dual Hamiltonian must carry both. At the self-dual point the network
admits a half-step transfer operation with $\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{1/2}^{2}$, so a sector whose
half-step eigenvalue carries a factor $\pm i$ lands after squaring at phase exactly $\pi$. This is a
discrete index rather than a tunable phase, consistent with the displacement being identical across
the couplings probed. Which sector carries the index is not settled from first principles, and we
note it in the outlook. What the main text needs is established here: the displacement exists for
every $p\neq0$, is absent for free fermions, and is pinned at $\pi$.

\subsection{Finite-size scaling of the mid-chain entropy}
\label{app:fss}

Section~\ref{sec:annni:equilibrium} reads the central charge from the shape of the entanglement

Running DMRG over a ladder of sizes and plotting the mid-chain entropy against $\ln L$ therefore
gives a straight line of slope $c/6$. Over $N=40$ to $200$ this returns $c\approx0.51$ at both
$p=0.1$ and $p=0.5$, consistent with the profile fit of the main text and with the Ising value.
The ladder uses fifteen DMRG sweeps with the bond dimension ramped to $400$ and a truncation cutoff of $10^{-10}$; the heavier $N=100$ chord fit of Section~\ref{sec:annni:equilibrium} uses fifty sweeps, a ramp to $1000$, and a cutoff of $10^{-12}$. Both ramps start with noise-assisted sweeps.

The same ground states allow a cross-check with the R\'enyi-2 entropy, the member used for every
temporal measurement in this thesis. Its mid-chain slope is $c/8$ rather than $c/6$, and it returns
$c\approx0.58$: the same universality class, but biased upward, because R\'enyi entropies with $n>1$
carry stronger corrections to scaling than the von Neumann one. This is the equilibrium counterpart
of the finite-time bias affecting the temporal R\'enyi-2 read, and it is why the von Neumann entropy
is the estimator of choice in equilibrium, where the full Schmidt spectrum is available at no cost.
Figure~\ref{fig:cft_L} compares the two finite-size estimates.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{imgs/cft_L.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Finite-size scaling of the mid-chain entropy.} At $p=0.1$ and $p=0.5$; the von Neumann
    entropy (solid, slope $c/6$) gives $c\approx0.51$; the R\'enyi-2 entropy (dashed, slope $c/8$),
    computed from the same ground states, gives $c\approx0.58$, with the larger corrections to

\subsection{The sound velocity from the equilibrium dispersion}
\label{app:velocity}

Every temporal formula that turns a transfer-matrix phase into a scaling dimension carries the sound
velocity $v(p)$, so it has to be known independently. We take it from equilibrium, using Alcaraz's
dispersion relation for this chain: the periodic ring Hamiltonian is diagonalized exactly at small
size and the velocity is read off the lowest excitation carrying momentum.

The construction is validated in four ways. It recovers $v=2$ exactly at $p=0$. The from-scratch ring
Hamiltonian matches the operator used elsewhere in the code to machine precision. The
sector-resolved and sector-blind reads agree. And, decisively, feeding our measured $v(p)$ into
Alcaraz's separate Casimir formula reproduces his published ground-state central charge
$c(p)\approx\tfrac12$. The measured values grow steadily with frustration,
\begin{equation}
    v(0)=1.98,\quad v(0.1)=2.60,\quad v(0.2)=3.21,\quad
    v(0.3)=3.80,\quad v(0.4)=4.37,\quad v(0.5)=4.93,
    \tag{G1}
\end{equation}
and it is these, rather than a fixed $v=2$, that make the boundary tower of Section~\ref{sec:results:tower} read as clean half-integers at every $p$. Figure~\ref{fig:velocity} shows them against $p$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{imgs/nb4_velocity_vs_p.png}
    \caption{\textbf{The sound velocity grows with frustration.} $v(p)$ at criticality, extracted from the equilibrium dispersion of periodic chains with $N=12$ sites. The dashed line marks the exact Ising value $v=2$ at $p=0$.}
    \label{fig:velocity}
\end{figure}

That the temporal transfer matrix really carries this $p$-dependent velocity was checked separately
with the anisotropic XY control, whose velocity $v(\gamma)=2\gamma$ is known exactly. The pipeline
recovers $x_1=\tfrac12$ there only when fed the correct $v(\gamma)$, which confirms that the method
is velocity-faithful.

\subsection{The branch constant in the eigenvalue central-charge fit}
\label{app:eq3}

The eigenvalue route to $c$ has a subtlety that ruins the fit if ignored. We work with
$\lambda_0=\log(-\mu_0)$, following Ref.~\cite{carignano_tagliacozzo2025}, and since
$\log(-\mu_0)=\log(\mu_0)+i\pi$ the imaginary part carries a constant $-\pi$ branch offset that the
bare fit form omits. Leaving it in dumps a spurious $-\pi/T$ contribution into the $1/T^2$ Casimir
slot and returns a central charge of order $10^3$.

The corrected procedure pins $B=-\pi$, verified to four digits at the integrable points, fits the
three-term form $\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda_0 = A\,T + B + C/T + D/T^3$ on the de-biased phase, and reads
$c=24\,v\,|C|/\pi$. This returns $c=0.4997$ on Ising and reproduces the reference Ising and Potts
values. Freeing $B$ instead makes the four-parameter fit unstable on a short window, which is why
the pinning matters and why an absolute read at $p\neq0$ requires a long baseline in $T$.
