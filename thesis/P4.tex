\section{Building the Time-Evolution MPO}
\label{sec:mporep}

The transverse contraction of Section~\ref{sec:transverse} requires the time-evolution operator
$U(\delta t)=e^{-iH\delta t}$ as a translation-invariant \ac{MPO}, since only then is every column of
the rotated network the same transfer matrix, and the contraction an eigenvalue problem. Any site
dependence would produce a different operator at each site and remove that structure.

The standard way of building such an operator is a Trotter--Suzuki decomposition into layers of
two-site gates, which for a nearest-neighbour chain gives a uniform sequence of identical tensors;
Appendix~\ref{app:mpo:standard} summarizes the construction. This is no longer possible in a simple
way once the Hamiltonian contains a term connecting sites $i$ and $i+2$, since such a term does not
act on a single bond of that pattern, and the available workarounds do not yield a
translation-invariant \ac{MPO}. We therefore build the operator by a different route, encoding the
Hamiltonian as a finite-state machine.

\subsection{The Hamiltonian as a finite-state machine}
\label{sec:mporep:fsm}

The alternative to spatial Trotterization is to write the Hamiltonian directly as a
block-upper-triangular \ac{MPO}, in which the virtual index acts as the internal state of a
\ac{FSM}: it records whether an interaction has been started, is being propagated, or has been
completed. For a generic extensive Hamiltonian the local tensor takes the block form
$W_H=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbb{I}&C&D\\ 0&A&B\\ 0&0&\mathbb{I}\end{smallmatrix}\big)$, where
$D$ holds the on-site terms, $C$ and $B$ initiate and terminate interactions, and $A$ propagates
them across intermediate sites. For the ANNNI-type model these blocks must accommodate the
transverse field $-\lambda\sigma^x$, the two nearest-neighbour terms $-\sigma^z\sigma^z$ and
$-p\lambda\,\sigma^x\sigma^x$, and the \ac{NNN} coupling $-p\,\sigma^z\sigma^z$, giving a tensor of
bond dimension $D_W=5$,
\begin{equation}
  W_H =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathbb{I} & -\sigma^z & -p\lambda\,\sigma^x & -p\,\sigma^z & -\lambda\,\sigma^x \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma^z \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma^x \\
    0 & \mathbb{I} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \mathbb{I}
  \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:Wannni}
\end{equation}
whose first row contains the initiators, whose last column contains the terminators, and whose
fourth row is the memory channel carrying the unfinished \ac{NNN} interaction.

The tensor is best read as a machine that scans the chain from left to right, using the virtual bond
as its memory. It starts in the identity state, the first row. From there it either applies an
on-site operator through $D$ and jumps to the final state, or opens a multi-site interaction through
$C$ and enters the memory block $A$. For a nearest-neighbour term it waits a single site and
terminates through $B$. The \ac{NNN} term is where the construction earns its place: initiating it
places the machine in a dedicated memory state, the fourth row of Equation~\eqref{eq:Wannni}, which
applies the identity at the intermediate site $i+1$ and carries the pending $\sigma^z$ operator
unchanged until the interaction terminates at site $i+2$. Once an interaction is complete the
machine moves to the absorbing final state and applies identities along the rest of the chain.
Because the matrix is upper triangular, the contraction can only advance through this sequence and
never run an interaction backwards. The bond dimension is simply the number of internal states: the
initial state, the final state, and one memory channel per propagating interaction.

This directionality has a consequence that carries through the rest of the work. Since the machine
reads the chain in one direction only, the tensor is asymmetric in its virtual space, and the
transfer matrix obtained after the $90^\circ$ rotation is correspondingly non-Hermitian and
non-normal. A symmetric \ac{MPO} would allow the right temporal state to be obtained by transposing
the left one; ours does not, so the two boundary states must be computed independently, which is the
setting of Section~\ref{sec:nonhermitian}.

\subsection{Exponentiating the finite-state machine}
\label{sec:mporep:exp}

What remains is to turn $W_H$ into an operator $W(\tau)\approx e^{\tau H}$ with $\tau=-i\delta t$.
This is not a simple matrix exponential. Powers of an upper-triangular block matrix mix the blocks,
producing cross terms such as $CD$, $BC$ and $AD$, each of which has to appear with exactly the
coefficient the Taylor series prescribes if the operator is to match it to the intended order. A
truncated Taylor series is not an option either, since $H^n$ is a sum of $\mathcal{O}(N^n)$ local
terms and the result is not size-extensive~\cite{vandamme2024}. The constructions used here avoid
both problems by organizing the expansion according to how the local terms overlap: terms on
disjoint regions commute and factorize, and are kept to all orders at no cost, so that only the
connected clusters of overlapping terms need to be captured, up to a controlled size. The machinery
is given in Appendix~\ref{app:mpo}.

Two constructions matter here, both built from the blocks of $W_H$. The operators $W^{\mathrm{I}}$
and $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ of Zaletel et al.~\cite{zaletel2015} have virtual bond $1+\chi$, with $\chi=3$
memory channels for our model, and $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ is the common choice in the literature. For a
strictly nearest-neighbour Hamiltonian it behaves as second order, because the overlaps it fails to
capture involve clusters that do not occur. The \ac{NNN} bridge produces exactly those clusters, so
for our model the same operator is genuinely first order. We therefore use the higher-order
construction of Van Damme et al.~\cite{vandamme2024}, whose order-$n$ member reproduces all
connected clusters up to a controlled size with a one-step error $\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{n+1})$. Its
order-1 member coincides with $W^{\mathrm{II}}$; we use the order-2 one, denoted VD2.

The choice has a cost and a benefit, and both propagate to the rest of the calculation. The cost is
a larger virtual bond, $1+\chi+\chi^2$ instead of $1+\chi$, which for our model means $13$ rather
than $4$. Under the $90^\circ$ rotation this becomes the physical dimension of the temporal sites,
so the tMPS carries a correspondingly larger local dimension than it would with $W^{\mathrm{II}}$. The benefit is that genuine second-order accuracy permits a
comparatively large time step at controlled Trotter error. This matters more than it might appear:
a smaller $\delta t$ means more temporal sites for the same physical time, hence a longer temporal
chain and more truncation steps, each contributing its own error. Working at $\delta t=0.05$ with a
second-order operator is what makes the long temporal networks of Section~\ref{sec:results}
accessible. Appendix~\ref{app:mpo:comparison} compares the two constructions directly and shows
the first-order behaviour of $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ for this model.

\subsection{The non-Hermitian transfer matrix}
\label{sec:nonhermitian}

Rotating the network turns the operator just constructed into the spatial transfer matrix
$\mathcal{E}$, which inherits the asymmetry of the finite-state machine and is therefore
non-Hermitian and non-normal, $\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}^\dagger\neq\mathcal{E}^\dagger\mathcal{E}$.
Section~\ref{sec:transverse} already used these properties; what is needed before the numerical
treatment is the notation they impose.

Such an operator has two eigenbases rather than one: right eigenvectors defined by
$\mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}=\mu_i\ket{R_i}$, and left eigenvectors by $\bra{L_i}\mathcal{E}=\mu_i\bra{L_i}$,
which in general are unrelated. Neither set is orthonormal on its own. The two are paired instead by
the biorthogonality relation $\braket{L_i}{R_j}\propto\delta_{ij}$, in which the pairing is the
bilinear overlap the network itself contracts, with no complex conjugation involved. Since the
eigenvalues $\mu_i$ are complex, we order them by modulus and write $\mu_0$ for the dominant one.
This is the notation used throughout Sections~\ref{sec:numerics} and~\ref{sec:results}.
