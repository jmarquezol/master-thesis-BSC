\section{MPO Representation of $e^{-iHt}$}
\label{sec:mporep}

We now construct an MPO for the time-evolution operator $U(\delta t)=e^{-iH\delta t}$ of the ANNNI-type model that is compatible with transverse contraction. As already emphasized in Section~\ref{sec:transverse}, the MPO should have translation invariance: this way, when the network is rotated and the columns of the time-evolution MPO become the spatial transfer matrices, the whole 2D network is simply the infinite repetition of a single identical column. Only then does the transverse contraction reduce to an eigenproblem for just one transfer matrix, giving easy access to the thermodynamic limit. Any site dependence in the MPO would require a different transfer matrix at every site and destroy the efficiency and simplicity of the method---in this case, of our version of the power method.

For standard \ac{NN} models, such as the transverse-field Ising model, generating this translation-invariant MPO is straightforward. The usual approach is to apply a Trotter--Suzuki decomposition in small time steps, $\delta t$, so that the full evolution is written as a product of short-time operators, $U(t)=\prod U(\delta t)$. Each $U(\delta t)$ is then split into alternating layers of even and odd two-site gates, forming the familiar ``brick-wall'' circuit. This works well because every gate acts on adjacent physical sites: pairs of decomposed tensors can be contracted analytically and reshaped into a uniform one-dimensional sequence of identical MPO tensors (see ITransverse paper for details).

The NNN coupling of our model, by contrast, breaks this simple picture. An interaction connecting site $i$ directly to site $i+2$ does not live on a single physical bond, so it cannot be placed into the same local two-site pattern as in the NN case. A Trotterized construction would then have to either group three sites into one enlarged tensor or introduce SWAP gates to bring the non-adjacent spins next to each other. Both options make the circuit more cumbersome and, more importantly for us, destroy the clean uniform structure required to build an efficient translation-invariant MPO.

\subsection{The Hamiltonian as a Finite-State Machine}

To keep a perfectly uniform, translation-invariant MPO for the NNN case, we must abandon spatial Trotterization and use a different strategy: we encode the full Hamiltonian directly as a block-upper-triangular MPO. In this representation, the virtual indices of the MPO behave like the internal states of a finite-state machine that records (into a memory) whether an interaction has been started, propagated, or terminated. For a generic extensive Hamiltonian, the local tensor can be written in the following block form
\begin{equation}
  W_H =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathbb{I} & C & D \\
    0 & A & B \\
    0 & 0 & \mathbb{I}
  \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $D$ contains the local on-site terms, $C$ and $B$ initiate and terminate interactions, respectively, and $A$ propagates an interaction across the intermediate sites.

For the ANNNI-type model, these blocks must accommodate the transverse field ($-\lambda\sigma^x$), the two NN interactions ($-\sigma^z\sigma^z$ and $-p\lambda\,\sigma^x\sigma^x$), and the NNN coupling ($-p\,\sigma^z\sigma^z$). The resulting local MPO tensor has bond dimension $D_w=5$ and reads
\begin{equation}
  W_H =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathbb{I} & -\sigma^z & -p\lambda\,\sigma^x & -p\,\sigma^z & -\lambda\,\sigma^x \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma^z \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma^x \\
    0 & \mathbb{I} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \mathbb{I}
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:Wannni}
\end{equation}
Here $D=-\lambda\sigma^x$ contains the on-site transverse field. The first row contains the interaction initiators, $C=(-\sigma^z,\,-p\lambda\sigma^x,\,-p\sigma^z)$; the last column contains the terminators, $B=(\sigma^z,\,\sigma^x,\,0)^T$; and the internal block $A$ stores the memory channels that propagate unfinished (NNN) interactions across the virtual bond.

It is useful to read this MPO as a directional \ac{FSM} that scans the chain from left to right, using the virtual bond as its internal memory. The machine starts in the identity state (the first row). From there, it can either apply an on-site operator through $D$ and jump directly to the final completed state, or it can open a multi-site interaction through the initiator block $C$ and enter the memory block $A$. For a NN interaction, the machine simply waits one site and terminates through $B$. The NNN term is where the FSM construction becomes especially useful: initiating it puts the machine into a dedicated memory state (the fourth row of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Wannni}), which applies an identity at the intermediate site $i+1$ and carries the pending $\sigma^z$ operator untouched until the interaction terminates correctly at site $i+2$. Once an interaction is finished, the machine moves into the final absorbing state, where it applies identities to the rest of the chain.

Because the matrix is strictly upper-triangular, contracting the MPO from left to right can only move forward through this sequence; it can never run an interaction backwards. As we have seen, this perfectly accounts for the long-range NNN terms while preserving translation invariance along the MPO tensors. On the other hand, the required bond dimension is simply the number of internal machine states: the initial state, the final absorbing state, and one memory channel for each propagating interaction, giving $D_W=5$ for the ANNNI Hamiltonian.


A consequence of this directional structure, important for what follows, is that the MPO explicitly breaks left--right parity. Because the finite-state machine reads the chain only in one direction, the block matrix is inherently asymmetric in its virtual bond space. When the MPO is rotated by $90^\circ$ to form the tMPO, the corresponding spatial transfer matrix is therefore non-Hermitian and structurally asymmetric. Symmetric MPOs admit useful shortcuts---for instance, the right temporal state can be obtained by transposing the left one---but generic asymmetric MPOs like ours require independent left and right boundary states. This is precisely the setting handled by the left--right power method implemented in \texttt{ITransverse.jl}, and we characterize the resulting non-Hermitian transfer matrix at the end of this section (Section~\ref{sec:nonhermitian}).


\subsection{Exponentiating the Finite-State Machine}

Once the Hamiltonian is encoded in $W_H$, the remaining task is to turn it into a time-evolution MPO $W(\tau)\approx e^{\tau H}$, with $\tau=-i\delta t$. Exponentiating an upper-triangular block matrix is not straightforward: its powers mix the blocks (e.g.\ $(\tau W_H)^2$ generates cross terms $CD$, $BC$, $AD$ and their reverses), each of which must appear with exactly the coefficient the Taylor series of $e^{\tau W_H}$ prescribes, or the operator stops matching that series at the target order. A naive truncated Taylor series is not even an option, because it is not size-extensive: each power $H^n$ is a sum of $\mathcal{O}(N^n)$ local terms, so successive orders scale with different powers of the chain length and the result cannot be normalized as $N\to\infty$~\cite{vandamme2024}. The way around both problems is to organize the expansion not by powers of $H$ but by how its local terms overlap: terms on disjoint regions commute and factorize, and can be kept to all orders for free, while the difficulty is confined to the connected clusters of overlapping, non-commuting terms, which need only be captured up to a controlled size. We summarize the resulting family of constructions here and defer the machinery---the connected/disconnected bookkeeping, the auxiliary-boson trick that generates the correct symmetric cross terms, and the time-step split that keeps them stable---to Appendix~\ref{app:mpo}.

Several constructions of increasing accuracy exist for this task, all built from the same four blocks $A,B,C,D$ of $W_H$. The classic ones are $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ and $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ of Zaletel et al.~\cite{zaletel2015}, both of virtual bond $1+\chi$ (with $\chi=3$ memory channels for our model). $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ is the standard choice in the literature, and for strictly nearest-neighbour Hamiltonians it is effectively second order; for longer-ranged interactions like our NNN bridge, however, it misses the larger connected clusters of overlapping terms and becomes genuinely first order, which would force impractically small time steps (we refer to~\cite{zaletel2015, vandamme2024} for the details of these constructions, and summarize them briefly in Appendix~\ref{app:mpo}). To recover true second-order accuracy regardless of the interaction range, we use the systematic higher-order construction of Van Damme et al.~\cite{vandamme2024}, whose order-$n$ MPO reproduces all connected clusters up to a controlled size with one-step error $\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{n+1})$; its order-1 member coincides with $W^{\mathrm{II}}$, and the one we use, ``VD2'', is order 2. This costs a modest enlargement of the virtual bond, from $1+\chi$ to $1+\chi+\chi^2$ (here $1+3+9=13$), with the closed-form blocks recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:mpo}. Two facts carry forward from this choice. First, under the $90^\circ$ rotation of Section~\ref{sec:transverse} the spatial virtual bond of $U(\delta t)$ becomes the physical dimension of the temporal sites, so the tMPS inherits a larger physical dimension for VD2 than for $W^{\mathrm{II}}$. Second, being genuinely second order, VD2 reaches long times with comparatively large steps ($\delta t=0.05$) at controlled Trotter error, which is what lets us access the longer temporal networks the transverse contraction needs.

\subsection{The non-Hermitian transfer matrix}
\label{sec:nonhermitian}

With the time-evolution MPO in hand, we can characterize the object the transverse contraction actually diagonalizes. After the $90^\circ$ rotation of Section~\ref{sec:transverse}, a column of the network becomes the spatial transfer matrix $\mathcal{E}$, and the directional FSM structure just described makes it non-Hermitian and also non-normal ($\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}^\dagger\neq\mathcal{E}^\dagger\mathcal{E}$). Non-Hermitian operators behave differently from the Hermitian operators of standard quantum mechanics, and these differences matter both for the physics we extract and for the numerical methods of Section~\ref{sec:numerics}.

A Hermitian operator has a single orthonormal eigenbasis. A non-Hermitian $\mathcal{E}$ has two: right eigenvectors, defined by $\mathcal{E}\ket{R_i}=\mu_i\ket{R_i}$, and left eigenvectors, by $\bra{L_i}\mathcal{E}=\mu_i\bra{L_i}$, and in general $\ket{L_i}\neq\ket{R_i}$. These sets are not individually orthonormal; instead they form a biorthogonal system, $\braket{L_i|R_j}\propto\delta_{ij}$, where the pairing is the bilinear overlap $\braket{L|R}$ that the network itself encodes---the same object contracted throughout the transverse method, with no conjugation involved. The practical consequence is that the dominant left and right temporal states must be obtained independently: the right state cannot be obtained by conjugating the left one, as it can for symmetric MPOs. The related distinction between the eigenvalues and the singular values of such an operator---and why truncating on the latter is a heuristic rather than a bound---was discussed in Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation}.

Since the eigenvalues $\mu_i$ are generally complex, we order them by their modulus. The eigenvalue with the largest modulus, $\mu_0$, controls the long-chain contraction and therefore determines the Loschmidt rate in the thermodynamic limit:
\begin{equation}
    \ell(T) = -\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log|\mathcal{L}(T)|
            = -\log|\mu_0(T)|.
    \label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
For a finite open chain, this relation receives $\mathcal{O}(1/N)$ boundary corrections, which have to be taken into account when comparing $-\log|\mu_0|$ with a finite-size Loschmidt echo divided by $N$. The subleading eigenvalues, ordered the same way, are suppressed in the contraction by powers of $|\mu_1/\mu_0|<1$---the gap ratio that, as Section~\ref{sec:powermethod} anticipated, both sets the convergence rate of the power method and closes toward one under the emergent dual unitarity of the critical quench.
