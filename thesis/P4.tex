\section{Building the Time-Evolution MPO}
\label{sec:mporep}

The transverse contraction framework introduced in Section~\ref{sec:transverse} requires the infinitesimal-time evolution operator $U(\delta t)=e^{-iH\delta t}$ to be represented as a translation-invariant \ac{MPO}. This ensures that every column of the rotated space-time network defines the same transfer matrix, effectively reducing the spatial contraction (which encodes the dynamics) to an eigenvalue problem. If the local tensors depended on the site, each column would instead define a different operator and this simple reduction would no longer apply.

For \ac{NN} Hamiltonians, such an operator is usually constructed through a Trotter--Suzuki decomposition into layers of two-site gates---the brick-wall circuit reviewed in Ref.~\cite{paeckel2019} and described, for the tMPO construction of the \texttt{ITransverse.jl} package used here, in Ref.~\cite{carignano2025itransverse}. A term coupling sites $i$ and $i+2$ does not fit into this bond pattern, and the standard extensions do not preserve a one-site translation-invariant \ac{MPO}. We therefore represent the Hamiltonian as a \acf{FSM} and exponentiate this representation directly.

\subsection{The Hamiltonian as a \texorpdfstring{\ac{FSM}}{FSM}}
\label{sec:mporep:fsm}

In the \ac{FSM} representation~\cite{pirvu2010}, the Hamiltonian is encoded as an \ac{MPO} whose local tensor has a block-upper-triangular form~\cite{parker2020}. This tensor is composed of two identity blocks, which propagate the initial and final states; an on-site block $D$; a block row $C$, which initiates interactions; a block column $B$, which terminates them; and a memory block $A$, which propagates unfinished interactions between their endpoints. The virtual index therefore records whether an interaction has not yet begun, is being propagated, or has terminated. This block form can also be generated automatically from the operator itself, which is the practical route for more complicated Hamiltonians~\cite{hubig2017}. For the ANNNI-type model, the local tensor has bond dimension $D_W=5$ and takes the form
\begin{align}
  W_H & =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathbbm{1} & C & D \\
    0 & A & B \\
    0 & 0 & \mathbbm{1}
  \end{pmatrix}=
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \mathbbm{1} & -\sigma^z & -p\lambda\,\sigma^x & -p\,\sigma^z & -\lambda\,\sigma^x \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma^z \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \sigma^x \\
    0 & \mathbbm{1} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & \mathbbm{1}
  \end{pmatrix}.
  \label{eq:Wannni}
\end{align}

Figure~\ref{fig:fsm} represents Eq.~\eqref{eq:Wannni} as the allowed paths through the five virtual states. Each node denotes a value of the virtual index, while an arrow from state $a$ to state $b$, labelled by an operator $O$, represents the tensor entry $(W_H)_{ab}=O$. The machine scans the chain from left to right, beginning in state $1$ and ending in state $5$.

The loop $1\to1$ applies identities until a Hamiltonian term is initiated. A direct transition $1\to5$ applies the on-site field and completes the term at the same site. For an \ac{NN} interaction, the machine instead follows either $1\to2\to5$ or $1\to3\to5$. The first transition applies the operator at site $i$ and stores in the virtual state whether a $\sigma^z$ or $\sigma^x$ endpoint is pending; the second applies the matching operator at site $i+1$ and completes the interaction. The \ac{NNN} term follows the path $1\to4\to2\to5$: the transition to state $4$ applies the first $\sigma^z$ at site $i$, the transition $4\to2$ inserts an identity at site $i+1$, and the transition $2\to5$ applies the final $\sigma^z$ at site $i+2$. Once state $5$ is reached, the loop $5\to5$ applies identities along the remainder of the chain. The zero entries of $W_H$ correspond to absent arrows, so no other transitions are allowed.

Because the \ac{FSM} is directional, its local tensor is asymmetric in the virtual indices. This asymmetry will then be inherited by the exponentiated operator and, after the $90^\circ$ rotation, by the spatial transfer matrix. The resulting operator $\mathcal{E}$ is therefore non-Hermitian and non-normal, $\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}^\dagger\neq\mathcal{E}^\dagger\mathcal{E}$. Its left and right eigenvectors are distinct, so the two temporal boundary states must be computed separately. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} introduces the notation used for their numerical treatment.

\subsection{Exponentiating the \texorpdfstring{\ac{FSM}}{FSM}}
\label{sec:mporep:exp}

We seek an approximation $W(\tau)\approx e^{\tau H}$, with $\tau=-i\delta t$, constructed from the blocks of $W_H$. The two most direct approaches are unsuitable. Exponentiating the local tensor as an ordinary matrix mixes its operator-valued blocks without reproducing the full many-body exponential. Expanding $e^{\tau H}$ as a Taylor series and truncating it at finite order avoids this problem locally, but the resulting operator is not size-extensive because $H^n$ contains $\mathcal{O}(N^n)$ contributions~\cite{vandamme2024}.

Throughout the remainder of this work, we instead use the construction of Ref.~\cite{vandamme2024}. It organizes the expansion according to the spatial overlap of the local Hamiltonian terms. Contributions with disjoint support commute and factorize, so they can be retained to all orders, while only connected clusters of overlapping terms must be reproduced explicitly. Retaining clusters up to size $n$ gives a one-step error of $\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{n+1})$. We use the second-order approximation, denoted VD2, whose virtual bond dimension is $1+\chi+\chi^2=13$ for the $\chi=3$ memory channels of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Wannni}. Appendix~\ref{app:mpo} gives the detailed construction.

Cheaper implementations are available, including the operators $W^{\mathrm{I}}$ and $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ of Ref.~\cite{zaletel2015}, which have bond dimension $1+\chi$. For a strictly \ac{NN} Hamiltonian, $W^{\mathrm{II}}$ is effectively second order. In the present model, however, the \ac{NNN} interaction generates connected clusters that this construction omits, reducing it to first-order accuracy. Appendix~\ref{app:mpo:comparison} demonstrates this difference. We therefore use VD2 despite its larger bond dimension.

After the rotation, the virtual bond dimension of the evolution \ac{MPO} becomes the physical dimension of each temporal site. VD2 consequently produces a locally larger temporal chain than $W^{\mathrm{II}}$, but its second-order accuracy allows us to use the larger time step $\delta t=0.1$ while keeping the discretization error controlled. This reduces the temporal length at fixed physical time and requires fewer truncation steps, making the long-time calculations of Section~\ref{sec:results} more computationally accessible.
