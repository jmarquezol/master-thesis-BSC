\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We now put the framework to work. The logic of this section mirrors the logic of the whole thesis. We first go to the integrable point $p=0$, where the model is the transverse-field Ising chain and the conformal answer is known exactly, and check that the transverse pipeline reproduces it (Section~\ref{sec:results:validation}). We then switch on frustration and ask whether the universal signatures survive as $p$ grows (Section~\ref{sec:results:frustration}). Finally, we turn the method on itself and map its limits---the point at which the entanglement barrier stops the calculation, and what exactly fails there (Section~\ref{sec:results:limits}).

Unless stated otherwise, all runs use the second-order VD2 time-evolution MPO (Section~\ref{sec:mporep}) with $\delta t=0.05$, a conformal regulator $\beta_0=0.2$ (i.e.\ $N_\beta=4$ imaginary-time sites split between the two ends, Section~\ref{sec:cft:boundary}), the initial state $\ket{\Psi_0}=\ket{X+}^{\otimes N}$, and the left--right block power method of Section~\ref{sec:nonhermitian} to obtain the dominant temporal states.

\subsection{Validation at the integrable point}
\label{sec:results:validation}

At $p=0$ every conformal prediction of Section~\ref{sec:cft} has an exact target, so this point is where we calibrate the pipeline and confirm that it measures what we think it measures. Four independent checks, each testing a different one of the universal formulas collected in Section~\ref{sec:cft:temporal}, all come out consistent with the Ising CFT ($c=\tfrac12$, $v=2$).

\emph{The conformal dome.} The generalized R\'enyi-2 temporal entropy, evaluated across every temporal cut of the converged tMPS, traces the symmetric ``dome'' of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} (Figure~\ref{fig:val_profiles}). Plotted against the conformal chord variable $\log[\tfrac{2T}{\pi}\sin\tfrac{\pi t}{T}]$, its real part collapses onto a straight line (Figure~\ref{fig:val_chord}) whose slope is the R\'enyi-2 coefficient $c/8$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:coeff}; the extracted slope returns $c\approx\tfrac12$, as it must. We stress the coefficient again because it is a common pitfall: for the generalized R\'enyi-2 entropy on a boundary cut the slope is $c/8$, not the bulk von Neumann value $c/3$.

\emph{The imaginary plateau.} The imaginary part of the same entropy sits on a flat plateau across the dome. Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:chord} predicts $\mathrm{Im}\,S^{\mathrm{gen}}=\pi c/12$, which for $c=\tfrac12$ is $\pi/24\approx0.131$; the measured plateau agrees. This is a genuinely independent read of the central charge, requiring no fit at all, and we find its value to be the same for the R\'enyi-2 entropy as for the $n\to1$ prediction---the imaginary offset is, empirically, $n$-independent.

\emph{The boundary exponent.} The gap between the two leading transfer eigenvalues gives the boundary scaling dimension through Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:eq4}. A fit of $\mathrm{Im}(\lambda_1-\lambda_0)$ against $1/T$ (Figure~\ref{fig:val_x1}) returns $x_1=0.502$, with a leading coefficient $a_1=0.788\approx\pi/4$. Both are the exact free-boundary Ising values, confirming that the field-polarized $\ket{X+}$ realizes the free (disordered) boundary condition of Section~\ref{sec:cft:boundary}.

\emph{Emergent dual unitarity, and the eigenvalue route to $c$.} Traced in the complex plane as $T$ grows, the leading eigenvalue $\lambda_0(T)$ moves along a circle of near-constant radius while its phase winds (Figure~\ref{fig:val_circle})---the modulus flattens and only the phase evolves, which is exactly the spectral fingerprint of the emergent dual unitarity of Section~\ref{sec:emergent}. From the imaginary part of that same leading eigenvalue we can extract $c$ a second, independent way, via Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3}: isolating the universal $\kappa/T^2$ Casimir term (Figure~\ref{fig:val_kappa}) and converting it returns a central charge consistent with $c=\tfrac12$. Having two independent routes to the same number---the entropy slope and the eigenvalue fit---is what makes the calibration trustworthy, and both are carried into the frustrated case below.

\subsection{Switching on frustration: the \texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-sweep}
\label{sec:results:frustration}

With the pipeline calibrated, we ask the central question of this work: do the universal signatures survive when the model is made non-integrable and longer-ranged? We answer it by tuning the frustration $p$ away from the Ising point and repeating the measurements above, using $p=0$ as the convention-fixing calibration and reporting the frustrated central charge relative to it. \textit{[OPEN: the full sweep, $p\in\{0,0.1,0.3,0.5,1.0,1.5\}$ extended to $T=20$, is running as a set of parallel jobs on the BSC cluster; the entries below are filled for $p=0.1$ and stubbed for the larger values, and the figures are designed to become a multi-$p$ comparison once that data lands. The larger-$p$ arms carry the warm-started, checkpointed block solver of Section~\ref{sec:blockpm} with the automatic block-size escalation, which is what makes the stronger-frustration points reachable at all.]}

\emph{The headline, at $p=0.1$.} The entropy dome remains conformal and the chord fit remains clean through a well-defined pre-wall window. Extracting the R\'enyi-2 slope over $T=4$--$9$ and calibrating against $p=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
    c(p=0.1) = 0.47 \pm 0.05,
    \label{eq:results:headline}
\end{equation}
with per-time values $0.43,\,0.46,\,0.56,\,0.48,\,0.46,\,0.42$---consistent, within the uncertainty, with the Ising value $c=\tfrac12$. The eigenvalue route corroborates this independently: fitting the Casimir term of Equation~\eqref{eq:cft:eq3} using the physically-tracked leading eigenvalue returns $c=0.50$--$0.53$, stable across every fit window we tried. The boundary content also carries over: the boundary exponent comes out $x_1=0.497$, again the free-boundary value. Taken together, these say that temporal Ising universality survives the introduction of next-nearest-neighbour frustration at $p=0.1$: the emergent dual-unitary structure and the conformal entropy scaling are not artifacts of integrability.

\emph{Frustration moves the barrier.} There is, however, a clear effect of $p$ on \emph{where} the method stops working. The transfer gap closes faster for the frustrated chain than for $p=0$ (Figure~\ref{fig:frust_gap}): the leading band of eigenvalues collapses toward a common modulus at smaller $T$, so the entanglement barrier---the wall of Section~\ref{sec:results:limits}---sets in earlier. This is the first hint that frustration and the conformal physics are in tension not in the \emph{value} of the universal data but in the \emph{reach} over which we can extract it.

\subsection{The limits of the method}
\label{sec:results:limits}

The most honest---and, for a thesis about pushing the classical-simulation frontier, arguably the most useful---result of this work is a precise characterization of \emph{where} and \emph{why} the method fails. The entanglement barrier is not an implementation detail we could engineer away; it is a physical consequence of the emergent dual unitarity that makes the whole approach possible. Here we map that boundary.

\emph{The block method buys reach, then hits a wall.} As the transfer gap closes, the single-vector power method loses its ability to separate the dominant eigenvector from its near-degenerate neighbours and stalls. The block power method of Section~\ref{sec:nonhermitian}, which tracks the whole leading cluster at once, extends the usable window considerably---for the frustrated chain it carries the analysis through the gap closing to $T\approx9$ (Figure~\ref{fig:limit_blockpm}). But it does not abolish the barrier. At $T\approx10$ the entropy dome inflates discontinuously---its peak jumps by more than a third in a single step, from $0.33$ to $0.71$ (Figure~\ref{fig:limit_wall})---while, tellingly, the transfer \emph{eigenvalues} themselves remain perfectly well-behaved. Against an exact dense diagonalization of the transfer matrix (feasible at small bond dimension), the block-method eigenvalues agree to between $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-13}$ straight through the wall. The eigenvalues are right; it is the eigenvectors, and hence the entropy built from them, that break.

\emph{The wall is a near-exceptional point.} Section~\ref{sec:rigidity} argued that this asymmetry---accurate eigenvalues, unusable eigenvectors---is the signature of an approaching exceptional point, and gave the phase rigidity $r_j$ of Equation~\eqref{eq:rigidity} as its order parameter. Measuring $r_j$ across the sweep confirms the picture precisely: it collapses geometrically over roughly five orders of magnitude as $T$ grows, uniformly across the members of the leading cluster, reaching the numerical floor exactly where the entropy dome inflates (Figure~\ref{fig:limit_rigidity}). The leading eigenvectors are becoming self-orthogonal in the bilinear form---the pair is trying to coalesce into a defective, non-diagonalizable configuration. This is not a failure of the iteration scheme; it is the physics of the dual-unitary limit, in which ``the'' dominant eigenvector ceases to be a well-defined object.

\emph{No post-processing rescues the entropy.} Because the eigenvalues survive, it is natural to ask whether a cleverer way of extracting the eigenvector from the (well-converged) leading subspace could push the entropy past the wall. We tested several, and report a uniformly negative result, because the negative result is itself the point. Reading the eigenvector off the converged block by a refined, residual-minimizing projection instead of the ordinary route changes nothing: the two give numerically identical residuals, which moreover grow with $T$ rather than being fixed by any $k\times k$ algebra. Reconstructing the entropy from the gauge-invariant subspace \emph{projector} inflates it, because that object is a mixed state and adds a classical mixing entropy. Transporting the physical eigenvector from one $T$ to the next through the best unitary map between successive subspaces (a Procrustes alignment) is meaningless here, because the subspaces themselves rotate too fast---the map is far from unitary, its deviation ranging from $0.4$ to $0.98$. Every repair asks the one question that is mathematically ill-posed at the degeneracy---``which direction, inside the cluster?''---and inherits its ill-posedness.

\emph{A diagnostic that does survive.} The one construction that behaves well is not a rescue but a cross-check. Instead of isolating an eigenvector, one can propagate the physical boundary state a finite number $\ell$ of columns into the network and read the entropy off the resulting pure state; this is an analytic matrix function of the transfer matrix, well-conditioned at any gap. In the clean window it reproduces the single-eigenvector dome exactly, as it must. Crucially, at the wall it does \emph{not} smooth over the discontinuity---built from an entirely independent selection of the boundary-visible cluster members, it reproduces the same jump. Two independent constructions landing on the same discontinuity is strong evidence that the jump is real spectral physics, not an artifact of how we track the dominant eigenvalue. It confirms the wall rather than removing it.

\emph{The wall is set by the physics, not by the resolution.} A natural worry is that the wall is really a numerical-resolution artifact: truncation always introduces an error $\varepsilon$, and if the wall sat wherever $\varepsilon$ caught up with the closing gap, more bond dimension would simply push it back. It does not. Halving the bond dimension, from $\chi=64$ to $\chi=32$, leaves the onset of the inflation at the same $T$: both domes track together and jump together. The same holds along the other two truncation knobs---tightening the discarded-weight cutoff, and switching the truncation from the RTM to the RDM scheme---neither of which moves the wall, and the phase rigidity $r_j$ collapses at an identical rate for all of them. This also settles the RTM-versus-RDM question raised in Section~\ref{sec:transverse:truncation} on the practical side: where both converge they return the same eigenvalues and the same rigidity, so the two are physically equivalent, and since the RDM scheme is several times more expensive for no gain, the gauge-invariant RTM truncation is the right production choice. The wall, in short, is \emph{gap-limited}, not resolution-limited: it is a property of where the physical transfer-matrix gap closes, which no amount of extra numerical resolution can change.

The overall picture is captured by the asymmetry of Equation~\eqref{eq:reach}: the \emph{spectrum} of the transfer matrix is robust arbitrarily deep into the dual-unitary regime, while the temporal \emph{entropy}, which needs an individual eigenvector, is trustworthy only in the pre-wall window where the leading pair is still isolated. The universal physics of Section~\ref{sec:results:frustration}---the central charge, the boundary exponent, the dual-unitary circle---is extracted precisely in that window; the wall is the price the method pays for the very conformal structure it exploits. \textit{[OPEN: how the wall position scales with $p$ is part of the ongoing sweep---the earlier onset at $p=0.1$ relative to $p=0$ suggests frustration systematically compresses the usable window, a trend the $p=0.3$--$1.5$ data will quantify. The origin of the near-$\pm$ pairing of the leading eigenvalues, which sharpens as the wall approaches, remains an open question. A systematic $\delta t$-convergence study of the wall position is also outstanding.]}
