\begin{frame}{Simulating Quantum Many-Body Systems}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{motivation_tn.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \idea{tensor networks organise a state by its entanglement}
  \begin{center}
    equilibrium in 1D is solved: MPS $+$ DMRG (area law)
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \note{Before the physics, the computational problem. A generic many-body
  wavefunction needs d to the N coefficients --- the curse of dimensionality:
  storing, let alone evolving, an arbitrary state is hopeless beyond a few
  tens of spins. Tensor networks are the way out: instead of all coefficients,
  keep a network of small tensors whose bond dimension chi controls how much
  entanglement the ansatz can carry --- entanglement becomes the organising
  principle. For one dimension this programme is essentially complete in
  equilibrium: ground states of local gapped Hamiltonians obey an area law
  --- entanglement across a cut saturates --- so matrix product states
  capture them with polynomial resources, and DMRG made them the
  state-of-the-art tool of the field.}
\end{frame}

    \end{column}

  \note{But we care about dynamics, and there the same idea runs into a wall.
  After a global quench, entanglement across spatial cuts grows linearly in
  time --- towards volume law --- so the bond dimension a matrix-product
  simulation needs grows exponentially. The figure is our own measurement:
  the maximum bond dimension a TDVP simulation requests as a function of
  time. It explodes and slams into the cap --- here two hundred and

  are excellent at short times, but this entanglement barrier is not an
  implementation detail; it is the physics of quenched entanglement growth.
  This thesis is about a different way of doing time evolution --- one that
  avoids the barrier entirely when we quench to a critical point in one
  dimension.}
\end{frame}

% 5 · THE LOSCHMIDT ECHO — one visual identity
% ============================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Loschmidt Echo}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.97\textwidth]{echo_identity.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    \small $\tau = \beta_0 + it$: real-time evolution with a cooling
    regulator $\beta_0$ \qquad strip width $\,2\beta = 2v\,(\beta_0 + iT)$
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \note{The object of the whole thesis, in one line. The Loschmidt echo: the
  amplitude for the initial state to return to itself after evolving for a
  time t. Three ways of reading the same object. Left: the definition.
  Middle: writing the evolution as a path integral and Trotterising it, the
  echo is the contraction of a two-dimensional tensor network --- one axis
  space, one axis time; the open circles at top and bottom are the initial
  product state closing the path integral. Right: in the scaling limit this
  network is the partition function of a two-dimensional CFT on a strip.
  The checklist of what makes the mapping work: (1) we give time a small
  real part, tau equals beta-nought plus i t --- a Wick rotation with a
  cooling regulator; beta-nought filters the high-energy content and makes
  the continuation well defined --- the cream bands in both pictures. (2)
  The initial product state sits at the two edges: boundaries of the
  geometry. (3) Under renormalisation those boundaries flow to conformal
  boundary states --- Cardy states, written B. (4) The strip width is
  complex: two v times beta-nought plus i T, growing linearly with time.
  (5) The sound velocity v converts lattice time into CFT geometry --- it
  is non-universal, and we measure it independently in equilibrium. (6) The
  quench must end at a critical point, and we work directly in the
  thermodynamic limit. Everything that follows exploits this identity.}
\end{frame}

      {\small virtual space $\to$ physical \emph{time}\\
        physical space $\to$ virtual direction}
      \idea{translation invariance $\Rightarrow$ every column is\\ the same
        operator: the transfer matrix $\E$}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{Here is the change of perspective. The network is fixed --- nothing
  moves --- but we read it column by column instead of row by row: we
  contract along space, not along time. Concretely, the roles of the
  indices swap, as the figure shows: legs that were virtual bonds of the
  evolution operator become the physical indices of states living along the
  time direction, and the physical spatial legs become the virtual
  direction we contract over. The payoff of translation invariance is that
  every column of the network is identical: a single column transfer
  matrix, itself a matrix product operator acting on temporal states. The
  entire dynamics is now encoded in one operator, applied over and over.}
\end{frame}

% 7 · EIGENPROBLEM
% ============================================================================
\begin{frame}{Dynamics as an Eigenproblem}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \[
        \Lech(T) \;=\; \bra{b_L}\,\E^{\,N}\ket{b_R}
        \;\xrightarrow[\;N\to\infty\;]{}\; c_0\,\mu_0^{\,N}
      \]
      \vspace{0.3em}
      \[
        \E\E^\dagger \neq \E^\dagger\E:
        \qquad
        \begin{aligned}
          \bra{L}\E &= \mu_0 \bra{L}\\
          \E\ket{R} &= \mu_0 \ket{R}
        \end{aligned}
      \]
      \idea{everything reduces to the dominant pair and\\ the spectrum of the
        non-Hermitian transfer matrix $\E$}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth,height=0.68\textheight,keepaspectratio]{eigenproblem.pdf}\\[0.3ex]
      {\footnotesize $b_L$, $b_R$: random seeds --- the power method
        converges them to $\bra{L}$, $\ket{R}$}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{Once every column is the same operator, contracting the network
  means applying the transfer matrix N times between two boundary columns.
  And applying the same operator many times is a power iteration: in the
  thermodynamic limit the echo per site is governed by the dominant
  eigenvalue --- the echo becomes c-nought times mu-nought to the N. So
  the contraction problem has become an eigenvalue problem. One essential
  caveat: this operator represents real-time evolution, so it is
  non-normal --- it does not commute with its adjoint. Its left and right
  dominant eigenvectors are genuinely different objects, obtained from the
  two sides, and they pair through the bilinear overlap without complex
  conjugation. Everything in this thesis reduces to computing the dominant
  pair --- and, later, the subleading spectrum --- of this non-Hermitian
  transfer matrix. And because of the CFT mapping, we know what that
  spectrum should be. That is the next slide.}
\end{frame}

% 8 · PREDICTIONS I — the spectrum of E
% ============================================================================
\begin{frame}{CFT Predictions I --- the Leading Eigenvalue}
  \[
    \E = e^{-\hat H}\;\;(a=1),\qquad
    \hat H = \frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(L_0 - \frac{c}{24}\Big)
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \mu_i = \exp\Big[-\frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(x_i - \frac{c}{24}\Big)\Big]
  \]
  \vfill
  1.\ emergent dual unitarity --- the moduli lock:
  \[
    \frac{|\mu_i|}{|\mu_0|} = 1 - \frac{b_i}{T^2}
    \;\longrightarrow\; 1
  \]
  \vfill
  2.\ the dominant phase --- the expression we fit:
  \[
    \frac{\operatorname{Im}\lambda_0}{T}
    = a_0 - \frac{\pi c}{24\, v\, T^{2}}
    \;\Rightarrow\; c,
    \qquad \lambda_0 = \log(-\mu_0)
  \]
  \vfill
  \note{Why do we know the spectrum? Because on the strip the CFT fixes the
  generator: the strip Hamiltonian is pi over beta times L-nought minus c
  over twenty-four --- L-nought's eigenvalues are the boundary scaling
  dimensions, the shift is the Casimir anomaly. The transfer matrix advances
  one lattice spacing, set to one, so its eigenvalues are these exponentials
  with the complex width beta equal to v times two beta-nought plus i T in
  the denominator. Expanding one over beta: the imaginary part is order one
  over T and feeds the phases; the real part is order beta-nought over T
  squared and feeds the moduli. Two predictions for the leading eigenvalue
  follow. First, emergent dual unitarity: any modulus difference carries at
  least one power of beta-nought over T squared, so the moduli of the whole
  tower lock onto one circle as T grows --- the sketch shows the subleading
  values sitting slightly inside at finite T, by exactly that order.
  Second, the phase of the dominant eigenvalue: its expansion gives a
  linear, non-universal term a-nought and a universal curvature minus pi c
  over twenty-four v T squared --- that is literally the expression we fit
  to measure the central charge.}
\end{frame}

% 18 · BLOCK POWER METHOD

\begin{frame}{Resolving the Tower: the Block Power Method}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    propagate $k$ right and $k$ left states; approximate an eigenvector
    inside the block:
  \end{center}
  \[
    \ket{\psi} = \sum_{j=1}^{k} v_j \ket{R_j}
  \]
  \vfill
  \[
    \sum_j M_{ij}\, v_j = \theta \sum_j S_{ij}\, v_j,
    \qquad
    S_{ij}=\braket{L_i}{R_j},
    \quad
    M_{ij}=\bra{L_i}\E\ket{R_j}
  \]
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    solving the $k\times k$ problem gives the Ritz values
    \[
      \theta_0,\,\theta_1,\,\dots,\,\theta_{k-1}
      \;\;\approx\;\;
      \mu_0,\,\mu_1,\,\dots,\,\mu_{k-1}
    \]
    --- the $k$ leading eigenvalues of $\E$, and from their phases the gaps
    $\Delta\theta_\alpha$
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}\footnotesize\color{ink!70}
    physical branch: modulus window around the previous $\mu_0$ $+$ phase
    continuity --- never largest modulus
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \note{For the subleading spectrum --- the boundary tower --- one vector is
  not enough: we propagate a block of k right and k left states. Repeated
  application of the operator drives the block towards the subspace of the
  k leading eigenvectors, though its individual members do not converge to
  particular eigenvectors --- they must be separated afterwards. That
  separation is a two-sided Rayleigh--Ritz projection: approximate an
  eigenvector as a combination of the block states, demand the residual be
  orthogonal to every left state, and the full eigenproblem collapses to a
  k-by-k generalised problem built from the biorthogonal overlap matrix S
  and the projected operator M. Its solutions --- the Ritz values --- are
  the approximations to the k leading eigenvalues of the transfer matrix,
  and their phases give the gaps that carry the boundary dimensions. Two
  properties worth stating: the convergence rate is set by the gap between
  the retained subspace and the rest of the spectrum, not by the tiny gaps
  inside the tower --- which is why the block converges even as the moduli
  coalesce; and the pencil is solved with a pseudoinverse, because near
  degeneracies the overlap matrix is singular. How the physical branch is
  tracked among the Ritz values --- modulus window, phase continuity ---
  follows the same rules as the single-vector method; details in the
  backup.}
\end{frame}

  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.3em}
    \item mixed canonical form $\Rightarrow$ the cut tensor $M$; its SVD
      $M=USV^{\dagger}$ is the Schmidt decomposition
    \item the surrounding isometries contract to $\mathbb{1}$:
      $\;\rho_B = U_B\, M_A\, U_B^{\dagger}$ --- the full RDM's spectrum
      lives in a $\chi\times\chi$ environment
    \item eigenvalues $=$ squared singular values, one-to-one $\Rightarrow$
      keep the $\chi'$ largest (optimal; error $=$ discarded weight)
  \end{itemize}
  \note{The standard scheme first, as the baseline. Put the chain in mixed
  canonical form: the whole state collapses onto one tensor M at the cut,
  and the SVD of M is the Schmidt decomposition. The point of canonical
  form is the middle bullet: every tensor away from the cut is an
  isometry and contracts to the identity, so the exponentially large
  reduced density matrix is similar to a chi-by-chi local environment ---
  same spectrum, computed locally. And for this Hermitian positive object
  the eigenvalues are exactly the squared singular values, one-to-one:
  keeping the largest chi-prime of them is the Eckart--Young-optimal
  truncation, with the discarded weight as the exact error.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup D3 --- RTM Truncation: the Same Reduction, Different Isometries}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{rtm_trunc_row.pdf}
  \end{center}
  {\small
  \begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.25em}
    \item generalised canonical form: each $\bra{L}$ tensor is paired with
      the corresponding $\ket{R}$ tensor --- the isometries on the two
      sides differ
    \item local reduction: $\;\tau_B = U_B\, M_A\, V_B$ with
      $U_B\neq V_B^{\dagger}$ $\Rightarrow$ eigenvalues NOT preserved, but
      $\mathrm{sv}(\tau_B)=\mathrm{sv}(M_A)$
    \item so we truncate the $\chi\times\chi$ environment on its
      \textbf{singular values}; for the RTM these are no longer one-to-one
      with the eigenvalues (non-Hermitian) --- no variational bound
  \end{itemize}}
  \note{Now the transverse analogue, and the one difference that matters.
  The canonical condition is imposed on the pair --- each left tensor
  against the corresponding right tensor, not against its own conjugate
  --- so the isometries on the two sides of the cut are different
  objects. The local reduction then reads tau-B equals U-B M-A V-B with U
  not the dagger of V: that is not a similarity, so the eigenvalues of
  the local environment are NOT those of the full RTM. What survives is
  the singular values --- isometries preserve them --- so the practical
  scheme truncates the chi-by-chi environment on its singular values. And
  here is the honest caveat: for the Hermitian RDM, singular values and
  eigenvalues coincided one-to-one; for the non-Hermitian RTM they do
  not, the trace is a signed sum the singular values do not control, and
  the Eckart--Young bound is gone. The empirical justification --- same
  answers as RDM at ten times smaller chi --- is on the next slide.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup D4 --- How the Iteration Stops}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      {\small
      \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.4em}
        \item $\mathrm{d}s$ $=$ change in the singular values of the
          temporal state between successive iterations --- the convergence
          criterion in the code
        \item stop when $\mathrm{d}s$ falls below tolerance, or after
          $150$ iterations without improvement
        \item RTM hits a noise floor (no-improvement stop) while RDM
          converges --- yet both return the same leading eigenvalue to
          better than 1 part in $10^{4}$
      \end{enumerate}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.7\textheight,keepaspectratio]{res_conv.pdf}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{How a run actually ends. The monitor, d-s, is the change in the
  singular values of the temporal state between successive applications
  of the transfer matrix --- that is the convergence criterion
  implemented in the code. A run stops either when d-s crosses the
  tolerance, or by the no-improvement rule: one hundred fifty iterations
  without a new best value. The figure shows the two truncation schemes
  from the same random seed at the same point: under RTM truncation d-s
  drops three orders of magnitude and then flattens at a noise floor
  above tolerance --- the run ends on the no-improvement rule --- while
  under RDM it crosses tolerance and terminates normally. The control is
  the last point: both runs return the same leading eigenvalue to better
  than one part in ten to the four, so the floor is a property of the
  monitor under the generalised truncation, not a bias in the answer.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup E1 --- the Block Iteration, Step by Step}
  \small
  \textbf{Algorithm (App.~F, Alg.~1):} the $k$ dominant eigenpairs of $\E$
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item $\ket{R_j},\,\bra{L_j} \gets$ random complex tMPS, \ $j=1,\dots,k$
    \item \textbf{apply:}\quad
      $\ket{\tilde R_j} \gets \mathrm{truncate}_\chi(\E\ket{R_j})$,\qquad
      $\bra{\tilde L_j} \gets \mathrm{truncate}_\chi(\bra{L_j}\E)$
    \item \textbf{project:}\quad
      $S_{ij}=\braket{L_i}{R_j}$,\qquad
      $M_{ij}=\bra{L_i}\E\ket{R_j}$
    \item solve $Mv=\theta Sv$ (via $\operatorname{pinv}(S)M$) for Ritz
      values $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k$ and coefficients $V$, $U$
    \item \textbf{separate:}\quad
      $\ket{R_j} \gets \mathrm{truncate}_\chi\big(\textstyle\sum_i
      V_{ij}\ket{\tilde R_i}\big)$, \ analogously $\bra{L_j}$ with $U$
    \item normalise; repeat 2--5 until the leading Ritz values stop
      changing within tolerance
  \end{enumerate}
  \note{If asked to walk through the block method. Six steps. Start from k
  random complex temporal states on each side. Apply: one transfer-matrix
  application per member, truncated back to chi --- the left states with
  the transpose. Project: build the k-by-k overlap matrix S and projected
  operator M from the biorthogonal contractions. Solve the small pencil
  --- with a pseudoinverse of S, because near degeneracies S is singular
  --- to get the Ritz values, which approximate the k leading
  eigenvalues, and the coefficient matrices. Separate: rebuild each block
  member as the coefficient combination of the propagated states,
  truncate, normalise, and iterate until the leading Ritz values stop
  changing. In the production runs we mostly use the eigenvalue-only
  variant, where step five keeps orthonormal bases from a QR instead of
  separated eigenvectors --- the separation is the unstable part and the
  spectrum does not need it.}
\end{frame}

    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{The deficit of the spectral central charge at the integrable point,
  quantified. Two separate points, and I want to keep them separate.
  First, the fitted form truncates the expansion. On the left you see what
  we actually fit: a constant, the branch term minus pi over T, and the
  one-over-T-squared term that carries the central charge. The next term
  is one over T to the fourth in this quantity --- that is one over T
  cubed in the imaginary part of lambda-nought --- and we drop it. Restore

  the truncation of the same expansion the fitted equation comes from.}
