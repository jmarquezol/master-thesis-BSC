\slidesection{motivation}
\begin{frame}{Simulating Quantum Many-Body Systems}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    \only<1>{\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{motivation_tn_a.pdf}}%
    \only<2>{\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{motivation_tn.pdf}}%
    \only<3>{\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{hilbert_corner.pdf}}
  \end{center}
  % the block must vanish on step 3, or its empty box pushes that caption
  % into the footer
  \only<1-2>{%
      \begin{center}
        \only<1>{$\displaystyle \ket{\Psi} = \sum_{i_1 \dots i_N}
              \hlb{accent}{$\Psi_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_N}$}\;
              \ket{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_N}$}%
        \only<2>{$\displaystyle \hlb{accent}{$\Psi_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_N}$}
              \;=\; \sum_{\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_{N-1}}
              A^{i_1}_{\alpha_1}\,A^{i_2}_{\alpha_1\alpha_2}\cdots
              A^{i_N}_{\alpha_{N-1}}$}%
      \end{center}}
  \begin{center}\small\color{ink!75}
    \only<1>{the \textbf{many-body problem}: a state is a list of $d^N$ numbers}%
    \only<2>{a \textbf{matrix product state (MPS)}: \ $\chi$ $=$ the
        entanglement it can carry $=$ the memory it costs}%
    \only<3>{a small $\chi$ already describes states with low entanglement\\
        and fast-decaying correlations --- the \textbf{area law}}%
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \note{Before the physics, the computational problem. Write a generic state
    in the product basis and its whole content is the highlighted object: one
    coefficient per configuration of the chain, d to the N of them. That is the
    curse of dimensionality --- storing, let alone evolving, an arbitrary state
    is hopeless beyond a few tens of spins. Tensor networks are the way out: instead of all coefficients,
    keep a network of small tensors whose bond dimension chi controls how much
    entanglement the ansatz can carry --- entanglement becomes the organising
    principle. For one dimension this programme is essentially complete in
    equilibrium: ground states of local gapped Hamiltonians obey an area law
    --- entanglement across a cut saturates --- so matrix product states
    capture them with polynomial resources, and DMRG made them the
    state-of-the-art tool of the field.}
\end{frame}

  \begin{center}\small
    \makebox[0.65\textwidth][c]{%
      \visible<2->{an \textbf{MPO}: an operator in the same language}}%
    \makebox[0.35\textwidth][c]{%
      \visible<3->{keep the largest Schmidt values}}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \note{One slide of vocabulary, because every figure after this is drawn in
    these pictures. Each circle is a tensor and each line an index, so the
    chain of circles is the matrix product state from the previous slide.
    Joining two legs means summing over that index --- a contraction, which
    for two tensors is just a matrix product. Now the operation this thesis
    lives on. An operator in the same language is a matrix product operator,
    an MPO --- for us, one step of time evolution. Apply it and the two chains
    merge into one, but the bond multiplies: chi times D. So we truncate back
    to a chi we can afford, keeping the largest Schmidt values, and repeat ---
    every step throws something away, and the errors compound. That is what
    the next slide is about.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dynamics: the Entanglement Barrier}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \begin{center}\small
        dynamics runs into a different problem:
      \end{center}
      \vspace{-0.2em}
      \begin{center}\small
        $\chi \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{apply MPO}\ }\; D\chi
          \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{truncate}\ }\; \chi$
      \end{center}
      \vspace{0.5em}
      \begin{center}
        {\footnotesize\color{ink!70} after a quench:}\\[0.3ex]
        $S_x(t)\sim t \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \chi\sim e^{ct}$
      \end{center}
      \vspace{0.3em}
      \onslide<2->{\idea{this work: time evolution\\ that avoids the barrier,\\
            for critical quenches in 1D}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      \centering
      % the band marks where the run has hit the bond-dimension cap and the
      % answer stops being the physics. Coordinates are fractions of the
      % image, so they follow the figure if it is ever resized.
      \begin{tikzpicture}
        \node[inner sep=0,anchor=south west] (bimg) at (0,0)
        {\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{entanglement_barrier.pdf}};
        % band spans from where the run first hits the cap (T = 4.5) to the
        % right edge of the axes; fractions measured off the figure itself.
        \begin{scope}[x={(bimg.south east)},y={(bimg.north west)}]
          \fill[warn,opacity=0.12] (0.680,0.218) rectangle (0.965,0.957);
        \end{scope}
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{But we care about dynamics, and there the same idea runs into a wall.
    After a global quench, entanglement across spatial cuts grows linearly in
    time --- towards volume law --- so the bond dimension a matrix-product
    simulation needs grows exponentially. The figure is our own measurement:
    the maximum bond dimension a TDVP simulation requests as a function of
    time. It explodes and slams into the cap --- here two hundred and
    fifty-six --- after which the simulation is simply wrong. TEBD and TDVP
    are excellent at short times, but this entanglement barrier is not an
    implementation detail; it is the physics of quenched entanglement growth.
    This thesis is about a different way of doing time evolution --- one that
    avoids the barrier entirely when we quench to a critical point in one
    dimension.}
\end{frame}

    critical point the microscopic details stop mattering, and systems that
    look nothing alike share the same long-distance physics. What survives is
    a short list. The central charge, c, which counts the degrees of freedom
    of the effective description. And the boundary dimensions, the operators
    the edges of the system are allowed to host. For the Ising class both are
    known exactly, so we have the answer sheet before we start --- which makes
    this a test rather than a measurement. The question is whether we can
    recover it from real-time dynamics alone. And here is what is new: this
    has been done before, but for models that can be solved exactly. Ours
    cannot.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dynamics as an Eigenproblem}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
      \[
        \Lech(T) \;=\; \bra{b_L}\,\E^{\,N}\ket{b_R}
        \;=\; \sum_i c_i\,\mu_i^{\,N}
      \]
      \vspace{-1.7em}
      \[
        =\; \hlb{accent}{$c_0\,\mu_0^{\,N}$}
        \underbrace{\Big[\,1+\sum_{i\ge1}\tfrac{c_i}{c_0}
            \big(\tfrac{\mu_i}{\mu_0}\big)^{\!N}\Big]}_{\textstyle\to\,1}
      \]
      \vspace{0.9em}
      \begin{center}\footnotesize\color{ink!70}
        $\E\ket{r_i}=\mu_i\ket{r_i}$, \quad $\bra{\ell_i}\E=\mu_i\bra{\ell_i}$\\[0.5ex]
        $c_i=\braket{b_L}{r_i}\braket{\ell_i}{b_R}$
      \end{center}
      \vspace{0.9em}
      \onslide<2->{%
          \idea{$\E\E^\dagger \neq \E^\dagger\E$: everything reduces\\
            to the dominant pair and to the\\
            spectrum of the non-Hermitian $\E$}}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.64\textheight,keepaspectratio]{eigenproblem.pdf}\\[0.6ex]
      \onslide<2->{\footnotesize $b_L$, $b_R$: random seeds --- repeated
          application \emph{projects} them onto the dominant pair,\\
          $\bra{b_L}\E^{N}\!\to\bra{L}$, \ $\E^{N}\ket{b_R}\!\to\ket{R}$}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \note{If every column is the same operator, then contracting the network
    just means applying that operator N times --- and applying one operator
    many times is a power iteration. In the thermodynamic limit only the
    largest eigenvalue survives; everything else dies off. So a contraction
    problem has turned into an eigenvalue problem, and the chain length has
    dropped out. One caveat that matters everywhere downstream: this operator
    is not symmetric, so its left and right eigenvectors are different
    objects and each has to be computed from its own side. Everything in the
    thesis reduces to finding that pair, and later the eigenvalues
    underneath it. And because of the mapping we already know what those
    should be.}
\end{frame}

    \qquad

    \qquad

  \]

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{apply_pair.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.2em}

          \raggedleft
          {\large\color{accent!80!black}\textbf{output:}}
        \end{column}

          \raggedright
          1.\ the dominant pair \ $\bra{L}$, $\ket{R}$\\[0.6ex]
          2.\ $\mu_0 \;=\; \dfrac{\bra{L}\,\E\,\ket{R}}{\braket{L}{R}}$
        \end{column}
      \end{columns}

      \hfill{\footnotesize\color{ink!70}
        \textbf{validity}: random complex seeds $\cdot$ independent seeds agree
        $\cdot$ phase continuous in $T$}}
  \note{How we actually get the dominant pair. Because the operator is not
    symmetric, the problem is two-sided: the left and right eigenvectors are
    genuinely different objects and each needs its own iteration. The
    procedure is the one from the second slide, and the figure shows it:
    apply, truncate back to chi, repeat, until the state stops changing.
    Panel (a) is the right vector, panel (b) the left, propagated with the
    transpose. Once both have converged the eigenvalue is a ratio of two
    overlaps. That gives us the three objects everything downstream is built
    from. The safeguards on the right are what make the answer trustworthy:
    we start from random complex seeds, we check that independent seeds
    agree, and we require the phase to move smoothly in T. The failure
    statistics are in the backup.}
\end{frame}

% 18 · BLOCK POWER METHOD

\begin{frame}{Resolving the Tower: the Block Power Method}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}\large
    the same iteration, on a \textbf{block} of $k$ states per side
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \[
    \sum_j M_{ij}\, v_j = \theta \sum_j S_{ij}\, v_j,
    \qquad
    S_{ij}=\braket{L_i}{R_j},
    \quad
    M_{ij}=\bra{L_i}\E\ket{R_j}
  \]
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  \begin{center}\footnotesize\color{ink!70}
    a $k\times k$ generalised eigenproblem, projected onto the block
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \onslide<2->{%
      \idea{$\theta_0,\dots,\theta_{k-1}\;\approx\;\mu_0,\dots,\mu_{k-1}$:
        the $k$ leading eigenvalues,\\ and from their phases the gaps
        $\Delta\theta_\alpha$}}
  \vfill
  \note{The second thing I built. One vector only ever finds the leading
    eigenvalue, but the boundary operators live below it --- so we run the
    same iteration on a block of k states per side. Repeated application pulls
    the block onto the subspace of the k leading eigenvectors, but its members
    do not settle onto particular eigenvectors, so they must be separated
    afterwards. That is what the equation does: project onto the block and the
    problem collapses to k by k. Its solutions approximate the k leading
    eigenvalues, and the phase gaps between them carry the boundary
    dimensions. One objection to answer before it is asked: the convergence
    rate is set by the gap between the block and everything below it, not by
    the tiny gaps inside the tower --- which is why this works even as the
    eigenvalues merge.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup A1 --- from the Euclidean Strip to Real Time}

  \vspace{-1.2em}

    $c/8 = (c/12)(1+1/n)$ at $n=2$ --- halved, because an edge-anchored cut

  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}\small
    continue to real time: \quad $\ell \to i\,vT$, \quad $x \to i\,vt$
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}\small
    the ratio $x/\ell = t/T$ stays real $\Rightarrow$ the sine is

  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \[
    \operatorname{Re}S_2 = s_0 + \frac{c}{8}\,W(t,T)
    \quad\text{(dome)}\qquad
    \operatorname{Im}S_2 = \frac{c}{8}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi c}{16}
    \quad\text{(plateau)}
  \]
  \vfill
  \note{The one step of the entropy derivation worth showing. The starting
    point is standard boundary conformal field theory: on a Euclidean strip
    of width ell, with the cut anchored at the edge, the Renyi-2 entropy is a
    logarithm of the chord distance with c over eight in front. That
    coefficient is c over twelve times one plus one over n, and it is halved
    relative to a cut in the bulk because entanglement counts correlations
    crossing a border, and an edge-anchored region has one border instead of
    two. Now the move that matters for us. Our strip is not Euclidean --- its
    width is imaginary --- so we continue ell to i v T and x to i v t. The
    crucial observation is that the ratio x over ell is t over T, which is
    real: the sine is untouched, and the shape of the dome survives the
    continuation intact. The only new object is the logarithm of i, which is i
    pi over two, a pure constant. Split into real and imaginary parts and you
    have exactly the two things we measure: the chord dome in the real part,
    and in the imaginary part c over eight times pi over two --- pi c over
    sixteen, the flat plateau. If you want the replica trick and the twist
    operators behind that first equation, I am happy to go through them.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup A2 --- the Spectrum, Derived I: from $T(z)$ to $\mu_i$}
  \vspace{-0.2em}
  \begin{center}\small\color{ink!75}
    $T$: the \textbf{stress tensor} --- the operator that generates conformal
    maps; its modes are the Virasoro generators $L_n$.\\[0.2ex]
    It is not primary: a map adds the inhomogeneous Schwarzian term,
    $\propto c$.
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.2em}
  \[
    T(w) = \Big(\frac{dz}{dw}\Big)^2 T(z) + \frac{c}{12}\{z;w\},
    \qquad
    \{z;w\}_{\log} = -\frac12\Big(\frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big)^2
  \]
  \[
    \Longrightarrow\quad
    \hat H = \frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(L_0 - \frac{c}{24}\Big)
    \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    \mu_i = \exp\Big[-\frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(x_i - \frac{c}{24}\Big)\Big]
  \]
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  \begin{center}\small\color{ink!75}
    $\hat H$ is not $T$ itself --- it is $T$ \emph{integrated across} the
    strip: the generator of translations \emph{along} it
  \end{center}
  \begin{center}\small
    on the lattice, two non-universal additions --- both the same for
    every level:
  \end{center}
  \[
    E_i \;=\; \underbrace{f\beta}_{\text{bulk}}
    \;+\; \underbrace{f_s}_{\text{boundary}}
    \;+\; \frac{\pi}{\beta}\Big(x_i - \frac{c}{24}\Big),
    \qquad \log\mu_i = -E_i
  \]
  \idea{level-independent terms cancel in every difference ---
    differences isolate $x_i - x_0$}
  \note{The spectral predictions, first half: where the spectrum comes
    from. The stress tensor is not primary: under the log map it picks up
    the Schwarzian, minus one half pi over beta squared, and through c over
    twelve that is the Casimir shift. The generator of translations along
    the strip is therefore pi over beta times L-nought minus c over
    twenty-four --- L-nought's eigenvalues are the boundary scaling
    dimensions because the boundaries identify the two chiral sectors,
    leaving a single Virasoro algebra. The transfer matrix advances one
    column, so its eigenvalues are the exponentials shown. On the lattice
    each level additionally carries a bulk free-energy term f beta and a
    boundary term f s --- microscopic, non-universal, but identical for
    every level. That is the key structural fact: anything level-independent
    cancels in differences of log-eigenvalues, which is why the phase gaps
    are clean universal numbers.}
\end{frame}

    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \frac1\beta = \frac{1}{i\,vT}\,\frac{1}{1 - \frac{2i\beta_0}{T}}
    = -\frac{i}{vT}\sum_{k\ge0}\Big(\frac{2i\beta_0}{T}\Big)^{k}
  \]
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  \begin{center}
    \begin{minipage}[c]{0.62\textwidth}\centering
      {\footnotesize\color{ink!70} a geometric series, and it converges:\\
        $2\beta_0/T \le 0.2$ in every run \ ($\beta_0 = 0.2$, $T \ge 2$)}
    \end{minipage}%
    \begin{minipage}[c]{0.30\textwidth}\centering
      {\small$
          \begin{array}{c|cc}
            k & \text{goes to} & \text{order}  \\\hline
            0 & \text{phase}   & 1/T           \\
            1 & \text{modulus} & \beta_0/T^2   \\
            2 & \text{phase}   & \beta_0^2/T^3
          \end{array}$}
    \end{minipage}
  \end{center}
  \[
    \operatorname{Im}\lambda_0 = A\,T + B + \frac{C}{T} + \mathcal{O}(T^{-3}),
    \qquad A=-fv,\quad B=-\pi,\quad C=-\frac{\pi c}{24 v}
  \]
  \begin{center}\small
    no $1/T^2$ term (that order is real $\to$ modulus); $B$ is pure branch
    (free fit: $B/\pi=-1.0000$)\\[0.3ex]
    the fit \textbf{truncates this expansion} $\Rightarrow$ part of the
    systematic on $c$
  \end{center}
  \[
    \frac{|\mu_i|}{|\mu_0|} = 1 - \frac{2\pi\beta_0 (x_i-x_0)}{v\,T^{2}}
    \;\longrightarrow\; 1
    \qquad \text{(the circle)}
  \]
  \note{Second half: the expansion behind every fit. The width is complex,
    v times two beta-nought plus i T; factor the large piece out and expand
    as a geometric series. The prefactor is imaginary and each order adds a
    factor of i, so the terms alternate: even k purely imaginary --- the
    phases --- odd k real --- the moduli, each carrying at least one power
    of beta-nought. Consequences, term by term, for the imaginary part of
    lambda-nought: the bulk term gives A T with A minus f v; the branch of
    the logarithm gives the constant, exactly minus pi --- releasing it in
    the fit returns minus one point zero zero zero zero times pi; the k
    equals zero universal term gives C over T with C minus pi c over
    twenty-four v --- the central charge. There is NO one-over-T-squared
    term: that order is real and lives in the modulus. The next phase term
    is already one over T cubed --- and since the fit truncates there, the
    truncation is a documented part of the systematic deficit on c. Finally
    the moduli: the leading real term gives the ratio one minus b-i over T
    squared --- all eigenvalues collapse onto one circle as T grows or the
    regulator is removed: the emergent dual unitarity.}
\end{frame}

      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}\par}
  \vspace{0.1em}
  \idea{the gap barely moves; the eigenvector \emph{basis} degenerates by
    $7$--$9$ orders}
  \note{Why the windows end --- the question the conclusions leave open, and
    this is what we can say about it. Start with the asymmetry at the top. The
    spectral route needs only eigenvalues, and eigenvalues obtained by
    projecting onto a subspace are second-order accurate in the error of the
    vectors that span it --- they are forgiving. The entropy route needs the
    individual eigenvectors, and an eigenvector's sensitivity goes as one over
    its gap to the neighbouring eigenvalues. Emergent dual unitarity shrinks
    exactly those gaps as time grows, so the eigenvectors degrade first, by
    construction, and the entropy route dies before the spectral one. That is
    the mechanism, and the first rejected run moves from eighteen at p equals
    zero down to three and a half at p equals one half. Now the obvious
    objection: is that just the two leading eigenvalues merging? The table says
    no. We build the rotated column as a dense matrix and diagonalise it
    exactly --- no truncation, no tensor networks anywhere --- and at matched
    dimension the separation between the two leading eigenvalues stays around
    two tenths at every coupling, while the condition number of the eigenvector
    basis jumps from three hundred at p equals zero to ten to the ten or eleven
    as soon as the interaction is switched on. Seven to nine orders of
    magnitude. So it is not a closing degeneracy. It is that for a non-normal
    operator the whole eigenvector basis can approach linear dependence with no
    two eigenvalues anywhere near each other. Why the coupling controls that,
    microscopically, we do not know --- and that is the honest open question of
    the thesis.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup E1 --- the Block Iteration, Step by Step}
  \small
  \textbf{Algorithm (App.~F, Alg.~1):} the $k$ dominant eigenpairs of $\E$
  \begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.35em}
    \item $\ket{R_j},\,\bra{L_j} \gets$ random complex tMPS, \ $j=1,\dots,k$
    \item \textbf{apply:}\quad
          $\ket{\tilde R_j} \gets \mathrm{truncate}_\chi(\E\ket{R_j})$,\qquad
          $\bra{\tilde L_j} \gets \mathrm{truncate}_\chi(\bra{L_j}\E)$
    \item \textbf{project:}\quad
          $S_{ij}=\braket{L_i}{R_j}$,\qquad
          $M_{ij}=\bra{L_i}\E\ket{R_j}$
    \item solve $Mv=\theta Sv$ (via $\operatorname{pinv}(S)M$) for Ritz
          values $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k$ and coefficients $V$, $U$
    \item \textbf{separate:}\quad
          $\ket{R_j} \gets \mathrm{truncate}_\chi\big(\textstyle\sum_i
            V_{ij}\ket{\tilde R_i}\big)$, \ analogously $\bra{L_j}$ with $U$
    \item normalise; repeat 2--5 until the leading Ritz values stop
          changing within tolerance
  \end{enumerate}
  \note{If asked to walk through the block method. Six steps. Start from k
    random complex temporal states on each side. Apply: one transfer-matrix
    application per member, truncated back to chi --- the left states with
    the transpose. Project: build the k-by-k overlap matrix S and projected
    operator M from the biorthogonal contractions. Solve the small pencil
    --- with a pseudoinverse of S, because near degeneracies S is singular
    --- to get the Ritz values, which approximate the k leading
    eigenvalues, and the coefficient matrices. Separate: rebuild each block
    member as the coefficient combination of the propagated states,
    truncate, normalise, and iterate until the leading Ritz values stop
    changing. In the production runs we mostly use the eigenvalue-only
    variant, where step five keeps orthonormal bases from a QR instead of
    separated eigenvectors --- the separation is the unstable part and the
    spectrum does not need it.}
\end{frame}

  \vfill
  \note{If asked how I know the block method is right. At short evolution times
    the rotated transfer operator is small enough to write down as a dense
    array and diagonalise outright, with no state truncation and no projection
    onto a block --- so we have the exact answer to compare against. Three
    cases, chosen to vary both the coupling and the size. The smallest, twenty
    seven dimensions, is the one that actually reaches the convergence
    tolerance, and there the two leading moduli agree with the exact result to
    two and a half parts in ten to the twelve: the reduced eigenproblem itself
    introduces no measurable error at all. The two larger runs hit the noise
    floor and end on the no-improvement rule, and they agree to about one part
    in ten to the seven --- including the interacting case at dimension two
    thousand one hundred and ninety-seven. So the accuracy we get is set by how
    far the iteration converges, not by the projection onto the block. What
    this does not validate is the late-time regime, where the eigenvalues are
    nearly degenerate and the dense matrix is out of reach --- that is what the
    seed ensembles and the continuity criterion are for.}
